The missing shadow: the analytic signal

Seen from the wall, a Ferris wheel cabin at mid-height could be going up or coming down, and both situations cast exactly the same shadow. An AM radio, though, pulls a voice out of a wave that goes up and down at full speed, and to do that it needs to know, at every instant, how big the wheel producing that wave is. How do you find out where a wave is in its turn when all you can see is its shadow?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 1, a wave was the shadow of a Ferris wheel on the wall, and that shadow was enough to read the size, the speed and the starting point. To know where the cabin is in its turn at every instant, it falls short, and you need the second shadow, the one on the floor. Together they give back the whole wheel: its radius, which tells you how big the wave is right now, and its angle, which tells you how far it has turned.

Step by step

One shadow is not enough

Imagine you can only see the shadow on the wall. If it is at mid-height, the cabin could be going up one side of the wheel or coming down the other, and both situations give the same height. At the very top or the very bottom there is no doubt, but at any other point there are two possible places, and a wave passes through all of them.

To decide, you need one more piece of information, and the wheel provides it for free: if you light it from above, the cabin casts a second shadow on the floor, which tells you which side of the wheel it is on. With the height and the side, the position is fixed. That pair of numbers is what mathematicians call a complex number, and together they give a radius, the size of the wheel, and an angle, the point in its turn where the cabin is.

There is a hidden cabin and all you can see is its shadow on the wall, the green mark. Drag your cabin around the wheel until it sits where the hidden one really is; if the height alone does not tell you, switch on the floor light.

Building the missing shadow

The microphone only hands you the shadow on the wall, so the one on the floor has to be built from it. For a single wheel that is easy, because its floor shadow is the same wave a quarter of a turn later: when the cabin is at the very top, its floor shadow passes through the center, and when the floor shadow reaches its extreme, the wall shadow passes through zero. If you delay the wave by a quarter of its period, the two shadows together draw a perfect circle, and its radius does not change even though the wall shadow goes up and down.

With several wheels at once, the delay trick fails. A fixed delay, say an eighth of a second, is a quarter of a turn for a wheel that makes two turns per second, but for another one that makes six it is three quarters. Each wheel needs to be turned a quarter of its own turn, whether it is slow or fast, and the operation that does this is called the Hilbert transform, named after the mathematician David Hilbert. It is computed in the spectrum, where each wheel is separate, and it leaves the size of each one untouched.

The original signal together with its Hilbert shadow makes up the analytic signal: the complete wheel, with both of its shadows, which you read as a radius and an angle at every instant.

Build the orange shadow for a single wheel (left) and for two wheels together (right). The path the two shadows draw, as a solid line, has to match the true path, dotted. Try the delay and the other way of building it.

The wheels that turn backwards

There is one loose end to tie up: the wall shadow does not know which way the wheel is turning. A wheel turning forwards and an identical one turning backwards cast exactly the same shadow on the wall, and they can only be told apart by the floor shadow, which switches sides. That is why the spectrum of any real signal comes out symmetric: each real wheel shows up as two half-wheels turning in opposite directions, and their floor shadows cancel out. The one turning backwards is a negative frequency, which is nothing mysterious, because it is simply a wheel going around the other way and, in a real signal, it always comes paired with one going forwards.

In 1946, Dennis Gabor was looking for a precise way to say “the frequency at this instant,” and his recipe fits on one line: in the spectrum, erase the wheels that turn backwards and double the ones that turn forwards. Back in the time domain, both shadows come out at once, the signal you measured and the one that was missing, with no negative frequencies at all. It is the same analytic signal as before, seen from the spectrum instead of from time.

This signal is an AM wave, like the ones in lesson 15: a 20 Hz carrier whose size breathes twice per second. Its spectrum has three wheels turning forwards and three turning backwards. Drag the blade to erase only the ones turning backwards and double the ones that remain, until the radius of the helix matches the dotted line.

The radius and the angle

With the analytic signal in hand, the wheel has a radius and an angle at every instant. The radius is the envelope: the size of the wheel, or the volume of the signal without the ups and downs of each turn. For a fading bell it is a smooth curve that slowly drops, and in a spoken sentence you can usually see one hump per syllable.

The angle is the instantaneous phase, the point in its turn where the cabin is. Since an angle is measured between −180° and 180°, every time the cabin completes a turn the value jumps from one end to the other, and to count the turns you have to do phase unwrapping, adding one whole turn each time it jumps. How fast that unwrapped angle grows is the instantaneous frequency from lesson 15, the wheel that speeds up and slows down, now calculated at every sample.

With the radius and the angle you can recover a message hidden in a wave, and doing so is called to demodulate it. In an AM station the message travels in the size of the wheel and is read from the radius, while in an FM one it travels in how fast the wheel turns and is read from the rate of change of the angle. A radio receiver does something similar with circuits instead of calculations.

Each station hides a three-note melody, one in the size of the wheel and another in its speed. For each one, choose what to look at, the radius of the analytic signal or the rate of its angle, and check whether the melody comes out.

A bearing gives itself away

The radius of the analytic signal is useful for far more than radio. A bearing is a ring of balls rolling between two tracks, and every time a ball hits a crack it gives a small knock. At constant speed, the rate of those knocks depends only on the geometry of the bearing, so its value gives away where the crack is: on the outer track, on the inner one, on a ball or on the cage.

The problem is that the knock is very weak. When a fault starts to develop, its vibrations are hidden under those of the shaft and the gears, and in the machine’s spectrum all you see is the shaft. But each knock makes the surrounding metal ring at a very high pitch, like a little bell that dies out right away. If you filter the vibration and keep only the band around that resonance, its envelope beats at the rhythm of the knocks.

This technique is called envelope analysis, and it is explained in detail by makers of instruments and of maintenance software, such as Brüel & Kjær and MathWorks (the links are in “Further reading”). Here the machine is synthetic, and choosing the band is the only hard part.

A synthetic machine vibrates with a shaft turning at 25 turns per second, and its bearing has a crack that knocks at a rate you do not know. Place the filter band where it needs to be so that the spectrum of its envelope, below, gives away that rate with a clear peak.

How far it goes

Everything above works because there is a single wheel, and one that changes its size and speed slowly compared with its spin. With an “aah,” which is a stack of harmonics, the instantaneous frequency comes out a mess unless you keep just one of them with a filter, and in the lab you will see this with your own voice. With two notes at once things get even stranger: the instantaneous frequency is neither of the two, but oscillates around the stronger note at the rhythm of the beats, and if both sound almost equally loud it can even come out negative.

The picture is the epicycles from lesson 3: the cabin of the sum is the tip of the big wheel with the small one mounted on top. If the small one gets close in size to the big one, the tip passes grazing the axis, and at that moment the angle seen from the axis makes half a turn all at once, sometimes backwards. That half-turn backwards is the negative frequency. It is not a calculation error either: with two wheels there is no single speed to measure, and that is why you have to separate the wheels before measuring them, which is the topic of lesson 17.

Two notes sound at once, one at 220 Hz and the other at 264 Hz. Raise the volume of the second one until the instantaneous frequency of the sum drops below zero.

What to look at. With the sentence, the envelope draws one hump per syllable and lets you count how many you say per second. With the whistle, the instantaneous frequency comes out clean, because there is only one wheel. With the “aah” it comes out a mess until you isolate one harmonic, and when you divide that harmonic by its number, your note comes back.

What to remember

  1. A real wave is just the shadow of a wheel on the wall. With the missing shadow, the one on the floor, which is built by turning each wheel a quarter of its own turn, you have the analytic signal: a radius and an angle at every instant.
  2. The radius is the envelope, the volume without the ups and downs; the angle, unwrapped, is the phase, and its rate of change is the instantaneous frequency. With the radius you demodulate an AM signal, and with the rate of the angle, an FM one.
  3. The instantaneous frequency only tells a clear story if there is a single wheel. With two at once it oscillates and can come out negative, which is why you have to separate the wheels before measuring it.

Common mistakes

  • “The Hilbert transform is the envelope.” The Hilbert transform is the other shadow, the signal with each wheel turned a quarter of a turn. The envelope is the radius, and it comes from putting the two shadows together.
  • “Negative frequencies don’t mean anything.” They are wheels turning backwards. In a real signal they always appear in pairs with the positive ones, which is why their floor shadows cancel out; erasing them and doubling the positive ones is exactly what gives the analytic signal.
  • “The instantaneous frequency of a chord is the note you hear.” With several notes at once it oscillates around the strongest one and can come out negative. With two equal notes you get their average, which doesn’t sound: what you hear is a beat. It only makes sense with a single wheel.
  • “For a bearing, just compute the envelope of the whole vibration.” Without filtering, the envelope is dominated by the shaft, and the peak shows up at its frequency. You first have to keep the band around a resonance, where the knocks of the defect dominate.
  • “You can differentiate the angle as is.” The calculated angle jumps from 180° to −180° on every turn, and you have to unwrap it or measure the rotation between two consecutive samples. Also, in silences the angle is pure noise, and you have to mask it.

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The whole wheel

The whole wheel is an arrow of length A that spins. Its wall shadow is the cosine and its floor shadow, the sine, and together they form a complex number:

A\,e^{i(\omega t + \varphi)} = A\,\cos(\omega t + \varphi) + i\,A\,\sin(\omega t + \varphi)

Negative frequencies

A real cosine is the sum of two half-wheels turning in opposite directions, and the one turning backwards is the negative frequency:

\cos(\omega t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{i\omega t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-i\omega t}

The Hilbert transform

The Hilbert transform multiplies each positive frequency by -i and each negative one by +i, that is, it turns each wheel a quarter of a turn without changing its size:

\widehat{\mathcal{H}\{x\}}(f) = -i\,\operatorname{sgn}(f)\,\hat{x}(f), \qquad \mathcal{H}\{\cos\omega t\} = \sin\omega t

In time, it is a convolution with one over pi times t (taking the principal value at zero):

\mathcal{H}\{x\}(t) = \frac{1}{\pi}\;\mathrm{p.v.}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x(\tau)}{t - \tau}\, d\tau

The analytic signal and Gabor’s recipe

The analytic signal is the signal plus i times its Hilbert transform. In the spectrum it doubles the positive frequencies, keeps the constant term and erases the negative ones, which is Gabor’s recipe:

z(t) = x(t) + i\,\mathcal{H}\{x\}(t), \qquad \hat z(f) = \begin{cases} 2\,\hat x(f) & f > 0 \\ \hat x(0) & f = 0 \\ 0 & f < 0 \end{cases}

With N samples (N even), you multiply each FFT bin by this weight and undo the FFT:

h[k] = \begin{cases} 1 & k = 0 \ \text{or}\ k = N/2 \\ 2 & 1 \le k < N/2 \\ 0 & N/2 < k \le N - 1 \end{cases}

The radius, the angle and the instantaneous frequency

The envelope is the magnitude of the analytic signal, and the phase is its angle counting the turns:

A(t) = |z(t)| = \sqrt{x(t)^2 + \mathcal{H}\{x\}(t)^2}, \qquad \varphi(t) = \arg z(t) \ \ (\text{unwrapped})

The instantaneous frequency is the rate of change of the phase. With samples, it is enough to measure how far the arrow turns from one sample to the next, so there is no need to unwrap:

f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{d\varphi}{dt}, \qquad f_i[n] \approx \frac{f_s}{2\pi}\,\arg\!\left(z[n+1]\,\overline{z[n]}\right)

Demodulating an AM signal

If the size changes slowly compared with the spin, that is, if its spectrum stays below the carrier frequency, the analytic signal of an AM wave is the size times the wheel, and its magnitude returns the message:

x(t) = a(t)\,\cos(\omega_c t) \;\Longrightarrow\; z(t) \approx a(t)\,e^{i\omega_c t}, \qquad |z(t)| \approx a(t)

Two notes at once

With two tones, the instantaneous frequency is the average of the two plus a term that oscillates with the beat and weights the difference of the squares of the amplitudes (Cohen, Loughlin and Vakman, 1999):

\omega_i(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}(\omega_a + \omega_b) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\omega_a - \omega_b)\,\frac{A_a^2 - A_b^2}{A^2(t)}, \qquad A^2(t) = A_a^2 + A_b^2 + 2A_aA_b\cos\!\big((\omega_a - \omega_b)t\big)

With amplitudes 1 and a < 1, the lowest value occurs when the two wheels point in opposite directions, and it is negative if a over one minus a exceeds the frequency of the first divided by the difference:

f_{\min} = f_1 - (f_2 - f_1)\,\frac{a}{1 - a}, \qquad f_{\min} < 0 \iff \frac{a}{1-a} > \frac{f_1}{f_2 - f_1}

With f_1 = 220 Hz and f_2 = 264 Hz the ratio is 5, so it turns negative from a = 5/6 \approx 0.83 on.

The knocks of a bearing

With n balls of diameter d on a circle of diameter D, contact angle \phi and a shaft at f_r turns per second, the knock rates of a defect on the outer track, on the inner track, on the cage and on a ball are:

\text{BPFO} = \frac{n f_r}{2}\left(1 - \frac{d}{D}\cos\phi\right), \quad \text{BPFI} = \frac{n f_r}{2}\left(1 + \frac{d}{D}\cos\phi\right)
\text{FTF} = \frac{f_r}{2}\left(1 - \frac{d}{D}\cos\phi\right), \quad \text{BSF} = \frac{D f_r}{2d}\left[1 - \left(\frac{d}{D}\cos\phi\right)^{2}\right]
