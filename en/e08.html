Level 3 · Lesson 8

The filter that goes haywire

At a wedding, someone is giving a speech and, as the microphone drifts too close to the loudspeaker, the whole room hears a piercing whistle that was not in anyone's voice. The whistle swells in an instant until you want to cover your ears, and moving the microphone a hand's width away is enough to make it stop. Nobody has played a note and nothing recorded is playing, so the sound has to be born from the setup itself. How do you make a sound out of nothing, and why does it go away with so little?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

A microphone connected to a loudspeaker is an echo that feeds on itself: what comes out of the loudspeaker goes back in an instant later, and what goes in comes out again. The only thing that decides how the story ends is how much comes back on each round trip, and that is why the control in the picture goes from 0 to 120%.

Below 100%, the echoes get smaller and smaller until they vanish. Above it, each echo exceeds the previous one and they all grow without stopping until they hit the ceiling of the amplifier, which is what you hear as a whistle. Right at 100% they neither die out nor grow, and that border is what the first challenge looks for.

Step by step

The whistle is an echo that comes back

A microphone, an amplifier, a loudspeaker and the air of the room form a closed circle: what goes into the microphone comes out of the loudspeaker, crosses the room and goes back in an instant later. When a system uses what it has just produced in this way, we speak of feedback, and the whistle that appears when it gets out of control is called acoustic feedback, or howl.

A proper name

In many countries the howl is called the Larsen effect, after the Danish physicist Søren Absalon Larsen, one of the first to describe it. The same feedback makes a hearing aid whistle when the volume is too high.

On each round trip, the sound comes back multiplied by a number that depends on everything in the circle: how sensitive the microphone is, how high the volume is, where the loudspeaker points, the distance between the two and what the walls reflect. That number is the loop gain, and it is the control in the picture above. Besides, not all frequencies come back the same: the room reinforces some and cancels others, and each one arrives with its wave at a different point of its turn, the phase from lesson 1.

For something to howl you need both things at once: that at some frequency the sound comes back just as strong or stronger, and that it arrives on time, with the wave at the same point of its turn, so that it adds to itself instead of subtracting. Then each round trip multiplies the sound by a number greater than one, the growth is exponential, and in an instant the whistle reaches the maximum the amplifier can give, and stays there. What you hear is the peak of the room that fits best, and that is why one specific frequency howls and not all of them.

The same story can be told with numbers, without loudspeakers. A filter that adds to its output a fraction of what came out a while earlier is an echo that feeds on itself. If the fraction is less than one, each echo is smaller than the previous one; if it is one, they are all equal, and if it is greater, each one exceeds the previous one. In the challenge, the echo takes a quarter of a second to come back and the fraction is the control.

Find the value of the control at which the echo neither dies out nor grows. Listen to how a clap sounds as it goes through the echo and, when you think you have found it, press “Check.”

Remembering or listening

In lesson 7, filtering was stamping stamps: each sample that comes in leaves a copy of the response of the filter, and the output is the sum of all the stamps. If the stamp has finite length, the filter only needs to remember the last few samples that came in: it multiplies each one by a weight and adds them up. That is an FIR filter, and its response to a clap ends when the weights run out, however long the recipe is.

There is another family that, besides what comes in, uses what it has put out before. It is the self-feeding echo from the previous challenge, and it is called an IIR filter, or recursive. Since each output depends on earlier outputs, which depend on still earlier ones, its response to a clap never ends: it is made of exponentials that fade away, hence the name, infinite impulse response.

The simplest IIR is the forgetful average: each output mixes a little of the new input with a lot of the previous output, and a single number, how much is kept from one sample to the next, decides how long it takes to forget. With a memory of 0.99, at 16,000 samples per second, its response falls to half in about 4 milliseconds; with 0.999, in about 43. That memory has to be less than one: with 1.01 the output doubles every 70 samples, and in a tenth of a second it is multiplied by about eight million.

That memory comes very cheap. For a low-pass that lets through everything up to 1 kHz with a drop of 1 dB at most and removes at least 40 dB from 2 kHz on, sampling at 16 kHz, a Butterworth-type IIR of order 8 needs about 20 multiplications per sample, and a Hamming-window FIR, 51. With more demanding templates the difference grows: Steven Smith, in his open book, estimates that an FIR is about ten times slower than a comparable IIR. In exchange, the FIR is always stable and can have an exactly linear phase, while the IIR can become unstable.

Each card describes a filter with a recipe, a block diagram and its response to a clap. Decide for each one whether it only remembers what has come in (FIR) or also listens to its own output (IIR), and press “Check.”

Two clues to decide: in the diagram of an IIR there is an orange arrow that goes back from the output to the sum, and its response to a clap keeps fading without ever quite reaching zero.

Poles and canvas

To know in advance whether an IIR is going to go haywire you do not need to try it: it is enough to look at where its poles are. The frequency response from lesson 6, the curve that says how much of each note is kept as it passes through the filter, can be imagined as the height of a canvas stretched over a plane. On that plane there are points that lift the canvas like tent poles, the poles, and others that pin it to the ground like thumbtacks, the zeros.

Going through the frequencies is walking along the edge of a circle painted on the plane: you start on the right, at 0 Hz, and at half a turn, on the left, you reach half the sampling rate. What the filter lets through at each frequency is the height of the canvas at that point of the edge. A pole near the edge lifts the canvas a lot right there, and the filter resonates with a tall, narrow peak at the frequency marked by its angle. The closer the pole is to the edge, the narrower the peak and the longer the response to a clap keeps ringing.

A pole inside the circle dies out by itself; one right on the edge gives a tone that never dies out, and one outside makes the response to a clap grow without restraint, which is what going haywire means. A filter is stable when all its poles are inside the circle. It is the same border as in the first challenge: in the self-feeding echo, the poles are at a distance from the center that crosses 1 exactly when the fraction that comes back crosses 100%.

That is why high-order IIR filters are not written as a single block: with rounded coefficients, the poles can leave the circle without anyone noticing, and the usual practice is to program them as a cascade of small sections of two poles, which are controlled separately.

Make the filter resonate at 1,000 Hz with a peak less than 52 Hz wide, without it going haywire. Drag the pole around the plane and fine-tune its frequency and its distance from the edge with the controls.

Every filter arrives late

A filter that works live can only use the past: it does not know what is going to arrive next, which is why it is called causal. To produce each output sample it has to wait until it has the samples that make it up, and that delays the output. How much it delays depends on the frequency, and that measure is the group delay: how long the envelope of a group of nearby frequencies takes to come out of the filter.

Delaying a Ferris wheel by a fixed time is the same as rotating its starting point, and the faster the wheel turns, the bigger that rotation. A filter that delays all frequencies by the same time rotates each wheel by an angle proportional to its speed, and the shape of the wave arrives intact, but late. That is what a symmetric FIR does, with equal weights on both sides of the center, and it delays everything by half its length: it is linear phase. An FIR of 641 coefficients at 16,000 samples per second arrives 320 samples late, which is 20 milliseconds. An IIR, on the other hand, delays some frequencies more than others, especially near its cutoff, and that is why it distorts bangs: the rising edge and the falling edge come out different.

Sharper filters arrive later, and it is the same law as the tuner in lesson 5: to tell two very close frequencies apart you have to listen for quite a long time. In a window-based FIR, the length is inversely proportional to the width of the transition band, and the delay is half the length: about 2 milliseconds if the transition is 1,000 Hz wide, 20 if it is 100 Hz and 200 if it is 10 Hz.

Each application has its delay budget. In television, according to the viewer tests behind ITU-R recommendation BT.1359-1, the offset between sound and picture starts to be noticed, on average, when the sound is about 125 ms behind or about 45 ms ahead. Those are averages from subjective tests, and digital television asks for stricter tolerances. In a hearing aid, according to a popular review of the research by Stone and Moore, it is best not to go past about 15 ms for your own voice. And in noise-cancelling headphones the margin is counted in microseconds: in a simple single-tone model, a mismatch of a tenth of a millisecond leaves 6% of a 100 Hz tone uncancelled and 62% of a 1,000 Hz one. We covered it in video 11 of the channel (in Spanish), Entiende la cancelación de ruido antes de comprar auriculares (“Understand noise cancellation before buying headphones”).

Forward and backward

If the recording is already made, there is a trick to remove the delay: you filter the signal, flip it in time, filter it again and flip it back. What the first pass delays, the second one, which works with the reversed signal, advances, and the two phase turns cancel out. It is zero-phase filtering: the result stays in place, with the magnitude squared, that is, with twice the decibels of attenuation.

The price has three parts. It costs twice the computation; transients appear at the ends of the signal, which are smoothed by extending it with its mirror image; and, above all, it needs the whole recording, because to flip it you have to know how it ends. Live, the end does not exist yet.

That is why a hearing aid, noise-cancelling headphones or the equalizer of a live concert have to live with their delay, while an electrocardiogram that is already recorded, in which you want to measure the exact instant of each heartbeat, or a podcast from which you want to remove a hum, can be filtered back and forth.

Get the heartbeat filtered, running forward and with no delay, using only the operations “Filter” and “Reverse.” Then try what happens live.

Back to the microphone

With all this, the wedding makes more sense. A sound that goes once around the circle takes as long as it takes to travel the way back, and only the frequencies at which a whole number of waves fits in that time arrive in phase. With the microphone 3 meters away, the sound takes about 9 milliseconds and those candidate frequencies are about 114 Hz apart. The one that howls is the candidate that also falls on a peak of the room. Moving or turning the microphone changes the path, the candidates and how much comes back, and that is why simply moving it is enough to make it stop.

Sound engineers take advantage of this before a concert: they raise the volume until something starts to howl, find that frequency and cut it with an equalizer like the one in lesson 6. That way they gain a few decibels, until the next one starts to howl, and they repeat. The highest volume before the loop starts to howl is the maximum stable gain. According to the review by van Waterschoot and Moonen, manual methods, which include placing microphones and loudspeakers well, putting notch filters on the modes of the room and equalizing, raise it by about 5 to 8 dB. And a margin of 2 or 3 dB below is always left, because just before howling the system already “rings”: a sound that takes a long time to die away, like that of a pole right next to the edge of the circle.

There are other ways to lower the loop gain: moving the microphone away or turning it so it does not point at the loudspeaker, lowering the volume, or shifting the whole signal by about 5 Hz, a trick that Schroeder described in the 1960s and that managed to gain 14 dB in measurements, although without audible beats only about 6 dB is acceptable. There are also filters that readjust themselves.

The challenge uses a simplified model: the room and the loudspeaker only change the volume of each frequency, with peaks at 630, 1,250 and 2,600 Hz, and the path only adds delay. In a real room, the cut also changes the phase and can create new howl frequencies next to it: in a more complete simulation, with the phase included, the cuts gained only one or two decibels and some made things worse.

In your voice

What to record. Three claps, or a spoken phrase, of about five seconds: they are sections 6 and 5 of the reference recording of the series. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The lab passes your voice through a low-pass filter that sounds as if you were talking on the other side of a wall, once live and once forward and backward, and measures how much the live version lags behind the forward-and-backward one, which stays in place. With the IIR it should come out at about three or four milliseconds, and with the very sharp FIR, 50, which when mixed with your voice is heard as a short echo that disappears with the forward-and-backward version. Further down you can put your voice into a self-feeding echo and hear how it stretches out as you raise the feedback.

What to remember

  1. A filter that listens to its own output, an IIR, gets with a few numbers a response that never ends, and that is why it comes cheap, but it is only stable as long as each round trip returns less than it receives, that is, as long as all its poles are inside the circle. An FIR has no output to listen to and cannot blow up. The howl of a microphone is the same phenomenon: the loop gain reaches 1 at a frequency that comes back in phase.
  2. Every filter that works live arrives late. A symmetric FIR delays all frequencies by half its length, so the shape arrives intact; an IIR delays some more than others and distorts it. The sharper the filter, the later it arrives.
  3. If the recording is already made, filtering forward and backward leaves the result in place and doubles the attenuation, at the price of doubling the computation and needing the whole signal. Live it cannot be done, and each application has to live with its delay budget.

Common mistakes

  • “It howls because the volume is too high.” Volume is only one factor of the loop gain, along with the distance, where the microphone and the loudspeaker point and the room. The phase also has to fit, and that is why one specific frequency howls and not all of them. At the same volume, moving the microphone away or turning it, it stops howling.
  • “A filter only changes the frequencies, not the timing.” Every live filter arrives late. A symmetric FIR delays everything equally, by half its length, and an IIR delays some frequencies more than others and distorts the wave.
  • “IIR filters are worse than FIR filters.” They are a different trade-off. An IIR does the same with far fewer operations, about ten times fewer, and usually delays less, but it has no linear phase and can become unstable. An FIR is always stable and can have an exactly linear phase, at the cost of more computation and, if it is sharp, more delay.
  • “Filtering forward and backward comes for free.” It costs twice as much, causes problems at the edges of the signal and, above all, needs the whole recording, so it is no use live. It is not what noise-cancelling headphones do either: that trick needs the future.
  • “An FIR can also go haywire.” Without feedback, its response to a clap ends. It can amplify one frequency a lot, but it cannot grow without restraint.

Math corner

The formulas, for those who want them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The general recipe

Each output sample is a sum of recent inputs, each with its weight, minus a sum of recent outputs, also with their weights. If the recipe has no outputs, the filter is an FIR; if it has them, it is an IIR:

y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\,x[n-k] \;-\; \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k\,y[n-k]

An FIR is a convolution with a finite stamp

With no outputs in the recipe, the weights are the response to a clap, the stamp from lesson 7, and the output is the convolution of the input with it:

y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} h[k]\,x[n-k], \qquad h[k] = b_k

The forgetful average

The output mixes the new input with the previous output. Its response to a clap is an exponential that never ends, it is stable if the memory a is less than 1 in absolute value, and its half-life is \ln(1/2)/\ln a samples:

y[n] = (1-a)\,x[n] + a\,y[n-1], \qquad h[n] = (1-a)\,a^{n}\quad (n \ge 0), \qquad n_{1/2} = \frac{\ln\tfrac{1}{2}}{\ln a}

Transfer function, poles and zeros

The whole filter is a quotient of two polynomials. The zeros make the numerator vanish, the thumbtacks, and the poles make the denominator vanish, the tent poles. A causal filter is stable if and only if all its poles satisfy |p| < 1:

H(z) = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\,z^{-k}}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k\,z^{-k}}

The canvas at the edge

The frequency response is the height of the canvas over the circle of radius 1, at an angle that grows with the frequency:

H\!\left(e^{i\omega}\right), \qquad \omega = 2\pi\,\frac{f}{f_s}

A two-pole resonator

With the poles at a distance r from the center and at angles \pm\theta, the filter resonates near \theta f_s/2\pi, and its peak narrows as r gets closer to 1:

y[n] = x[n] + 2r\cos\theta\;y[n-1] - r^{2}\,y[n-2], \qquad f_0 \approx \frac{\theta\,f_s}{2\pi}, \qquad B_{-3\,\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{(1-r)\,f_s}{\pi}

The self-feeding echo

An echo with a delay of D samples that returns a fraction g of its output has peaks every f_s/D hertz, of height 1/(1-g), and is stable if |g| < 1:

y[n] = x[n] + g\,y[n-D] \;\Longrightarrow\; H(z) = \frac{1}{1 - g\,z^{-D}}, \qquad f_k = k\,\frac{f_s}{D}, \qquad |H|_{\max} = \frac{1}{1-g}

The microphone loop

If G is the path from the microphone to the loudspeaker and F the acoustic path back, what comes out of the loudspeaker for each unit of sound that goes in is

\frac{U(\omega)}{V(\omega)} = \frac{G(\omega)}{1 - G(\omega)\,F(\omega)}

There is acoustic feedback when the denominator can vanish: at some frequency the loop gain reaches 1 and its phase is a whole number of turns. This is the Nyquist stability criterion (1932), defined for linear time-invariant systems; in acoustics it is applied by assuming that everything changes slowly:

\left|G(\omega)\,F(\omega)\right| \ge 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \angle\,G(\omega)\,F(\omega) = 2\pi n, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}

Phase delay and group delay

The phase delay says how much each wheel is delayed separately, and the group delay, how much the envelope of nearby frequencies is delayed:

\tau_\phi(\omega) = -\frac{\phi(\omega)}{\omega}, \qquad \tau_g(\omega) = -\frac{d\phi(\omega)}{d\omega}

Linear phase

A symmetric FIR of M+1 coefficients delays all frequencies by M/2 samples:

h[k] = h[M-k] \;\Longrightarrow\; H\!\left(e^{i\omega}\right) = A(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega M/2}, \qquad \tau_g = \frac{M}{2}

Forward and backward

The forward pass multiplies the signal by H and the backward pass, by its conjugate, so that what remains is the magnitude squared and a phase equal to zero:

H_{\text{forward-backward}}\!\left(e^{i\omega}\right) = H\!\left(e^{i\omega}\right)\,\overline{H\!\left(e^{i\omega}\right)} = \left|H\!\left(e^{i\omega}\right)\right|^{2}

Sharpness costs time

In a window-based FIR, the length is inversely proportional to the width of the transition, measured as a fraction of the sampling rate, and the delay is half that length:

M \approx \frac{4}{\mathrm{BW}}, \qquad \tau \approx \frac{M}{2 f_s} = \frac{2}{\Delta f}
