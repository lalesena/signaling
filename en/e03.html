Level 1 · Lesson 3

The recipe for a sound: adding Ferris wheels

A violin and a flute play the same note, at the same loudness, and you can still tell them apart with your eyes closed. A tuning fork, on the other hand, sounds almost like a single Ferris wheel, the clean shadow that the first lesson talked about, and neither a violin nor a flute does. There must be something more inside a real sound for it to belong to one instrument or another with the same note. What does it carry inside?

  • About 60 minutes

The idea in one picture

This chain of Ferris wheels is the picture of the whole lesson. The first Ferris wheel is big and slow, the next one is smaller and turns twice as fast, the next one is smaller still and turns three times as fast, and so on. The height of the final cabin is the sum of the heights of all of them, and the curve it jots down is a wave that none of them would draw alone.

Start with a single Ferris wheel and keep adding. With the “square” shape you will see how the curve, which at first is a round wave, gets straightened until it looks like a step that goes up and down. If you listen to the sound of each sum, you will notice that each new Ferris wheel adds some brightness.

Step by step

Adding is stacking heights

In lesson 1, a wave was the height of a Ferris wheel cabin jotted down instant by instant. If two Ferris wheels are turning at the same time and you want to know what wave they make together, you add the height of one cabin and the height of the other at every instant: that is superposition. What gets added are the heights. A Ferris wheel of 100 turns per second plus another of 300 do not give one of 400, but a curve that contains both.

When the two Ferris wheels turn at the same speed, the sum is another Ferris wheel of that speed, and its size depends on how out of step they are. If the cabins always go together, the heights add up and the resulting Ferris wheel is twice as large. If one is a quarter of a turn behind the other, it measures 1.41 times one of them. And if one goes up exactly as much as the other goes down, the heights offset each other and the sum stays at zero. Reinforcing or canceling each other this way is interference, and it is the principle behind noise-canceling headphones.

A tide is a sum

The tide at a port is calculated by adding Ferris wheels: one that goes around every 12 hours and 25 minutes, because of the Moon, another every 12 hours, because of the Sun, and a few more. At times they are in step and reinforce each other, and at times they are out of step and get in each other’s way: they line up every 14.8 days, and that is why there are spring tides about every 15 days. For decades, machines made of pulleys and gears did this; the one in the United States, finished in 1910, added up 37 Ferris wheels.

Two Ferris wheels turn at the same time: the ink one is fixed and you control the orange one. Drag the orange cabin, with your finger or the keyboard arrow keys, until the sum stays still at zero.

One Ferris wheel on another

So far the two Ferris wheels were each in its own place. Now imagine that the second one is not on the ground, but fixed to the cabin of the first: it turns with it and, besides, makes its own turns. The cabin of the second one no longer draws a circle, but flowers, stars, or loops, depending on how fast each Ferris wheel turns. A Ferris wheel mounted on another is called an epicycle.

Greek astronomers explained the back-and-forth of the planets with circles that turned mounted on other circles. Ptolemy used them extensively in the second century. Many centuries later, Fourier’s mathematics gave the deeper reason: with enough circles you can approximate any closed curve, and that is what you will end up doing in the last challenge.

With two Ferris wheels, the trick is in the speeds. If the first makes one turn while the second makes k, the figure repeats as many times per turn as the difference between their speeds, counting the direction: a negative speed means turning the other way.

Mount the second Ferris wheel on the first and get a four-petal flower. Choose how many turns the second one makes while the first makes one, and its size.

The Ferris wheels of a repeating sound

The note of an instrument is a repeating sound: its wave is the same again and again, for example 220 times per second. If you want to write that wave as a sum of Ferris wheels, not just any will do, because the sum has to start over the same way in every repetition. That happens only if each Ferris wheel makes a whole number of turns in each repetition.

The Ferris wheel that makes one turn per repetition is the slowest of all and is called the fundamental; it sets the note we hear. The ones that make two, three, or four turns per repetition are the harmonics. With a fundamental of 220 Hz, its harmonics are at 440, 660, 880 Hz, and so on, and the complete list is the harmonic series. Each Ferris wheel on the list has its size and its starting point; the list of sizes is the recipe of the sound.

The simplest recipe is that of the square wave, which jumps between two values like a switch. It is built only from the odd harmonics, the first, the third, the fifth, each one smaller than the previous: a third, a fifth, a seventh. The more you add, the straighter the sides get, and the sound goes from the softness of a single Ferris wheel to a bright, harsh whistle.

Adjust the recipe of nine Ferris wheels by dragging the height of each bar until the sum, in green, looks like the dotted square wave. Listen to it while you build it.

In 1807, Joseph Fourier, who was studying how heat spreads through an object, claimed that any curve that repeats can be written as a sum of waves. Lagrange and Laplace, two of the great mathematicians of the time, did not believe it: how could smooth waves form a corner? Fourier published his theory in 1822, and he was right, with a nuance you will see in a moment. The complete recipe of a repeating wave, with the Ferris wheels it contains, their size, and their starting point, is its Fourier series.

Timbre: the same note, another recipe

A violin and a flute can play the same A, with the same fundamental. What changes is the recipe: a flute played softly is almost a single Ferris wheel, while a bowed string carries many strong harmonics, and the clarinet in its low register carries mostly odd harmonics, like the square wave. That recipe is a good part of what we call timbre, what tells apart two sounds that have the same note and the same loudness.

The other part of timbre lies in how the note starts: if you are given only the middle of a note and not its beginning, you get right much less often which instrument it was. And the same happens with your voice. Your “aah” is not a Ferris wheel, but a stack of Ferris wheels, and its recipe is part of what makes it sound like you; an “eee” on the same note has another recipe.

There is one more piece in the recipe, the starting point of each Ferris wheel. If you change it, the shape of the wave changes a lot, but a sustained sound hardly sounds different, because the ear pays attention mostly to the sizes. It is not completely deaf to phase: if the change scrambles the sound in time, you notice. And for the note, the big Ferris wheel does not even need to be there. The telephone lets almost nothing through below about 300 Hz, and the fundamental of a low voice is usually between about 90 and 155 Hz; even so, on the other end you do not hear anyone higher-pitched, because your ear reconstructs the note from the harmonics that do arrive, which repeat at the rhythm of the fundamental. That note without its big Ferris wheel is the missing fundamental.

For the same note, a D, there are three recipes: a soft flute, a bowed string, and a low clarinet. Listen to each one and then let one play blind. Guess which one it is three times in a row.

The fringes that do not go away

The more Ferris wheels you add, the more the sum looks like the square wave, except for one detail: next to each jump there is a fringe that overshoots. As you add Ferris wheels, the fringe gets narrower and sticks to the jump, but it does not shrink: it stays around 9% of the jump. This is the Gibbs phenomenon, and it is the nuance that escaped Lagrange. Right at the jump, the sum sits at the midpoint, and on each side the fringe is thinner and thinner but just as tall.

Notice that the 9% is of the jump, not of the amplitude: in a square wave that goes from −1 to 1, the jump measures 2, so the fringe sticks out almost 0.18 above the top. It was first described by Henry Wilbraham in 1848, although it carries the name of Josiah Willard Gibbs, who explained it in 1899. It shows up in practice: a filter that cuts a signal off sharply makes its edges oscillate.

Raise the number of harmonics of the square wave until the first fringe is less than 1/100 of a period from the jump. When you get there, write how far the fringe sticks out, as a percentage of the jump, and press “Check.”

Drawing anything with Ferris wheels

Ferris wheels do not only add waves. A point in the plane that traces a closed outline, a star, a letter, or a silhouette, can also be written as a sum of Ferris wheels, mounted on top of each other and turning some one way and some the other. The big, slow ones give the general shape; the small, fast ones, the points, just as in the jump of the square wave, where many fast Ferris wheels were needed.

In 2008, two scholars of the history and philosophy of science, Santiago Ginnobili and Christián Carman, mounted a thousand Ferris wheels on top of each other and got the last one to draw the silhouette of Homer Simpson, which you can see in their video. And in 1898, the physicist Albert Michelson built a machine of gears and springs that added eighty Ferris wheels and drew the result with a pen; in the article where he presented it, one of the curves it draws is the profile of a face.

Adding Ferris wheels is easy: given the recipe, the curve comes out. Doing it the other way around, given the curve, finding out which Ferris wheels make it up, is the job of lesson 4. In the challenge, the page already knows how to do it for you, with a tool you will meet there, and you only decide how many Ferris wheels you let it use.

The ink star is copied with a sum of Ferris wheels, in green. Choose how many Ferris wheels the copy uses and get the error below 2% of the size of the figure with as few as you can. If you like, press “Draw my own” and draw your own figure with your finger or the mouse.

In your voice

What to record. A sustained “aah” and then an “eee” on the same note, segment 3 of the series’ reference recording, and a sustained whistle, segment 2, for comparison. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The lab finds the steadiest stretch of your recording, measures its note, and draws, in amber bars, the size of each harmonic: your recipe. Then it adds the Ferris wheels back up, and with the buttons you choose how many to keep. With one Ferris wheel you hear a whistle on your note; with five or ten it starts to resemble your vowel; with twenty the vowel is recognizable, but it sounds like an organ or a synthesizer, because the recipe lacks the noise of the air and the small natural variations of a voice. In a whistle you will see almost a single bar. If you record very low voices with your phone, bar 1 may come out weak: small microphones attenuate the low frequencies, and that is the missing fundamental. You can drag over the recording to move the window and save a recipe as a reference, to compare it with another.

What to remember

  1. Adding waves means adding their heights instant by instant. If they are in step, they reinforce each other; if one goes up when the other goes down, they cancel.
  2. A repeating sound is a sum of Ferris wheels that make a whole number of turns per repetition: the fundamental, which sets the note, and its harmonics. The list of sizes is the recipe, and the recipe, together with how the sound starts, is a good part of timbre.
  3. With the recipe, any repeating wave can be rebuilt by adding Ferris wheels. Next to the jumps there is a fringe of around 9% of the jump that gets narrower but does not shrink, and closed outlines are drawn the same way, with Ferris wheels mounted on top of each other.

Common mistakes

  • “Adding two waves means adding their frequencies.” The heights are added, instant by instant. A 100 Hz Ferris wheel plus a 300 Hz one do not give a 400 Hz one, but a curve that contains both.
  • “If you add enough harmonics, the fringes disappear.” They get narrower and stick to the jump, but their height stays around 9% of the jump. They disappear only in the sense that they take up less and less room and their energy goes to zero.
  • “The note is the strongest harmonic, and if you remove the fundamental, the note changes.” The note is set by the rate at which the wave repeats, which matches the spacing between harmonics. The strongest may be the second or the third, and even if the fundamental is missing, the note stays.
  • “Timbre is the shape of the wave.” The shape depends on the starting points, to which the ear is not very sensitive in a sustained sound. What weighs most are the sizes of the harmonics and how the note starts.
  • “The square wave contains all the harmonics.” Only the odd ones, with sizes 1, 1/3, 1/5…; the triangle wave also contains only the odd ones, but they drop much faster, as 1/9, 1/25…

Math corner

The formulas, for anyone who wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

A sum of Ferris wheels

Each Ferris wheel has its size A_k, turns k times faster than the fundamental f_0, and starts at its angle \varphi_k; the wave is the sum of the heights of all of them:

x(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_k \sin\!\left(2\pi k f_0 t + \varphi_k\right)

Interference

Two Ferris wheels of the same speed and the same size A, with their cabins separated by an angle \varphi, add up to another Ferris wheel of that speed whose size depends on the offset:

A_{\text{sum}} = 2A\left|\cos\frac{\varphi}{2}\right|

The Fourier series

A reasonable periodic wave, of period T, is a constant plus a sum of cosines and sines that make k turns per period:

x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[a_k \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k t}{T}\right) + b_k \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi k t}{T}\right)\right]

Each coefficient is obtained by comparing the wave with its Ferris wheel over one period: they are multiplied and the area is measured, which is the idea of lesson 4:

a_k = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k t}{T}\right)dt, \qquad b_k = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi k t}{T}\right)dt

Square, sawtooth, and triangle

The square wave that jumps between −1 and 1 has only odd harmonics, with sizes that drop as one over k:

x(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin \omega t + \frac{\sin 3\omega t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5\omega t}{5} + \cdots\right)

The sawtooth contains all the harmonics, which drop as one over k; the triangle wave, only the odd ones, which drop as one over k squared:

\text{sawtooth: } \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k\ge 1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\sin k\omega t, \qquad \text{triangle: } \frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{k\ \text{odd}}\frac{(-1)^{(k-1)/2}}{k^2}\sin k\omega t

The Gibbs phenomenon

With the first n odd harmonics, the first peak is at one period divided by four times n from the jump, and its height approaches a fixed value that is written with the “sine integral”:

t_{\text{peak}} = \frac{T}{4n}, \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty} \max x_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Si}(\pi) \approx 1{.}179, \qquad \mathrm{Si}(\pi) = \int_0^{\pi}\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du \approx 1{.}8519

Expressed as a fraction of the jump, the overshoot is:

\frac{\mathrm{Si}(\pi)}{\pi} - \frac{1}{2} \approx 0{.}0895

Even so, the energy of the error goes to zero: the energy of the wave is the sum of the energies of its Ferris wheels (Parseval’s identity), so what you leave out is less and less:

\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T |x(t)|^2\,dt = \sum_{k} |c_k|^2

Epicycles

A point in the plane is written as a complex number z = x + iy, and a Ferris wheel of radius r that makes k turns per period is r\,e^{i(2\pi k t/T + \varphi)}. A closed drawing is a sum of Ferris wheels turning in both directions:

z(t) = \sum_{k=-K}^{K} c_k\, e^{\,i 2\pi k t / T}, \qquad c_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T z(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi k t / T}\,dt

With N points of the outline, the coefficients come from the discrete transform, which is covered in lesson 5:

c_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} z_n\, e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}

Two almost equal Ferris wheels

Two Ferris wheels of periods T_1 and T_2 that are almost equal line up every so often, the beat time:

T_{\text{beat}} = \frac{1}{\left|\dfrac{1}{T_1} - \dfrac{1}{T_2}\right|} \qquad (12\ \text{h and}\ 12{.}42\ \text{h} \ \Rightarrow\ \approx 354\ \text{h} \approx 14{.}8\ \text{days})
