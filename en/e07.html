Level 3 · Lesson 7

Convolution: every sound leaves its echo

A clap in the living room sounds dry, and the same clap in the bathroom keeps ringing for quite a while. A studio can make your voice sound as if you were singing inside a cathedral without you ever leaving home, and all it needs is a recording of a balloon that someone popped there once. A balloon is a noise that lasts an instant, nothing like a voice, and yet it tells you how anything would sound in that room. How does that work?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

A digital signal is, as you saw in lesson 2, a row of sticks, one for each sample, with the height the signal has at that instant. Imagine that, as it passes through a system, each stick stops being a stick and stamps a copy of a fixed shape, the stamp, as tall as the stick and placed at its instant. A tall stick stamps a big stamp, a short one stamps a small one, and one that points down stamps it upside down. What comes out is the sum of all the stamps, which overlap one another, and that sum is the convolution.

Drag the input sticks and watch how their stamps and the output change. Try a single stick: the output is then the stamp itself, and that is almost the whole idea, because a system is completely described by the stamp a single stick leaves. Then change the shape of the stamp: a long tail smears what goes in, a moving average blurs it, and an echo repeats it.

Step by step

One stamp for each stick

Clap once in the living room and once in the bathroom. The one in the living room dies away quickly, and the one in the bathroom keeps ringing like a tail that slowly fades, because the sound bounces off the hard walls and reaches your ears again and again, a little weaker each time. That tail does not depend on how you clap: it is a characteristic of the room. An impulse is a sound so brief that it barely has time to have a shape, a single stick, and the shortest one in digital audio is a single sample; a clap comes close to it, and a bursting balloon even closer. What the room returns in response to an impulse, its fingerprint, is the impulse response, and it is exactly the stamp in the picture.

Two rules explain why that fingerprint is enough to know what the room will do with any sound. The first is that if you clap twice as hard, the tail comes out twice as strong, and if you put two sounds together, the room responds to each one on its own and adds up the responses. The second is that if you clap one second later, the same tail comes out one second later, because the room does not change over time. A system that obeys both rules is a linear time-invariant (LTI) system, and rooms, the filters of lesson 6, lenses and the moving average all are, at least approximately. With those two rules, your voice is a row of sticks and the room responds to each stick with its stamp.

The fingerprint of a room

The impulse response of a room can be seen at a glance: a bang at the start, a few early reflections off the nearest walls, and a tail that fades away. What sets a living room apart from a bathroom or a cathedral is mostly how long it takes to fade. That tail is the reverberation, and it is measured with the reverberation time, the T60: the time it takes to drop by 60 dB, the decibels of lesson 5, which means falling to one millionth of the power.

To give your voice the fingerprint of a cathedral you need its impulse response, and to have it you have to measure it. Acousticians use a short, loud bang, such as a starter pistol or a bursting balloon, or a sweep of tones that rises through a loudspeaker and is later undone with a calculation; the sweep is the most precise and the bang the most practical. Your clap is the homemade version: imperfect, but enough to hear the idea. In studios, convolution reverb arrived as a device in 1999, with the Sony DRE-S777, which the magazine Sound On Sound presented as the first commercial real-time convolution reverb, and as software in 2001, with Altiverb, according to its own maker.

Hagia Sophia, with balloons

In 2010, the Stanford researcher Bissera Pentcheva walked into Hagia Sophia, in Istanbul, with a bag of balloons: an assistant popped them with a pin tied to a pole under the dome, and the tail was recorded, with more than ten seconds of reverberation, according to the CCRMA project Icons of Sound. A balloon is not usable as it is, because it does not radiate equally in all directions and its spectrum has gaps that color the sound, so the team processed the recordings. With them, a choir was able to sing live at Stanford as if it were inside the basilica.

How long a tail can last is shown by the Guinness record: 75 seconds, measured in 2012 in an abandoned oil tank at Inchindown, in Scotland, with a starter pistol. Guinness calls it an “echo,” but it is reverberation.

Listen to the same clap in three mystery rooms, A, B and C, and say which room each one sounds like. At the top you have the impulse response of each room.

The echo, the simplest stamp

The simplest stamp you can imagine has only two sticks: one at instant zero, which is the direct sound, and another one, weaker and later, which is a copy. That is a mountain echo: your voice goes out, and a while later it comes back, weaker. Convolving with that stamp adds your voice to a copy of itself, delayed and a bit fainter. A room is the same thing, but with thousands of sticks, one for each possible reflection, and that is why a clear echo is heard as an echo and a room with thousands of them is heard as one continuous tail.

Convolution does not care which one is the voice and which one is the room: it makes no difference whether you put the voice into the stamp or the stamp into the voice. Some reverb programs take advantage of that, and accept any sound as a “room,” for example a piano chord.

The moving average, once more

In lesson 6 the moving average was the simplest filter of all: replace each data point with the average of the last N. Seen with stamps, it is a rectangle: each input stick stamps N equal sticks of height 1/N, and the output is the sum of all of them. The longer the stamp, the more samples are averaged and the more the random noise drops, which it does with the square root of N. In exchange, a jump gets smeared: it takes as many samples to rise as the stamp is long.

There is a step buried in noise. Lengthen the rectangular stamp until the noise drops to a third, without the step taking more than 20 samples to rise.

Photos have stamps too

In an out-of-focus photo, every point of light has turned into a disk; if the camera moved, into a streak, and with air turbulence, into a soft blotch. That stamp of light is called the point spread function, and the blurry photo is the sum of the stamps of all its points, just as the output of a room is the sum of the tails of all the sounds. The only difference is that the stamp has two dimensions and the sticks are pixels.

In 1990, the Hubble telescope sent back blurry photos because the edge of its main mirror was 2 microns too flat, according to ESA. Since the stamp each star left was known, astronomers tried to peel it off with calculations, and that is called deconvolution: undoing the convolution to recover the original image. It worked only halfway, because where the stamp erases something that information is lost, and when you try to recover it the noise shoots up. The definitive fix came in December 1993, with corrective mirrors installed by astronauts.

Adding stamps is multiplying spectra

There is a bridge between this lesson and the previous one. When the equalizer multiplied the spectrum by a curve, it did so in frequency, and when a room adds up stamps, it does so in time, but they are the same operation seen in two ways. The convolution theorem says that convolving in time is multiplying the spectra, so the frequency response of a filter is the spectrum of its stamp. That is why a room cannot create notes: it only raises, lowers and delays the ones that were already there. If new frequencies appear, something is not linear, such as saturation.

It also explains why an echo is a filter. A two-stick stamp adds the signal to a delayed copy, and that sum reinforces the frequencies at which the copy arrives in phase with the original and cancels those at which it arrives in antiphase, which draws a comb in frequency. The bridge also has a practical use: stamping by hand is extremely expensive, because with a 2 s stamp at 48,000 samples per second each output sample takes 96,000 multiplications, about 4.6 billion per second per channel. That is why reverb programs switch to frequency with the FFT of lesson 5, multiply, and switch back.

You have a 2,000 Hz note that you want to keep and a 1,000 Hz whistle that you want to silence. Choose the delay and the strength of a two-stick stamp that completely silences the whistle without touching the note.

In your voice

What to record. The claps from the reference recording, section 6: three loud, dry claps in your room and three more in the bathroom or on the stairs, with a couple of seconds between one and the next. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The lab finds the claps and, for the one you choose, cuts out its stamp: from a little before the bang until the tail sinks into the background noise. It shows you that stamp and how long it takes to fade, with an estimate of the reverberation time, and convolves a synthetic phrase with it so you can hear it inside your room. Compare a clap in the bedroom with one in the bathroom: with hard, tiled walls, the tail is usually longer and denser. Keep in mind that the clap has its own color and that some phone apps trim the tails, so what comes out is not exactly what an acoustician would measure.

What to remember

  1. Each input sample stamps a copy of the stamp, scaled to its height and placed at its instant, and the output is the sum of all the stamps: that is convolution.
  2. The impulse response is the complete fingerprint of a linear time-invariant system, such as a room, a filter or a lens: what it returns in response to an impulse is enough to predict what it will do with any signal.
  3. Adding stamps in time is multiplying spectra in frequency, and that is why the stamp seen in frequency is the curve of an equalizer: a system like that raises, lowers and delays notes, but does not create them.

Common mistakes

  • “Convolving is multiplying the two signals point by point.” That is what happens with their spectra. In time, convolving is adding shifted and scaled stamps.
  • “A clap is a perfect impulse.” It is brief, but it has its own color and carries noise, and the response you get is that of the room seen through the clap.
  • “Reverberation adds new notes.” A linear time-invariant system only multiplies the frequencies that were already there and delays them. If new frequencies appear, something is nonlinear, such as saturation.
  • “If I know the stamp, I can recover the perfect photo.” Where the stamp erases a frequency, the information is lost, and when you try to recover it the noise shoots up. Hubble was only partly corrected with calculations.
  • “Passing the voice through the room is not the same as passing the room through the voice.” Convolution is commutative: it does not matter which one you call the signal and which the stamp, and that is why a reverb program can use a piano chord as the room.

Math corner

The formulas, for those who want them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

Linear and time-invariant

A system is linear if the response to a mix is the mix of the responses, and time-invariant if delaying the input only delays the output:

\mathcal{S}\{a\,x_1 + b\,x_2\} = a\,\mathcal{S}\{x_1\} + b\,\mathcal{S}\{x_2\}, \qquad \mathcal{S}\{x[n - n_0]\} = y[n - n_0]

The signal, as a row of sticks

Every signal is the sum of shifted impulses, each with the height of its sample, where \delta[n] is the impulse, a stick of height 1 at instant zero:

x[n] = \sum_{k} x[k]\, \delta[n - k]

The sum of stamps

The output is the sum of stamps: each sample x[k] stamps the impulse response h, scaled by its height and shifted to k. That sum is written with an asterisk:

y[n] = (x * h)[n] = \sum_{k} x[k]\, h[n - k]

Length and order

If the input has L samples and the stamp M, the result has L + M - 1, because the last stick stamps a stamp that sticks out M - 1 samples. And it makes no difference which one is the signal and which the stamp:

\text{length}(x * h) = L + M - 1, \qquad x * h = h * x

The bridge to frequency

Convolving in time is multiplying in frequency:

Y(f) = X(f)\, H(f)

The echo, a two-stick stamp

An echo is a two-stick stamp, the direct sound and a copy of strength g delayed by D samples. In frequency it is a comb that reinforces and cancels alternating frequencies:

h[n] = \delta[n] + g\,\delta[n - D], \qquad H(f) = 1 + g\, e^{-i 2\pi f D / f_s}

Its size is the following, and with g = 1 it cancels completely at the frequencies where the delay is half a turn, one and a half turns, and so on:

|H(f)| = \sqrt{1 + g^2 + 2g\,\cos\!\left(2\pi f D / f_s\right)}, \qquad f = \frac{f_s}{D}\left(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\right),\; m = 0, 1, 2, \dots

The moving average

The moving average is a rectangular stamp of N samples, all of height 1/N, which was studied in lesson 6:

h[n] = \frac{1}{N} \quad (0 \le n \le N-1), \qquad h[n] = 0 \ \text{otherwise}

A blurry photo

A blurry photo is the sharp photo f convolved in two dimensions with the stamp of light p, the point spread function:

g[m, n] = \sum_{k}\sum_{l} f[k, l]\; p[m - k,\, n - l]

Convolving with the FFT

With the FFT you pad with zeros up to at least L + M - 1, multiply the spectra and come back. Without the padding, the stamps that stick out would wrap around and overlap:

x * h = \mathrm{IFFT}\big(\mathrm{FFT}(x_{\text{padded}}) \cdot \mathrm{FFT}(h_{\text{padded}})\big), \qquad N_{\text{FFT}} \ge L + M - 1

Undoing a stamp

Undoing a stamp is dividing spectra, and where H is almost zero the noise shoots up, which is why a brake K is added:

\hat{X}(f) = \frac{Y(f)}{H(f)} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \hat{X}(f) = \frac{H^{*}(f)\, Y(f)}{|H(f)|^{2} + K}

And to remove an echo it is enough to subtract from the recording a delayed copy of what has already been cleaned, a filter that uses its own output and is studied in lesson 8:

\hat{x}[n] = y[n] - g\, \hat{x}[n - D]

The reverberation time

The reverberation time is how long the energy of the tail takes to fall by 60 dB. It is estimated with the energy that remains from each instant to the end:

E(t) = \int_t^{\infty} h^2(\tau)\, d\tau, \qquad T_{60} = \text{time at which } 10\log_{10}\frac{E(t)}{E(0)} \text{ drops by } 60\ \text{dB}
