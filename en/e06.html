Level 3 · Lesson 6

Filters: keeping what matters

With the equalizer of a music app you turn the bass up, turn the treble down, and the song sounds different. The old landline phone network leaves a voice thin and tinny, and an electrocardiograph has to remove the hum of the power grid from a signal that comes out of the heart. An app, a phone line and a hospital machine have nothing in common and yet all three do the same thing to the signal that reaches them. What is it?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

An equalizer is a filter, and how it works fits in one picture: a row of knobs, one for each band of frequencies, and each knob multiplies the Ferris wheels in its band by a number. If you turn up the bass knob, the slow wheels grow, and if you turn down the treble knob, the fast ones shrink. From lesson 4 you know that the spectrum says how much of each wheel there is, so filtering is shaping that spectrum.

Move the knobs and watch two things at once. The spectrum changes exactly as the curve says: where the curve goes up, the spectrum goes up, and where it goes down, it goes down. And the wave at the bottom, which is what actually reaches the speaker, changes shape, because a wave with less bass or less treble is a different sum of wheels. The rule is applied frequency by frequency, but the result is seen and heard in time.

Step by step

One knob for each band

Each knob on the equalizer carries a number, the gain, which is measured in the decibels of lesson 5: 6 dB more doubles the height of the wheels in its band, 6 dB less leaves it at half, and with 0 dB nothing changes. Turning up the bass knob is multiplying the slow wheels by a number greater than one, and turning down the treble knob is multiplying the fast wheels by a number smaller than one.

The line that joins the knobs is the frequency response of the filter, and it is all the information needed to describe it, because it says, for each frequency, by how much it is multiplied. Filtering is multiplying the spectrum of the signal by that curve, frequency by frequency, and what comes out of the speaker is the signal whose spectrum is that product.

Make the music sound like an old radio: drag the orange dots until your curve covers the dashed one.

Each dot is a knob: drag it up or down, or tap near it. With the keyboard, tab to the canvas, choose a knob with the left and right arrow keys and move it with the up and down ones.

Four shapes of curve

With that same equalizer you can draw almost any filter, and the most common ones have names according to the shape of their curve. The one that lets the lows through and holds back the highs is a low-pass, and you have one at home: a wall lets low pitches through better than high ones, and that is why from the neighbor's music you mostly get the bass while their voice sounds muffled. The opposite, which removes what is slow and lets what is fast through, is a high-pass, and it is used to remove the rumble of a knock on the microphone or the drift of a line that slowly rises and falls.

If only one band is let through, the filter is a band-pass, like the one in the phone voice you will see shortly. And if only a narrow band is removed and everything else is let through, it is a band-stop, also called a notch, which is the tool against the hum of the power grid.

Listen to a sound and choose the curve of the filter it went through. They are all the same sentence. Get three right in a row.

The phone voice

For decades, the digital phone network has guaranteed an almost flat response between about 300 and 3,400 Hz, with 8,000 samples per second. Outside that band the standard no longer promises anything: at the top it is cut off so that the sampling isn't fooled, because with 8,000 samples per second nothing above 4,000 Hz fits, as you saw in lesson 2, and at the bottom each operator decides. It is a band-pass, and you can hear it: a good part of the energy of the “s” and the “f” is above 3,400 Hz, and so little of it reaches the classic phone that they are hard to tell apart.

The curious thing is that the pitch of the voice is still heard. The fundamental of most adult voices, when speaking, is below 300 Hz and the classic phone barely lets it through, but you can tell whether the voice is low or high, because your brain works out the note from the harmonics, which do get through. They are the Ferris wheels of lesson 3: a stack of speeds that are multiples of the fundamental.

The HD voice of mobile phones widens the band to 50–7,000 Hz, and the most modern codecs, such as EVS, reach up to 20,000 Hz when the network and both phones allow it.

Turn this voice into an old-style phone call: drag the two handles of the filter until you let through just the band the phone used to let through.

Drag each handle left or right, or tap near it. With the keyboard, tab to the canvas, switch handles with the up and down arrow keys and move one with the left and right ones.

The hum

The power grid sneaks into recordings as a hum of 50 Hz in Europe and 60 Hz in much of the Americas. It is not a single line, but the frequency of the grid and its multiples, and sometimes the strongest is the one at 100 Hz, depending on where the hum comes from. That is why a notch at 50 Hz is not enough: the 100 and 150 Hz are still there, and you need a notch at each one.

A narrow notch removes the hum almost without touching the voice, because the voice has nothing right at those frequencies. The price of being narrow is that it takes time to settle, and the narrower it is, the longer it takes: very narrow ones need several hundred milliseconds to take full effect.

The hum as a clock

The hum you want to remove serves a forensic examiner as a clock. The grid is never exactly at 50 Hz, its small fluctuations are recorded in the audio and, by comparing them with a record of the grid, you can check whether a recording has been cut. According to a 2014 SWGDE working document, some laboratories use it to date recordings, although there is no guarantee that the hum will show up.

The same problem appears in an electrocardiograph, where the 50 Hz of the grid falls in the middle of the band of the heart signal. There a poorly chosen filter doesn't just remove noise, it can distort the tracing, and that is why the 2007 recommendations for the electrocardiogram ask the device to let through from 0.05 Hz up to at least 150 Hz in adults. In this course, the electrocardiogram is just an example of a signal: what matters is how it is filtered, not what it says.

Get the hum down by at least 30 dB without touching the voice. Tap the peaks in the spectrum to put a notch on them; you have four.

The red peaks are the hum and the yellow ones are the voice. Tap a peak to put a notch on it and tap it again to remove it. With the keyboard, tab to the canvas, move through the peaks with the left and right arrow keys and add or remove the notch with the Enter key.

Cutoff and slope

A filter doesn't cut off abruptly. The cutoff frequency is the point where the power has already dropped to half, which is about 3 dB down, and from there the curve keeps falling with a slope that depends on the order of the filter. In the Butterworth filter, from 1930, which was designed to be as flat as possible in the band it lets through, each order adds about 6 dB of drop per octave, and an octave is doubling the frequency.

A low order is a gentle ramp and a high order is a cliff. To separate two notes that are an octave apart you need a cliff, but it has a price: the more abrupt a filter is, the more it delays the signal, the longer it takes to settle and the more it can distort the wave, as you will see in lesson 8.

Let the 1,000 Hz note through almost intact and sink the 2,000 Hz beep below −40 dB. You have two knobs: the cutoff and the order.

The moving average

The simplest filter of all doesn't need to know anything about frequencies: each data point is replaced by the average of the last N. It is the moving average, and the pandemic charts used it every day. In 2020, daily cases went up and down every week, because on weekends fewer tests were done and reported, and the 7-day average erased that sawtooth.

It works because a 7-day average always contains a Monday, a Tuesday, a Wednesday and so on up to Sunday, so anything that repeats exactly every week is seen in full, with its highs and lows canceling out. In general, a moving average of N samples completely erases everything that makes a whole number of turns in N samples. It does have two catches. If it is computed with today and the six previous days, it describes what was happening three days ago, so the curve arrives three days late; you can center the average, but then you need the data from the next three days. And for cleaning up random noise without blurring the jumps it is the best possible filter, but for separating one frequency from another it is among the worst, according to Steven Smith in his book on signal processing.

In Spain people talked about the 14-day incidence: the cases of the last two weeks per 100,000 inhabitants, which was among the main indicators of Spain's Ministry of Health. It is a moving sum, the same filter with a 14-day window, and that is why it didn't have weekend teeth either.

These are daily cases, synthetic, for a wave with a weekly rhythm. Get a curve without teeth that marks the peak on the day it really happened, day 70.

In your voice

What to record. The sentence from the reference recording (segment 5) and, separately, a few seconds of the silence of your room (segment 1). Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples, one of them with hum.

What to look at. Run your sentence through the classic phone: it is understandable, it sounds “canned,” the “s” loses its hiss and the pitch is still there even though the fundamental is missing. Run it through the wall and you will see the voice gets muffled and the words are hard to make out. With the silence of your room, the lab looks for thin lines at 50 or 60 Hz and their multiples; if there are any, it proposes a notch for each one and measures how much the hum drops. With a phone's microphone the most normal thing is that there are none, and then it tells you so and lets you try the example.

What to remember

  1. A filter multiplies each frequency by a number, and the curve of those numbers is its frequency response. Low-pass, high-pass, band-pass and band-stop are four shapes of that curve.
  2. A filter doesn't cut off abruptly: the cutoff frequency is where the power drops to half, and the slope depends on the order. Abruptness has a price, because it delays more and distorts more.
  3. The moving average is the simplest filter: it erases everything that fits a whole number of times in its window and, in exchange, delays the curve. A filter only separates what occupies different frequencies.

Common mistakes

  • “A low-pass removes the bass.” The other way around: it lets the lows, the bass, through and holds back the highs. The name says what passes, not what is removed.
  • “The cutoff frequency is where the filter cuts off abruptly.” It is where the gain falls to −3 dB, half the power. From there the curve keeps going down, with a slope that depends on the order.
  • “A filter removes noise.” Only noise that falls outside the band of the signal. Noise that shares frequencies with the voice passes along with it: to separate John Lennon's voice from the piano on a demo, in 2023, on the song “Now and Then,” it took a machine-learning program, not an equalizer.
  • “The more abrupt, the better.” An abrupt filter delays more, takes longer to settle and can distort the wave, which is the reason for the electrocardiogram recommendations.
  • “The 7-day average gives you today's figure.” If it is computed with today and the six previous days, it actually describes what was happening three days ago.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

Filtering is multiplying

Filtering is multiplying, frequency by frequency, the spectrum of the input X(f) by the response of the filter H(f):

Y(f) = H(f)\,X(f)

Gain and cutoff frequency

The gain in decibels is twenty times the logarithm of the size of the response, and the cutoff frequency is where the power drops to half, that is, about 3 dB:

G(f) = 20\,\log_{10}|H(f)|, \qquad |H(f_c)| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; G(f_c) \approx -3\ \text{dB}

The Butterworth filter

The Butterworth low-pass of order n is flat in the passband and, far from the cutoff, falls about 6 dB per octave for each order:

|H(f)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + (f/f_c)^{2n}}

The high-pass is the same drawing turned around: it holds back what is below the cutoff.

|H(f)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + (f_c/f)^{2n}}

At k octaves above the cutoff, the low-pass attenuates, in decibels:

A_k = 10\,\log_{10}\!\left(1 + 4^{\,nk}\right) \approx 6\,n\,k\ \text{dB}

The moving average

The backward moving average adds up the last N samples and divides by N:

y[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} x[n-k]

Its response has zeros where a whole turn fits an exact number of times in N samples, and it delays the signal by (N-1)/2 samples:

|H(f)| = \left|\frac{\sin(\pi f N / f_s)}{N\,\sin(\pi f / f_s)}\right|, \qquad H(f) = 0 \ \text{ if } f = m\,\frac{f_s}{N},\; m = 1, 2, \dots

The random noise that comes out is the square root of N times smaller than what goes in:

\sigma_y = \frac{\sigma_x}{\sqrt{N}}

The notch

A notch puts two zeros right at the frequency of the hum and two poles very close by, just inside, so that the gap is narrow; the closer r is to 1, the narrower the notch. Poles and zeros are explained in lesson 8.

H(z) = \frac{1 - 2\cos\omega_0\, z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 - 2r\cos\omega_0\, z^{-1} + r^2 z^{-2}}, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi f_0}{f_s},\; r \lesssim 1

A preview

Multiplying in frequency is equivalent, in time, to a convolution with the impulse response of the filter, and from there comes lesson 7:

Y(f) = H(f)\,X(f) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; y[n] = \sum_k h[k]\, x[n-k]
