Why your tuner needs time

A tuning app takes a moment to decide which note you are playing, and it isn't that your phone is slow: computing the spectrum of a few thousand samples takes it a few thousandths of a second, or less. The wait comes from somewhere else, and no program can skip it. What law is behind it?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

Two strings at 440 and 442 Hz sound together with a swell in volume, a wah-wah-wah that repeats twice a second: it is a beat, and it happens because the two Ferris wheels, which start out together, drift apart at the rate of two turns per second. As long as they haven't drifted apart by a whole turn, there is no way to tell two wheels from one.

That is the law of the lesson: to see two notes that differ by a few hertz you have to look for at least the time it takes them to gain a turn on each other, which is one divided by that number of hertz. With a tenth of a second, the minimum difference is about 10 Hz, and with two seconds, half a hertz. Move the difference between the two notes and the length of the piece, and watch the moment when the mountain splits in two.

Step by step

Beats: two strings almost in tune

When two almost identical notes sound at once, the volume rises and falls with a swell you hear as a wah-wah-wah. It pulses as many times per second as the number of hertz the two notes differ by: 440 and 442 Hz give two beats per second. Piano tuners take advantage of this, because when the swell disappears the two strings are in tune.

The Ferris wheel explains it well. The two cabins start together and add their heights, so at first the sound is loud. Since one turns a little faster, it pulls ahead until it ends up on the opposite side, and then the two cancel each other and the sound almost dies away. When it has gained a whole turn, they coincide again. One beat is one turn of difference, and the time between beats is how long they take to gain that turn.

Make the wah-wah disappear: turn the peg to tune the second string to the first one, which sounds at 440 Hz.

A row of test wheels

The test wheel of lesson 4 faced every possible speed, and a computer can only try a finite row. With a piece of T seconds, the test wheels of the discrete Fourier transform, the DFT, are the ones that make a whole number of turns inside the piece: zero turns, one, two, three and so on up to half the sampling rate from lesson 2. That row leaves them spaced one divided by T apart: 2 Hz apart with a half-second piece, and 0.5 Hz apart with two seconds.

From this comes the tuner's law. Two notes that differ by less than that spacing fall into the same mountain, and the frequency resolution can't tell them apart: with a tenth of a second it is about 10 Hz and with two seconds, half a hertz. Separating them cleanly costs somewhat more, depending on the window you use and how the two notes are placed; with the Hann window, which you will see in step 4, you need about two and a half times that time. The sampling rate changes none of this: it only sets the ceiling of the frequencies, which is half of it.

A semitone is 6% of the frequency. On the low E of a guitar, at 82.41 Hz, the next semitone is about 5 Hz away, and separating them takes on the order of 0.2 s or more; on the high E, at 329.63 Hz, it is about 20 Hz away and on the order of 0.05 s is enough. A tuner doesn't take long to compute: it takes long to gather enough signal, and somewhat longer with low notes.

Computing the DFT from its definition takes a number of operations that grows as the square of the number of samples. The fast Fourier transform, the FFT, computes exactly the same DFT by splitting the work into halves that are then recombined, and with a million samples it goes from a trillion operations to about twenty million. It is not another transform or an approximation, but the same calculation arranged in a different way.

A longer story

Around 1805, according to historians, Carl Friedrich Gauss came up with the same shortcut while computing the orbit of some asteroids from a few observations. He didn't publish it, and it appeared in Latin in his collected works, in 1866. In 1965, James Cooley and John Tukey published the method in a five-page paper aimed at computers. One reason in those years, among several, was a 1963 meeting of President Kennedy's science advisers that discussed how to detect Soviet nuclear tests by listening to the Earth with seismometers; the 1965 paper doesn't even mention it, and Cooley himself would say that the computers of the time weren't up to processing all those signals.

Pick a pair of notes and find the shortest piece that separates them into two peaks. Get all three pairs.

The seam of the piece

The DFT hides an assumption: it treats the piece as if it repeated forever, one copy pasted after another. If the wheel makes a whole number of turns in the piece, the end joins the beginning and everything fits, and the area shows up in a single bar. If not, pasting the copies leaves a jump at the seam, and drawing a jump takes many different wheels: the energy of the wheel spills over into the neighboring bars. That spreading is called spectral leakage.

With a real voice the turns almost never fit, because nobody sings a note exactly at the frequency of a DFT bar, so leakage is the norm. It is also normal for the peak of a note to be split between two neighboring bars: the note falls between two test wheels, and that doesn't mean there are two notes or that the measurement is wrong.

Get all the energy to land in a single bar: adjust the frequency until the piece makes a whole number of turns.

Smoothing the edges

To keep the seam from having a jump, the piece is multiplied by a bell that starts and ends at zero, a window. The most widely used one carries the name of the Austrian meteorologist Julius von Hann, and many programs call it “hanning,” a name that stuck out of habit. With it, the end and the beginning are both zero and join without a jump, and the skirt of the peak drops a great deal.

In exchange, the mountain widens a little: it is a trade-off between the width of the peak and the height of the leakage. The window doesn't remove noise or increase resolution. To separate two equal tones that are very close together, the rectangular one, which means using no window, separates them sooner; to see a faint note next to a strong one, the Hann window wins, or the Blackman, which lowers the skirt even more.

Next to a strong note there is another about three hundred times weaker. Find its peak: try the windows and tap the spectrum where you see it.

Zeros don't work miracles

There is a trick to make a spectrum come out smoother: add zeros to the end of the piece before computing the DFT, which is called zero padding. With more zeros you get more bars, closer together, and the mountain is drawn with more points and looks smoother. But the zeros are not signal: the mountain is the same, with the same width, just better drawn.

It is useful for something else. With a single note, clean and steady, padding helps locate the center of the mountain more finely than the DFT grid allows, by interpolating between bars. That is precision, and it is different from resolution: you can say exactly where a note is, but not separate two that are inside the same mountain. For that you need more signal, not more zeros.

Two notes of 440 and 450 Hz sound together, but you see only one peak. Get yourself to see two.

Looking at the big and the small at once

A spectrum is usually looked at in decibels, because on a linear scale the small things get squashed against the floor. Decibels count how many times a factor of ten fits: with amplitudes, every 20 dB the amplitude is divided by ten, so a harmonic at −40 dB is a hundred times smaller than the peak, and one at −60 dB, a thousand times. The decibel is a tenth of a bel, which is named after Alexander Graham Bell.

There is a common trap: −3 dB is half the power, whereas −6 dB is half the amplitude, which is a quarter of the power.

The spectrum of a synthetic “aah” has many harmonics, each one smaller than the last. How many stick out above −60 dB?

In your voice

What to record. A sustained whistle of a few seconds or a long “aah”: they are segments 2 and 3 of the series' reference recording. Try to hold the note as steady as you can. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The lab picks the piece of your recording with the most sound in it and computes its spectrum twice, with a short one, a tenth of a second long, and with a long one, which you can stretch up to two seconds. With the Hann window, the width of the peak is about 20 Hz with the tenth of a second and 1 Hz with the two seconds. But a human note is not a perfect Ferris wheel: it varies by a few cents, and that is why the peak of the long piece often comes out wider than the theory says, or with two humps, and listening for longer only sharpens the reading if the note stays steady. With the “aah” you will see the row of harmonics from lesson 4, each one thinner with the long piece.

What to remember

  1. The DFT tries only the wheels that make a whole number of turns in the piece, spaced one divided by its duration apart. Two notes closer together than that merge into one mountain, and to separate them you have to listen for longer, not raise the sampling rate or pad with zeros.
  2. If the wheel doesn't make whole turns, the seam of the piece has a jump and the energy spills over into the neighboring bars: that is spectral leakage. A window reduces it in exchange for widening the peak a little.
  3. The FFT is the same DFT computed much faster, and decibels let you see the small next to the large: every 20 dB, the amplitude is divided by ten.

Common mistakes

  • “The FFT is another transform, more approximate than the DFT.” It is an algorithm for computing exactly the same DFT, only with far fewer operations.
  • “With more zeros, or more bars, I see more detail.” Padding interpolates: the mountain is the same. Detail, resolution, comes only from listening for longer.
  • “If I raise the sampling rate, I separate two close notes better.” Resolution depends on the duration and not on the sampling rate, which only raises the ceiling of frequencies. With the same number of samples, raising it shortens the piece and the resolution gets worse.
  • “The Hann window removes noise and is always better.” It reduces leakage, not noise, and it widens the peak. To separate two equal tones that are very close together, the rectangular window separates them sooner.
  • “−6 dB is half the power.” Half the power is −3 dB. With −6 dB, the amplitude is cut in half and the power to a quarter.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The DFT

Coefficient number k of the DFT is the area of the product of the signal and test wheel number k, with N samples:

X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1

The frequency grid

Test wheel number k makes k turns in the piece; its frequency is k times the sampling rate divided by N, and the bars are spaced one divided by the duration T of the piece apart:

f_k = \frac{k\,f_s}{N}, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{1}{T}

Beats

Two nearly equal sines add up to an intermediate note multiplied by a slow cosine, whose frequency is half the difference. The volume is the absolute value of that cosine, which reaches its maximum twice per cycle, so the ear counts as many beats per second as there are hertz of difference:

\sin(2\pi f_1 t) + \sin(2\pi f_2 t) = 2\cos\!\left(2\pi\,\tfrac{f_1 - f_2}{2}\,t\right)\sin\!\left(2\pi\,\tfrac{f_1 + f_2}{2}\,t\right), \qquad f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|

Cutting a piece is applying a window

Cutting a piece is the same as multiplying the signal by a window, and the spectrum of the piece is that of the signal blurred by that of the window (the star * is the convolution from lesson 7):

x_w[n] = x[n]\,w[n] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_w(f) = (X * W)(f)

The Hann window

The Hann window is a cosine bell that starts and ends at zero; this is the periodic version, the right one for the DFT:

w[n] = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi n}{N}\right), \qquad n = 0, \dots, N-1

How long you have to look to separate two peaks

To separate two peaks, the window must span about K periods of the difference frequency. A sufficient condition is K = 2 with the rectangular window and K = 4 with the Hann one (Smith, Spectral Audio Signal Processing); with the criterion used in the challenges, a valley of at least 3 dB, you get about 1.5 and about 2.4:

T \ge \frac{K}{|f_2 - f_1|}

Zero padding

Padding with zeros up to L samples just takes more points of the same continuous curve, the spectrum of the windowed piece:

X_{\text{relleno}}[k] = X_w\!\left(\frac{k\,f_s}{L}\right), \qquad L > N

Decibels

In decibels, a ratio of amplitudes is measured with 20 times its base-10 logarithm, and a ratio of powers with 10 times:

L_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10}\frac{A}{A_{\text{ref}}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P}{P_{\text{ref}}}

The FFT

The FFT splits the DFT into that of the even samples, E, and that of the odd ones, O, and recombines them with a twist:

X[k] = E[k] + e^{-i 2\pi k/N}\,O[k], \qquad X[k + N/2] = E[k] - e^{-i 2\pi k/N}\,O[k]

Repeating this until you reach pieces of one sample leads to a cost of about (N/2)\log_2 N complex multiplications instead of the N^2 of the definition:

N^2 \;\longrightarrow\; \tfrac{N}{2}\log_2 N
