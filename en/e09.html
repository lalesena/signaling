A needle in a haystack: correlation

The signal that a GPS satellite sends to your phone arrives with a power of the order of one ten-thousandth of a trillionth of a watt. In that same band, the noise that any antenna picks up just by being at room temperature is tens of times stronger, so if you plotted what the phone receives, you would see only noise. Even so, in a few seconds the phone knows where you are. How do you find something that is below the noise?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

Imagine you are looking for a phrase in a book without knowing which page it is on. You put your phrase at the beginning of the text, compare letter by letter with what is underneath, note how many match, move it one letter to the right and compare again, all the way to the end. The position with the most matches is where the phrase is. Correlation does the same with a signal: at each position it multiplies the template by what lies underneath, point by point, and adds up.

With the noise stronger or weaker, the curve below changes its look, but the peak stays in place as long as the template is long enough. The control in the picture raises the noise until the chirp can no longer be seen in the signal above and, even so, the curve below finds it.

Step by step

The sliding template

In lesson 4, transforming was measuring resemblance: you place a test Ferris wheel next to the signal, multiply the two point by point and look at the area that remains. Here the template does not have to be a Ferris wheel. It can be any shape you know in advance, such as the short chirp that a sonar emits or the waveform of a word. The known shape you are looking for is called the template.

You place the template over the beginning of the signal, multiply the two point by point, add up the products and write down the result. Then you slide it one step to the right and repeat, until you reach the end. The list of numbers that comes out, one for each position of the template, is the cross-correlation, and the number of steps the template has been shifted at each point of the list is the lag.

Where the template does not resemble what is underneath, the products come out sometimes positive and sometimes negative, and the sum stays close to zero. Where the template fits, a crest falls on a crest and a trough on a trough, almost all the products come out positive and the sum jumps. To be able to compare positions and signals of different volume, you can divide the sum by the energy of the template and by that of the piece of signal it covers, and then the resemblance falls between −1 and 1.

It is almost the same operation as the convolution of lesson 7, with one difference: there the stamp was flipped before sliding it, and here the template is slid as it is.

Find where the chirp is hidden. Drag the purple template along the signal and watch the orange curve below, which is drawn wherever you pass: when the template lands right on top of the chirp, the peak will jump.

The peak is very narrow: when you let go, if the template is very close to it, it snaps into place by itself. With the keyboard, focus the graph with the Tab key and use the arrows, one sample at a time (with Shift, ten at a time).

Why it beats the noise

Noise also resembles any template a little, so the curve is never completely flat. The difference is in how the products add up. Each point of the signal that coincides with the template contributes a product in favor, all with the same sign, and their sum grows in proportion to the number of points. The products of the noise come out sometimes in favor and sometimes against, they half cancel each other, and their sum grows only as the square root of that number.

With four times as many points, the signal adds up four times as much and the noise only twice as much, so the peak stands out twice as much. The longer the template, the bigger the advantage, and that advantage is called processing gain. Measured in decibels, as in lesson 5, it is ten times the logarithm of the number of points: with a thousand points it is 30 dB, which means multiplying by a thousand the power of the signal relative to that of the noise. That is why a signal can be below the noise at every instant and still be found.

Hidden in the signal is a string of ones and minus ones, and at each sample the signal is about four times weaker than the noise, twelve decibels. Lengthen the template until the peak of the orange curve stands out at least five times above the rest of the curve and marks the right place.

Radar and sonar

If you know the shape of what you are looking for, the best template is that same shape. When the noise is white, that is, equally strong at all frequencies, correlating with the exact shape gives the highest peak over the noise that a linear system can give. Using correlation this way is called a matched filter, and the idea is attributed to D. O. North, who described it in a 1943 report from the RCA laboratories. With noises of other colors you have to whiten the signal first, something that lesson 10 deals with.

Radar and sonar use it this way. They emit a pulse of known shape and what comes back is a delayed, very weakened copy, buried in noise. You slide the pulse over what was received and the lag of the peak is the time it took to go and come back. Since the wave travels the path twice, the object is at half of what it has traveled: the distance is the speed times the time, divided by two.

At 20 °C, sound travels at about 343 meters per second in air, at about 1,480 in fresh water and at about 1,540 in seawater. The challenge uses the rounded value of 1,500. You can try it with your phone: the phyphox “Sonar” experiment emits 5 ms chirps that rise from 1 to 4 kHz, correlates the recording with them and converts the time of each echo into a distance.

A sonar has sent out a chirp and received its echo, so weak that it is lost in the noise. Tap the peak of the orange curve to read when it comes back and work out how many meters away the object is, with sound at 1,500 m/s. Enter the distance and press “Check.”

The shape of the template matters

A radar wants two things that fight each other. A long pulse carries a lot of energy, so it is detected from farther away, but its correlation is a wide, clumsy peak that does not separate two nearby objects. A short pulse gives a narrow peak, but carries little energy. The solution is to encode the pulse: it is still long, but it is made up of a string of short elements, each with its sign, and its correlation concentrates into a narrow peak with small sidelobes on either side. This is pulse compression, and it makes it possible to have energy and precision at the same time.

The classic example is the 13-element Barker code: plus, plus, plus, plus, plus, minus, minus, plus, plus, minus, plus, minus, plus. Its correlation has a peak of 13 and sidelobes that never exceed 1, that is, 22.3 dB below the peak. No Barker code with more than 13 elements is known, and there are very strong indications that none exists. A chirp, a pulse whose frequency rises while it lasts, is also compressed this way.

Sidelobes matter because they are false echoes: if they are high, a lobe of the nearby airplane can be mistaken for another airplane.

Two airplanes fly almost side by side and the radar receives their two echoes with noise. Choose the pulse with which the orange curve shows two separate peaks and the sidelobes do not exceed 10% of the peak. All three pulses last the same and carry the same energy.

Comparing a signal with itself: autocorrelation

If the template is the signal itself, the correlation says at which distances the signal resembles itself, and it is called autocorrelation. At zero shift the resemblance is total, and it is always the maximum that can be reached. Noise only resembles itself without moving, so its autocorrelation is a peak at zero and almost nothing else. A whistle resembles itself each time it completes one turn of the Ferris wheel, and a vowel, each time the vocal cords vibrate.

That is where the note of a voice comes from. The first tall peak after zero marks how long one vibration lasts, and its inverse is the fundamental frequency from lesson 3. It is a classic method, which Rabiner studied in depth in 1977 and which Praat uses with Boersma's improvements. In practice, you subtract the mean, multiply the segment by a window, use about 40 ms, which leaves at least three periods of the lowest pitch you are looking for, and you say “no note” when no peak reaches a minimum height, from 0.4 to 0.45: white noise gives peaks of about 0.25.

There are traps. The peaks at two and three vibrations are almost as tall as the first, and picking the tallest one gives a note an octave below the real one, the octave error from lesson 20. A small peak before the first one can come from the first formant of the vowel, and that is why you choose the first peak that reaches a height similar to that of the tallest, not the first one that passes a low threshold.

Autocorrelation also has an intimate relationship with Fourier. The transform is a correlation with Ferris wheels, one peak for each wheel that fits, and the power spectrum is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation. White noise has a flat spectrum because it only resembles itself without being shifted, and lesson 10 starts from there.

This is the autocorrelation of a sung “aah,” from 0 to 25 ms. Tap the peak that corresponds to a single vibration of the vocal cords and see what note comes out. With the buttons below you can hear the vowel and then a pure tone of the peak you choose, to compare.

Back to the needle: GPS

All GPS satellites emit the civil signal on the same frequency, 1,575.42 MHz, and all at the same time. What distinguishes each one is its code, a string of 1,023 elements, each one a one or a minus one, called chips, which repeats every thousandth of a second. They are pseudorandom codes: they look like chance, but the satellite and the receiver know them. They resemble each other so little that the correlation of one code with a different one only takes the values −65, −1 or 63, compared with the 1,023 of the peak of a code with itself.

The official GPS specification sets as a minimum for this signal a received power of −158.5 dBW, which is −128.5 dBm. The round figure of −130 dBm, which many texts quote, comes from earlier specifications; with either one, it is of the order of 10⁻¹⁶ watts. Any receiver at room temperature also picks up thermal noise of about −174 dBm per hertz, and in the 2 MHz that the signal occupies that is about −111 dBm. The signal therefore arrives between 17.6 and 19 dB below the noise, between 58 and 80 times weaker in power.

The receiver does not see the signal: it looks for it. It slides the code of each satellite over what it receives, and correlating one full period gives a gain of 10 times the logarithm of 1,023, about 30 dB, so the signal goes from being about 19 dB below the noise to being about 11 above it, in an ideal receiver. Each time the correlation time is doubled, about 3 dB more are gained, and up to one navigation data bit, which lasts 20 ms, the gain reaches about 43 dB.

Since the satellite moves, the frequency can arrive shifted by the Doppler effect by up to about 5 kHz, and the receiver searches in two directions: the lag of the code and the frequency. The lag is what measures how long the signal took to arrive. A chip lasts about 977.5 nanoseconds, the time in which light travels about 293 meters, and the signal takes about 67 ms to come down from the 20,180 km altitude of a satellite that is directly overhead.

One of four satellites is in the sky, and its signal arrives much weaker than the noise and with a delay you do not know. Try the code of each satellite with different integration times until a clear peak comes out, and find out which one it is and with how many chips of delay it arrives. It is a model without the Doppler effect.

What to remember

  1. Correlation slides a template along the signal and measures the resemblance at each position: you multiply point by point and add up. Where the template fits, the curve has a peak, and the lag of the peak says where what you are looking for is, or how far away it is, if it is an echo.
  2. It beats the noise because the points of the signal add up in favor and those of the noise half cancel each other: the advantage grows with the length of the template and is measured in decibels, ten times the logarithm of the number of points. With white noise, the best template is the very shape you are looking for, and the shape decides how narrow the peak comes out and how tall the sidelobes are.
  3. Autocorrelation compares the signal with itself: noise only resembles itself without moving and a note, every period. The first tall peak marks the period, and picking another peak gives a note an octave lower.

Common mistakes

  • “To find a signal, it has to be stronger than the noise.” Correlation adds up in favor all the points of the signal while the noise half cancels out. With a long template you can find a signal tens of times weaker than the noise, like the GPS one.
  • “The peak of the correlation has the shape of what you are looking for.” The peak measures the resemblance at each position, it does not copy the shape. Without noise it is symmetric even if the template is not, and it lasts twice as long as it. Also, noise resembles any template a little, so there are always some extra small peaks.
  • “The tallest peak of the autocorrelation always marks the period.” In a very regular voice, the peaks at two and three periods are almost equally tall, and choosing one of them gives a note an octave or more below the real one. The period is the first tall peak.
  • “Any template works, as long as it is long.” Length gives energy, but the shape decides how narrow the peak comes out and how tall the sidelobes are. With white noise, the best template is exactly the shape you are looking for.
  • “Each GPS satellite transmits on a different frequency.” They all emit the civil signal on the same frequency, and the code tells them apart: each one has its own and they resemble each other so little that, when you slide the code of one satellite, only that satellite responds.

Math corner

The formulas, for those who want them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

Cross-correlation

The template y slides \ell samples over the signal x; at each shift you multiply point by point and add up:

r_{xy}[\ell] = \sum_{n} x[n+\ell]\; y[n]

Normalized resemblance

To compare between positions and signals, you divide by the energy of the template and by that of the piece of signal it covers, and the result falls between −1 and 1:

\rho[\ell] = \frac{\sum_n x[n+\ell]\, y[n]}{\sqrt{\sum_n y[n]^2 \;\sum_n x[n+\ell]^2}}

Correlation and convolution

Correlating with a template is convolving with the template flipped backward:

r_{xy}[\ell] = (x * \tilde{y})[\ell], \qquad \tilde{y}[n] = y[-n]

Why it beats the noise

If the signal contributes A at each of the N points and the noise has standard deviation \sigma per point, the signal adds up to NA and the noise to \sigma\sqrt{N}, so the power ratio improves by a factor of N:

\frac{(N A)^2}{N \sigma^2} = N\,\frac{A^2}{\sigma^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad G = 10\log_{10} N \ \text{dB}

For GPS, with 1,023 chips in one millisecond and with 20 ms, which is 1.023 million chips per second divided by the 50 bits per second of the data:

10\log_{10} 1023 \approx 30.1 \ \text{dB}, \qquad 10\log_{10}\frac{1.023\cdot 10^{6}}{50} \approx 43.1 \ \text{dB}

The matched filter

With white noise of variance \sigma^2 per sample, the template that gives the highest signal-to-noise ratio at the peak is the shape s itself, written as a convolution, backward. That maximum ratio is the energy of the shape divided by the variance of the noise:

h[n] = s[N-1-n], \qquad \mathrm{SNR}_{\max} = \frac{\sum_n s[n]^2}{\sigma^2}

Echo and distance

The wave goes and comes back, so the distance is the speed times the time of the echo, divided by two:

d = \frac{v\, t}{2}

Autocorrelation

The signal compared with itself. At zero it equals its energy and is never exceeded, and if the signal repeats every P samples, the peaks return every P, and that is where the fundamental frequency comes from:

r_{xx}[\ell] = \sum_n x[n]\, x[n+\ell], \qquad |r_{xx}[\ell]| \le r_{xx}[0], \qquad f_0 = \frac{f_s}{P}

The window correction

When you multiply by a window, the autocorrelation falls with the shift. Dividing it by the autocorrelation of the window itself undoes that fall, as Boersma proposes:

r_x(\tau) \approx \frac{r_a(\tau)}{r_w(\tau)}

Fourier is a correlation with Ferris wheels

Each value of the spectrum is the correlation of the signal with a Ferris wheel of frequency f:

X(f) = \sum_n x[n]\, e^{-i 2\pi f n / f_s}

The bridge to noise

The power spectrum is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation, and that is why white noise, with its autocorrelation as a peak at zero, has a flat spectrum:

S_{xx}(f) = \sum_{\ell} r_{xx}[\ell]\; e^{-i 2\pi f \ell / f_s}

The GPS budget

Thermal noise at 290 K is about −174 dBm per hertz. The ratio of the signal to the noise in the band it occupies, of width B, is the received power C minus that noise, and the correlation then adds its processing gain:

\frac{C}{N}\Big|_{B} = C_{\text{dBm}} - (-174) - 10\log_{10} B, \qquad \mathrm{SNR}_{\text{after correlating}} = \frac{C}{N}\Big|_{B} + G

With C = -130 dBm and B = 2.046 MHz, the signal is about 19 dB below the noise, and with the 30 dB gain of one millisecond of code it ends up about 11 dB above it, in an ideal receiver.
