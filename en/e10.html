Noise has colors

Sleep and focus apps offer white, pink and brown noise, as if they were three cans of paint. But a noise is exactly what is left when there is no note, no rhythm and no words, and drawn out it is a trembling tangle with no color in sight. What has color in something you can’t even see?

  • About 45 minutes

The idea in one picture

White light is a mixture of all colors in equal amounts, and the color of a light is the way it spreads its energy among them. Noise works much the same way, only the spread is across frequencies: drawn in time, a noise is always a tangle that never repeats, and what tells one apart from another is the shape of its spectrum, which can be flat, sloped or more steeply sloped.

The spectrum of a single instant comes out hairy, because the noise is different every time, but the slope is always there. Move the averaged chunks and you will see that averaging removes the tremble without moving the line: it throws away what changes and keeps what stays. That same idea will later pull a tiny signal out from under the noise.

Step by step

A tremble with no memory

A random noise is a signal you can’t predict: even if you know every previous sample, you don’t know which one comes next. What does stay the same is its character, such as how loud it is or how it divides between low and high pitches, and that is why two different chunks of the same noise sound alike even though they look nothing alike.

The simplest is white noise, in which each sample has nothing to do with the one before, like a roll of dice. It is called white after light: white light carries all colors in equal amounts, and white noise carries the same power in every hertz. The random motion of electrons in a circuit produces white noise, and so does shower water landing on the tray, and it sounds like radio static.

The other two have more power in the low pitches: pink noise may remind you of rain, and brown noise, of a waterfall. Exactly what sets them apart is the subject of the steps ahead, but you can already listen to them.

Sleeping and focusing, according to the studies

Many people use these noises to sleep or to concentrate, and what the research says is more modest than the apps promise. A review of 38 studies on continuous noise and sleep concluded that the quality of the evidence that it helps you sleep is very low, with results ranging from better sleep to worse sleep. In a 2026 laboratory study with 25 healthy adults, pink noise reduced REM sleep, and earplugs protected better against aircraft noise. With babies, keep an eye on the volume: of 14 infant sleep machines, every one exceeded 50 decibels at 30 centimeters at maximum volume, which is the recommended limit in neonatal units, and three went above 85.

For concentration, a 2024 meta-analysis found a small benefit of white or pink noise in children and young people with ADHD, in laboratory tasks, and a slightly negative effect in those without it. For brown noise, so fashionable, it found not a single study. We don’t give health advice here: if something worries you, ask a professional.

Listen to the three noises and say which is white, which is pink and which is brown. All three sound equally loud: the only difference is how they spread their power between low and high pitches.

If you can’t listen, “Show the spectra” draws the one for each noise: white is the flattest, and the more a spectrum falls toward the high pitches, the lower that noise sounds.

The noise that turned out to be the universe

Every conductor, simply by being at some temperature, has electrons jiggling at random, and that jiggling produces thermal noise. J. B. Johnson measured it and H. Nyquist explained it, both in 1928. It is practically white in the radio and audio bands, and its power grows with temperature, so radio astronomers describe the noise of an antenna with a noise temperature: the temperature, in kelvin (degrees above absolute zero), at which a resistor would have to be to produce the same noise.

Between July 1964 and April 1965, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, of Bell Labs, measured the noise of a horn antenna about 6 meters long, in Holmdel (New Jersey), at 4,080 MHz. Pointing upward, the total noise temperature was 6.7 K. They could account for 2.3 K from the atmosphere, 0.8 K from losses in the antenna itself and less than 0.1 K from the back lobes, and about 3.5 K were still left over, the same in every direction and in every season of the year.

The excess did not rise when they pointed toward New York or toward the horizon and did not fit known radio sources, which have a different spectrum, so they suspected the antenna. Inside the horn, a pair of pigeons had nested and covered everything, as Wilson told it, with “a white dielectric material familiar to anyone who lives in a city.” They evicted the pigeons and cleaned up, and the temperature barely dropped. In the spring of 1965 they cleaned thoroughly again and covered the riveted joints with aluminum tape, and the excess did not drop either.

The explanation came through a conversation with Bernard Burke, of MIT, who told them about the work of Peebles in Dicke’s group at Princeton. Two letters were published together in The Astrophysical Journal: the measurement, by Penzias and Wilson, and its interpretation, by Dicke, Peebles, Roll and Wilkinson, because Penzias and Wilson left cosmology out of theirs on purpose. That excess was the cosmic microwave background, the glow left over from the young universe, and it earned them half of the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics. Today its temperature is known far more precisely: 2.72548 K.

Be a radio astronomer: how many kelvin of noise are left over in the antenna once you subtract everything they could explain? Type the result.

The color is the shape of the spectrum

To see the color of a noise you have to look at its spectrum. The spectrum of a noise has no peaks like that of a note: it is a continuous curve that tells you how much power there is in each hertz, and that is why it is called the power spectral density. If you draw it in decibels and with frequency on an octave scale, where each mark is double the previous one, the colors of noise are straight lines. An octave is the jump from 500 to 1,000 Hz, for example, like between two equal notes on the piano, one low and one high.

White noise is a flat line: the same power in every hertz. Pink drops 3 decibels every time the frequency doubles, and brown drops 6. The other two colors are their mirror images: blue and violet noise rise 3 and 6 decibels per octave, and that is why they have more highs than lows.

The name of brown noise does not come from any color, but from Robert Brown, a botanist who in 1827 studied the random trembling of pollen particles in water. That trembling, one random step after another, is the recipe for brown noise: each sample is the previous one plus a small random step, so the signal wanders instead of jumping without memory, as white does. The more the spectrum falls toward the high pitches, the more slowly the signal changes, and that is why brown is drawn as slow hills and white as a swarm of ants.

Not every color is defined

Only some names have a standard definition, such as white and pink; others are informal. Gray, for example, is not a slope: it is white noise corrected with an equal-loudness curve, so that the ear perceives it as equally loud at all frequencies.

Why white noise hisses

The fact that white noise spreads the same power in every hertz does not mean you hear it as equally low and high, and the reason lies in how frequencies are counted. An octave band is the strip between one frequency and its double, and each octave going up contains twice as many hertz as the one before: from 125 to 250 Hz there are 125 hertz, from 250 to 500 there are 250, and from 4,000 to 8,000 there are 4,000. With the same power in every hertz, each octave carries twice the energy of the one below it, which is 3 decibels more.

The ear counts notes in octaves, and that is why white noise sounds like a hiss, with half of its energy in the top octave. Pink noise corrects that split: its spectrum drops 3 decibels every time it goes up an octave, exactly what offsets the doubled number of hertz, so each octave carries the same energy. That is why it sounds balanced. Brown drops twice as much, with half the energy in each octave as in the previous one, and sounds low.

To turn white into pink, an equalizer like the one in lesson 6 is enough: you lower each octave by 3 decibels more than the previous one. And to turn it into brown, a filter that feeds on its own output works, like those in lesson 8: if each output sample is the previous one plus the incoming sample, the result is a random walk.

This is white noise divided into six octaves, and each bar is the energy of one. Drag the top of each bar until all the octaves carry the same energy, as in pink noise.

One chunk is not enough

If you compute the spectrum of one chunk of noise, you don’t get the clean line of the earlier drawings, but a hairy curve that wiggles about 5.6 decibels from one point to the next. That curve is called a periodogram, and it is hairy because the chunk is only one sample of a noise that comes out different every time.

What is hard to believe is that a longer chunk doesn’t fix it: it gives more resolution, that is, more points and closer together, but each point still jumps just as much. The way out is to cut the signal into many chunks, multiply each one by a Hann window, as in lesson 5, compute the spectrum of each chunk and average them. This is Welch’s method, and the tremble drops with the square root of the number of chunks: with about a hundred, which is typical, it comes down to around half a decibel.

The price is the one from the tuner in lesson 5. To have many chunks, each one has to be short, and a short chunk tells two nearby frequencies apart poorly. More chunks give a smoother but blurrier spectrum, and fewer chunks a sharper but hairier one, so you have to pick the middle ground according to what you want to see.

Averaging to find what is hidden

A noise is a nuisance to the extent that it covers up what you want to measure, and that extent is called the signal-to-noise ratio: how many times more power the signal has than the noise, usually in decibels. At 10 dB, the signal has ten times more power, which is about 3.2 times more amplitude; at 0 dB, the two have the same, and with negative values the noise wins.

Some signals are born far below the noise. When a click sounds in a newborn’s ear, the brainstem responds with an electrical signal of less than half a microvolt, an evoked potential, buried in a much larger electrical noise: about 2 µV if the baby is asleep and more than 20 µV if it moves. This test, auditory brainstem evoked potentials, is one of those used in newborn hearing screening in Spain. A single measurement won’t do, but the response always arrives the same and at the same instant after the click, and the noise doesn’t.

With synchronized averaging, which adds up hundreds of repetitions aligned with the click, the response adds up and the noise, which sometimes goes up and sometimes down, half cancels out. The remaining noise drops with the square root of the number of repetitions: with 100, it is down to a tenth, and with 10,000, to a hundredth. To divide it by ten you need a hundred times more repetitions, and that is why the test is done with the baby asleep: awake and moving, the noise is ten times larger and you would need a hundred times more.

A baby’s brain response to a click is buried in noise. Add up sweeps until the response is three times larger than the noise that remains.

In your voice

What to record. Segment 1 of the reference recording: the silence of your room, about 10 seconds with the phone still, without speaking or moving. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic example.

What to look at. Silence is not zero: it has a spectrum with a shape. The lab computes the one for a single chunk, which comes out hairy, and the one for all the chunks averaged, which comes out smooth, and it measures the slope between 200 and 4,000 Hz to tell you which color your room resembles. The low rumble of a refrigerator, a fan or traffic tilts the spectrum toward pink or brown, while the electrical noise of the microphone itself tends to be flat, like white. The hum of the power line may also show up, at 50 Hz in Europe (60 Hz in much of the Americas), as in lesson 1, together with its multiples.

There are three precautions. Phone microphones are designed for the voice, roughly between 200 and 5,000 Hz according to the phyphox documentation, and outside that range the slope is not reliable; that is why the fit is done between 200 and 4,000 Hz. A compressed audio file, such as an MP3 or a voice message, can alter the faintest parts, so the color of a compressed silence can’t be fully trusted. And a bump or a cough dominates the average, so the lab discards the chunks with much more energy than the rest.

What to remember

  1. A random noise can’t be predicted, but it can be measured, and what has color is the shape of its spectrum: white spreads the same power in every hertz, pink the same energy in every octave, with a 3 dB drop per octave, and brown is a random walk that drops 6.
  2. The spectrum of a single chunk of noise comes out hairy, and a longer chunk doesn’t fix it. You have to cut it into windowed chunks and average, which is Welch’s method, at the cost of losing resolution.
  3. Averaging aligned repetitions keeps the signal and lowers the noise with the square root of their number: to divide it by ten you need a hundred times more repetitions. That is how a newborn’s brain response to a click shows up.

Common mistakes

  • “White means the values follow a bell curve.” White is about the spectrum: the same power in every hertz, with samples unrelated to each other. Values spreading out in a bell shape, which is called Gaussian, is a different property: there is white noise that isn’t Gaussian and Gaussian noise that isn’t white.
  • “Pink is just white with fewer highs.” Pink is a precise slope, 3 dB less per octave, which spreads the same energy across every octave. An arbitrary filter that removes highs does not give pink noise.
  • “A longer chunk gives a less noisy spectrum.” It gives more resolution with the same level of noise. To smooth it you have to average chunks, and that costs resolution.
  • “Averaging twice as many times divides the noise by two.” It divides it by the square root of two. To divide it by two you need four times more repetitions, and to divide it by ten, a hundred times more.
  • “It has been proven that white or pink noise helps you sleep.” The published evidence is of very low quality, and in a recent laboratory study pink noise reduced REM sleep. For concentration, the only thing published is small effects in young people with ADHD.

Math corner

The formulas, for anyone who wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The signal-to-noise ratio

The power of the signal is compared with that of the noise and given in decibels. With RMS amplitudes, the factor is 20 instead of 10 because power goes with the square of the amplitude:

\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\,\log_{10}\frac{P_s}{P_n} = 20\,\log_{10}\frac{A_s}{A_n}

The power spectral density

It tells you how much power there is per hertz, and the power of a band is the area under the curve between its two frequencies:

P_{[f_1,\,f_2]} = \int_{f_1}^{f_2} S(f)\,\mathrm{d}f

White noise

A noise is white when its density is constant, and that is equivalent to each sample having nothing to do with the others: its autocorrelation, from lesson 9, is nonzero only at zero lag. With the variance \sigma^2 and the Kronecker delta \delta:

S(f) = \text{constant} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r[\ell] = \sigma^2\,\delta[\ell]

The colors as powers of frequency

The density of the colored noises falls like a power of frequency. In decibels per octave, the slope equals minus ten times \beta times the logarithm of 2: zero for white (\beta = 0), -3.01 for pink (\beta = 1), -6.02 for brown (\beta = 2), and +3.01 and +6.02 for blue and violet (\beta = -1 and \beta = -2):

S(f) = \frac{C}{f^{\beta}}, \qquad \text{slope} = -10\,\beta\,\log_{10}2 \approx -3.01\,\beta \ \text{dB per octave}

The energy of an octave

The energy of an octave is the area under the density between a frequency f and its double. In white it grows with f (it doubles per octave), in pink it is always the same, and in brown it is cut in half per octave:

\int_{f}^{2f} \frac{C}{x^{\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \begin{cases} C\,f & \beta = 0 \\ C\,\ln 2 & \beta = 1 \\ \dfrac{C}{2f} & \beta = 2 \end{cases}

The random walk

In brown noise, each sample is the previous one plus a random step w[n]. Far below half the sampling rate, its density falls like the inverse of the square of the frequency:

y[n] = y[n-1] + w[n] \;\Longrightarrow\; S_y(f) = \frac{\sigma^2}{4\,\sin^2(\pi f/f_s)} \approx \frac{\sigma^2 f_s^2}{4\pi^2 f^2} \quad (f \ll f_s)

Averaging repetitions

If each repetition is the same signal s plus a different noise with deviation \sigma, the average keeps s and divides the noise by the square root of N. The signal-to-noise ratio in power improves N times, which is 10\,\log_{10} N decibels:

\bar{x}[n] = s[n] + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k[n], \qquad \sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}

The periodogram

It is the spectrum of a single chunk, with its window w[n]. For Gaussian noise, each value fluctuates as much as its own mean (it follows an exponential distribution), and that does not improve with the length of the chunk: the deviation is about 5.6 dB.

P(f) = \frac{1}{f_s \sum_n w[n]^2}\,\Big|\sum_{n} w[n]\,x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi f n/f_s}\Big|^2

Welch’s method

It averages K periodograms of windowed chunks. The relative deviation drops as one over the square root of K and, in decibels, comes out at about 4.34 over the square root of K:

\hat{S}_W(f) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} P_k(f), \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{10}{\ln 10}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}} \approx \frac{4.34}{\sqrt{K}}

Thermal noise and noise temperature

A conductor at temperature T gives a noise power proportional to T and to the bandwidth B, with Boltzmann’s constant k. That is why a noise can be measured as a temperature. At 290 K, kT is about 4.0\cdot 10^{-21} watts per hertz, which is about -174 dBm per hertz:

P = k\,T\,B, \qquad kT\big|_{290\,\mathrm{K}} \approx 4.0\cdot 10^{-21}\ \mathrm{W/Hz} \approx -174\ \mathrm{dBm/Hz}, \qquad T_A = \frac{P}{k\,B}

The Penzias and Wilson arithmetic

What is left over is what remains after subtracting the known contributions from the measured total:

T_{\text{excess}} = 6.7 - 2.3 - 0.8 - 0.1 \approx 3.5\ \mathrm{K}
