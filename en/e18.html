Moving the energy to its place: phase and reassignment

A spectrogram paints a pure note as a wide band and a sharp knock as a thick column, and that blur is blamed on the fact that the signal is viewed through a window. To draw it, each slice was run through Fourier, and the result for each slice has two pieces of data, the size and the starting point, of which only the size was painted. What if the information for sharpening the picture was right there in the half we threw away?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

A spectrogram, like the one in lesson 11, divides the energy into tiles that cannot be shrunk (lesson 12), and each cell paints the average of what falls inside its tile. That energy does not have to be at the center, and the phase of the cell, which the spectrogram throws away, says where it actually is.

Move the control by hand and notice that the dots do not all move the same way: those of the note only go up or down to their line, those of the knock move sideways, and those of the chirp travel diagonally. Each one goes to its own place, and the phase knows where.

Step by step

The half we throw away

The spectrogram from lesson 11 is made by cutting the signal with a sliding window, running Fourier on each slice and painting one column of color per slice. Each cell of that column is the result of comparing the slice with a test wheel, as in lesson 4, and a wheel has two pieces of data: the size, which is the only one that gets painted, and the starting point, which is the phase of the cell and gets thrown away.

Does it matter what gets thrown away? There is a very well-known demonstration with images. An image can also be broken down into wheels, in this case into stripes, each with its own size and starting point. If you take two pictures, compute the transform of each and cross the halves, so that one picture supplies the sizes and the other the starting points, what you see is the picture that supplied the phase. This is called a phase swap, and it is what the challenge below does with two synthetic pictures made on this very page.

Alan Oppenheim, Jae Lim and their colleagues studied the matter in the late 1970s. An image made only from the phase, with all the sizes set equal, keeps the edges of the original like an enhanced, somewhat noisy version, whereas one made only from the sizes is a small bright patch in the center, with no resemblance to the original.

A nuance

Phase is not “more important” than size in general. In a whole image or a long sound it holds the edges and the positions, but in short stretches of speech the ear relies mostly on the size of each note, as those authors pointed out in the same 1979 work. What reassignment takes advantage of is not the phase itself, but how it changes.

Get an image that resembles the letters using the sizes of the wheel. Choose which picture the sizes come from and which one the starting points come from; the readouts tell you how much the result resembles each original.

The arrow that turns too much

Think of each cell as an arrow whose length is the size and whose direction is the phase. That arrow comes from comparing the slice with the test wheel of its row, and it behaves like the strobe from lesson 2: if the wheel hidden in the signal turns at exactly the speed of the test wheel, the arrow stays still; if it turns a bit faster, the arrow advances slowly, and if it turns a bit slower, it goes backward. The arrow therefore turns at the speed of the difference.

That gives an instrument much finer than the width of the blob. A cell in the 1,000 Hz row belongs to a blob that spans tens of hertz, but if its arrow turns 36° too much in 2 milliseconds, the wheel inside it makes 50 more turns per second than its row. It is the instantaneous frequency from lesson 15, read inside each cell, and that is why it is called local instantaneous frequency. An equivalent trick was already used in 1966 in the phase vocoder of Flanagan and Golden, which calculated the frequency of each channel this way.

The phase also holds the instant. If instead of comparing two consecutive snapshots you compare two neighboring rows, how the phase changes from one to the other reveals when what is in that cell really happened, the local group delay. A knock is the clearest example, because its phase changes from row to row at the rate set by the exact instant it landed.

Calculate the real speed of the wheel inside the cell of the 1,000 Hz row. The arrow has moved between two snapshots a few milliseconds apart, and how far it has turned tells you everything you need.

Each dot, to its center of gravity

Each value of the spectrogram is an average: the energy of the signal that falls inside its tile, spread in any possible way. The spectrogram always hangs it at the center of the tile, which is like weighing a plank with the weights piled up at one end and assuming all the weight is in the middle. The place where it belongs is the center of gravity.

The two readings of the phase, the local frequency and the local instant, are exactly the two coordinates of that center of gravity. Carrying each value of the spectrogram there is reassignment, and the resulting picture is the reassigned spectrogram: the clouds of energy shrink until they become lines.

The idea was published in the late 1970s by Kodera, Gendrin and de Villedary, in a geophysics journal and under the name “modified moving window method.” It was little used for years and, according to Auger and his colleagues, for a specific reason: from the image it produced there was no way back to the signal. In 1995, Auger and Flandrin gave it the name reassignment and a cheaper recipe: instead of differentiating the phase, you compute three transforms, one with the window, one with its derivative and one with the window multiplied by time.

With a tone, a linear chirp or a knock, the reassigned image is in theory a line with no thickness, whatever the window. The spectrogram, on the other hand, forces you to choose between a short window, which sharpens the knock, and a tailored one, which sharpens the chirp. The windows in this chapter are Gaussian and their width is given by a single number, σ, the deviation in milliseconds, which is the one you will move in the challenges.

Make the knock and the chirp come out sharp at the same time: the chirp under 5 Hz wide and the knock under 1 ms. Move the window and switch between the spectrogram and the reassigned one.

What reassignment does not fix

Reassignment does not break the limit from lesson 12. It moves each value to its center of gravity, but if two notes fit in the same tile, the energy of both is mixed inside each cell and the center of gravity of a mixture falls between them. No phase can split them apart. For them to come out as two lines, the separation between the notes has to be greater than the half-height width of the blob of a single note: about 37 Hz with a 10 ms window, and less the longer the window is.

Noise is the other enemy. A value with almost no energy also has a phase, and reassignment moves it just like the others, so the noise turns into a shower of bright specks. That is why an energy threshold is set and what falls below it is not moved, with the idea of leaving the noise out without losing the weak parts that matter. You will be able to move it in “In your voice.”

The two notes in the figure, at 1,000 and 1,040 Hz, have to come out as two thin lines: make more than 90% of the reassigned energy fall within 4 Hz of the notes. You can only move the window.

A perfect picture, with ghosts

There is another picture of the time–frequency plane that nails a chirp without the blur of the spectrogram: the Wigner–Ville distribution, which Wigner defined in 1932 in quantum physics and Ville brought to signals in 1948. Its problem appears as soon as there are two components. Besides drawing the two, it draws a third one, a ghost, exactly halfway between them, which flickers between positive and negative values, and a negative energy makes no sense.

The relation with what came before is simple: the spectrogram is the Wigner–Ville picture smoothed with that of the window itself. Smoothing erases the ghosts, but it blurs everything else, which is exactly what happened to the spectrogram. Reassignment sharpens the picture again and, according to Auger and his colleagues, gets rid of most of the interference.

Place the second note so that the ghost lands on the orange target. Tap the plane to place it; the first note does not move.

A picture with no way back

Reassignment gives a wonderful picture, but only a picture. To get back to the sound you would need three things, and it has lost all three. It keeps energy and not complete numbers, so the phase is gone. It also moves values in time, so they are no longer at the instant they were measured. And it stacks at a single point values that came from different places, which can no longer be separated. As Auger and his colleagues sum it up, with the reassigned image there is no simple way to reconstruct the components.

You can check it with your ear, by undoing each picture with what it has: the complete transform with its phase, the spectrogram, which only keeps the sizes, and the reassigned one. The sentence that plays is synthetic. The next lesson starts right here, because the phase has what is needed to sharpen the picture and it will have to be used without losing the way back.

What to look at. On the left, the spectrogram, and on the right, the reassigned one with the same window. In a sung melody, each harmonic should be a thin line that goes up and down with your intonation, and vibrato a clean wave; abrupt attacks, like claps, go from wide columns to vertical streaks. With background noise, specks appear all over the plane, and then it is a good idea to raise the threshold. With a very low voice and a very short window, the harmonics get in each other’s way and come out as blobs again.

What to remember

  1. The spectrogram throws away the phase, and the phase holds where things are: how much the arrow of each cell turns gives the local instantaneous frequency, and how it changes from one row to the next gives the instant.
  2. Reassignment carries each value of the spectrogram to its center of gravity, and the clouds of energy shrink until they become lines. It nails tones, linear chirps and knocks, but it does not break the uncertainty: two notes inside the same tile still get in each other’s way, and the noise moves too.
  3. The reassigned image has no way back, because it has thrown away the phase, moved values to other instants and stacked points. The question that remains is whether you can sharpen it without losing the way back.

Common mistakes

  • “The spectrogram has everything; the phase is noise that can be thrown away.” The phase holds where things are: in an image, the edges, and in the spectrogram, the local frequency and the instant of each blob.
  • “Reassignment beats uncertainty.” It only sharpens when one component dominates each tile. Two notes closer together than the blob of a single one measures keep getting in each other’s way, no matter how much phase there is.
  • “Reassignment takes each point to the maximum of its blob, without leaving its column.” It takes it to its center of gravity, calculated with the phase, which can be at another instant. In a tone the point only goes up or down to the line, but in a chirp it travels diagonally to the straight line, which is why it comes out perfect.
  • “The Wigner–Ville picture is the perfect one.” It is, with a single component. With two, it draws a ghost halfway between them that oscillates and can be negative.
  • “From the reassigned spectrogram I can hear the signal again.” No: it has thrown away the phase, moved values to other instants and stacked points that came from different places.

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The short-time transform and the spectrogram

Each cell compares the signal, cut by a window g centered at the instant t, with a test wheel of frequency f, and the spectrogram keeps the squared size:

V_x(t,f) = \int x(s)\,g(s-t)\,e^{-i2\pi f s}\,ds, \qquad S_x(t,f) = \left|V_x(t,f)\right|^2

The phase tells the frequency and the instant

If the cell is referred to the center of its window, \tilde V_x, the speed at which its phase turns in time is the local instantaneous frequency, and how its phase changes with frequency gives the group delay, which corrects the instant:

\tilde V_x(t,f) = e^{i2\pi f t}\,V_x(t,f), \qquad \hat f(t,f) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\arg \tilde V_x(t,f), \qquad \hat t(t,f) = t - \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial f}\arg \tilde V_x(t,f)

Auger and Flandrin’s recipe

The same coordinates, without differentiating the phase: three transforms are computed, with the window, with its derivative, in 1/s, and with the window multiplied by time, in s, and they are divided by one another:

\hat f(t,f) = f - \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\operatorname{Im}\!\left\{\frac{V_x^{g'}(t,f)}{V_x^{g}(t,f)}\right\}, \qquad \hat t(t,f) = t + \operatorname{Re}\!\left\{\frac{V_x^{t g}(t,f)}{V_x^{g}(t,f)}\right\}

The arrow between two snapshots

If the arrow of row f_k turns \Delta\varphi radians too much in \Delta t seconds, the wheel inside it runs at this frequency, with the turn measured between -\pi and \pi:

\hat f \approx f_k + \frac{\Delta\varphi}{2\pi\,\Delta t}, \qquad \Delta\varphi \in (-\pi, \pi]

With the numbers from the text, 36° is 0.2\pi radians and, in 2 ms, the wheel inside is at this distance from its row:

\frac{0.2\pi}{2\pi\cdot 0.002} = 50\ \text{Hz}

The reassigned spectrogram

Each value of the spectrogram is added at the point its reassigned coordinates point to:

\hat S_x(t',f') = \iint S_x(t,f)\,\delta\big(t' - \hat t(t,f)\big)\,\delta\big(f' - \hat f(t,f)\big)\,dt\,df

A chirp lands on its line

If the frequency rises \alpha hertz per second, each reassigned point obeys the chirp law, and that is why the image comes out as a perfect straight line:

x(t) = e^{i2\pi\left(f_0 t + \alpha t^2/2\right)} \;\Rightarrow\; \hat f = f_0 + \alpha\,\hat t

The center of gravity

Each value of the spectrogram is an average of the signal’s Wigner–Ville distribution, weighted by that of the window around (t,f), and the reassigned coordinates are the center of mass of that average:

\hat t(t,f) = \frac{\iint s\,W_x(s,\xi)\,W_g(s-t,\xi-f)\,ds\,d\xi}{S_x(t,f)}, \qquad \hat f(t,f) = \frac{\iint \xi\,W_x(s,\xi)\,W_g(s-t,\xi-f)\,ds\,d\xi}{S_x(t,f)}

The Wigner–Ville distribution and its ghosts

It compares the signal a little forward with the signal a little backward, for all delays, and runs Fourier on that product:

W_x(t,f) = \int x\!\left(t + \tfrac{\tau}{2}\right)\,x^{*}\!\left(t - \tfrac{\tau}{2}\right)\,e^{-i2\pi f\tau}\,d\tau

That of a sum is not the sum of those of each part, because a cross-term is left over:

W_{x_1+x_2} = W_{x_1} + W_{x_2} + 2\,\operatorname{Re}\,W_{x_1,x_2}

With two notes at (t_1, f_1) and (t_2, f_2), the cross-term is at the midpoint and oscillates at a frequency that grows with the distance between the notes:

(t_m, f_m) = \left(\tfrac{t_1+t_2}{2},\, \tfrac{f_1+f_2}{2}\right), \qquad 2\,\operatorname{Re}\,W_{x_1,x_2} \propto \cos\!\big(2\pi\left[(f_1-f_2)(t-t_m) - (t_1-t_2)(f-f_m)\right] + \phi\big)

The spectrogram is that picture smoothed with that of the window, and that is why it erases the ghosts at the cost of blurring:

S_x(t,f) = \iint W_x(s,\xi)\,W_g(s-t,\ \xi-f)\,ds\,d\xi

Minimum separation between two notes

With a Gaussian window of deviation \sigma, the half-height width of the blob of a single note is the following quantity, which is the minimum separation in hertz for two notes to come out as two lines:

\Delta f_{\min} = \frac{\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{\pi\,\sigma}

With \sigma = 10 ms that is about 37 Hz, and with 12 ms, about 31 Hz.

The phase swap

You take the size of the transform of an image A and the phase of that of another image B, and undo the transform:

I_{AB} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{\,\left|\mathcal{F}\{A\}\right|\;e^{\,i\arg \mathcal{F}\{B\}}\right\}
