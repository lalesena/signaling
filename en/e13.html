Wavelets: a microscope for signals

Your ear follows a very fast cymbal and a very long low note at the same time. A spectrogram, on the other hand, looks at every frequency through the same window, and you already know a window can’t be short and long at once. To see the cymbal well you need a window of a few milliseconds, and to separate two low notes you need one of about 150 milliseconds or more. What if the microscope changed its magnification depending on what it was looking at?

  • About 55 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 11, the spectrogram cut the sound with a sliding window, and in lesson 12 you saw that each cell of that map is a tile that can be stretched or squashed, but not shrunk. With a fixed window, all the tiles on the map are the same, from the lows to the highs.

A wavelet is a short wave, just a few turns long, that you use as a template, like the test Ferris wheel of lesson 4 or the sliding template of lesson 9. The difference is that a wavelet has no fixed size: it stretches to look for low notes and shrinks to look for high ones, and at each instant it measures how much it resembles the signal. Move the frequency it looks for and notice that the drawing of the wavelet doesn’t change shape, only size.

Step by step

A zoom that adapts

To recognize a note you need a few turns of its Ferris wheel, say four. Four turns of a 4,000 Hz wheel last one millisecond, and four turns of a 50 Hz wheel last about 80 milliseconds. On top of that, high sounds tend to be brief, like a cymbal or a consonant, and low sounds tend to last, like a bass note. It makes sense to look at the highs with a short window and at the lows with a long one, instead of using the same window for everything.

Music asks for the same thing from another angle. On a piano, one semitone multiplies the frequency by 1.0595, so in hertz the distance between notes grows with pitch: between the two lowest keys there are 1.6 Hz, and between the two highest, about 235 Hz. With the rule from lesson 12, separating the low notes takes listening for more than a second, while for the high ones less than a hundredth of a second would do. The seven A notes of the piano, from 55 to 3,520 Hz, are each at double the frequency of the one before. That distance, from a note to the one at double its frequency, is an octave, and on the keyboard all octaves measure the same, even though in hertz each one is twice as wide as the previous one.

The seven A notes of the piano play one after another, and below you can see their spectrum over time. Choose the frequency axis on which the seven end up at the same distance from each other.

The mother wavelet and its copies

You start from a single short wave, with as many ups as downs so that its average is zero, called the mother wavelet. From it you make copies of the same shape, more stretched or more shrunk, and the amount of stretching is the scale: a stretched copy looks for low frequencies and a shrunk one looks for high ones. Each copy is then placed at every instant of the signal, which is called translation, and at each place you measure the resemblance, which is the same old question: multiply point by point and add up the area.

The wavelet most used with sounds is the Morlet wavelet: a Ferris wheel inside a Gaussian bell, that is, the Gabor atom from lesson 12. Its setting, written w0, says how many turns it has inside the bell. With the most common value, w0 = 6, you see about four oscillations, and its scale almost exactly matches the period of the note it looks for. It makes no difference whether it looks for 100 Hz or 2,000 Hz: it always has the same four turns, and the only thing that changes is the time it takes to make them.

Wavelets weren’t born in a math department but while looking for oil. In the late 1970s, Jean Morlet, an engineer at the French oil company Elf Aquitaine, was trying to read better the echoes that layers of rock send back when a wave is sent into the ground, waves of a few tens of hertz, as low as a bass. He thought of comparing them with short waves, all with the same shape but stretched to different sizes, and called them waves of constant shape. His colleagues didn’t take it very seriously, and the physicist Alex Grossmann, in Marseille, gave it its mathematical form: in 1984 they published the continuous wavelet transform together.

This wave is a pure note. Stretch or shrink the Morlet wavelet, using the frequency control, until it fits the wave: its crests have to land on the crests of the wave. The amber curve says how much they resemble each other for every frequency it could look for.

Tiles that change shape

The Morlet wavelet is a Ferris wheel clipped by a Gaussian bell, and that is exactly the signal that, according to lesson 12, reaches the minimum tile area. So each wavelet has a tile of minimum area, but its shape depends on pitch. At the top, with the high notes, the wavelet is very short and its tile is narrow in time and tall in frequency: you know very well when, but not so well which one. At the bottom, with the low notes, the wavelet is long and its tile is wide and short: you know very well which one, but not so well when.

What stays the same is the proportion. In constant relative resolution, the width of each tile in frequency is a fixed fraction of its frequency, about 12% with w0 = 6. It is the same thing a third-octave graphic equalizer does, like the one in lesson 6, with up to 31 bands spaced a third of an octave apart: its filters are narrow in hertz at the bottom and wide at the top. The sliders of a graphic equalizer are already spaced the way wavelets are.

Every tile has the same area, the Gabor minimum, but not the same shape. Place the three in their frequency band: the tall narrow one, the square one, and the short wide one. You can drag them or choose their band with the selectors.

The scalogram and the hits

If you repeat the measurement at every scale and at every instant, you get a map: the scalogram. It has time running to the right, frequency going up on a logarithmic axis, on which each octave takes up the same space, and energy in magma colors, in decibels. The whole procedure is the continuous wavelet transform. In this lesson the color measures amplitude: a pure note of amplitude one shows up at 0 dB in its row.

A hit is what wavelets see best. A click lights up all the scales at the same instant: at the top, where the wavelets are very short, it leaves a thin line that points almost to the exact instant, and at the bottom, where they are long, it widens into a cone that points at that instant. That is why wavelets are good for finding hits, cracks in a material, or the arrival of an earthquake.

An earthquake sends two waves that travel at different speeds. The first to arrive, the P wave, feels like a sharp thump, and a few seconds later the S wave arrives, a sway that usually does more damage; S waves generally travel at about 60% of the speed of P waves. Finding on a seismogram the exact instant when each one starts means looking for a sudden change, and that is one of the things wavelets do best. Earthquake early warning systems take advantage of those seconds: sensors detect the P wave and send out the alert before the strong shaking arrives, which in the best case can give tens of seconds.

Near the start and the end of the signal, long wavelets spill out of it and the result gets distorted. The unreliable zone is called the cone of influence, and it is wider the lower the row is: on each side it measures about 1.4 times the scale, a little more than one period of the note it looks for.

There is a click hidden in a sustained note, so faint that it is barely visible in the wave. Tap on the scalogram the instant when it happens, within a margin of 3 milliseconds.

The cymbal and the bass

Here is what the spectrogram couldn’t do. Imagine a bass playing two low notes at once, a whole step apart, with a cymbal ticking away on top. To separate the two low notes you need a long window, and to see each hit of the cymbal you need a short one. With a spectrogram there is no window that works for both.

With a wavelet there is a setting that does. The w0 setting is the number of turns of the wavelet: if you raise it, the wavelet is longer, and it gains sharpness in frequency at the expense of time. But it does so in its own way, because the duration of the wavelet still depends on pitch: with a high w0, the wavelet for the lows lasts tens of milliseconds and the one for the highs lasts barely more than one. That doesn’t break the rule from lesson 12, because each tile has the same minimum area; what it does is distribute sharpness differently, long at the bottom and short at the top.

With a single control, manage to see at the same time the two low notes separated and each hit of the cymbal, with a gap of at least 3 dB in both cases. Try the spectrogram first, and then the wavelet.

What wavelets don’t solve

Wavelets don’t see better than the spectrogram at everything; they distribute sharpness in a different way. At the top they are blurrier in frequency than a long-window spectrogram, and that is why the high harmonics of a voice melt together: they are always at the same distance in hertz, the distance of the note, but the tiles get taller with frequency. At the bottom they are blurrier in time than a short-window one.

There is also more than one way to color the map. If you normalize by energy, a low sine comes out stronger than a high one of the same amplitude and white noise comes out flat; if you normalize by amplitude, the sines come out equal and white noise grows toward the highs. It pays to always look at the legend and know which normalization was used.

The ear resembles a wavelet transform in one respect, but it isn’t one. In the cochlea, each stretch of a coiled membrane vibrates with certain frequencies, and the filters they form are narrow for the lows and wide for the highs. Above about a thousand hertz, their width grows almost in proportion to frequency, just as in wavelets; below that, it stays at a few tens of hertz, more like a fixed window. Besides, the ear’s response changes with loudness, it is not linear, and the brain makes use of the moment when nerve impulses arrive. Wavelets resemble the ear, but they are not the ear.

This synthetic “aah” has its harmonics in horizontal lines, and at the top you can see little vertical lines, one for each pulse of the vocal cords. With w0 = 6, count how many harmonics show up separated and type the number. Then adjust w0 to see at least five separated harmonics without losing the little lines at the top.

The picture is still blurry, because the Gabor limit still rules. And one question remains unanswered: what the scalogram shows in each cell is an energy, but what exactly is “the frequency at this instant”? That is what lesson 15 is about.

In your voice

What to record. Three claps followed by a long “aah” on a comfortable note, which are segments 6 and 3 of the series’ reference recording, or any other short segment. Clap away from your phone so they don’t clip. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples. At most 4 seconds are analyzed.

What to look at. The Morlet scalogram of your audio, with a 32 ms spectrogram next to it, with the same logarithmic axis and the same color scale. In the claps, the vertical lines are very thin at the top and widen toward the bottom, like a cone. In your “aah,” two or three lines show up separated at the bottom, and higher up the harmonics melt into a band, with little vertical lines at the rhythm of your vocal cords. Raise w0 and you will see more harmonics separate, at the cost of widening the lines in time. The shading at the edges is the cone of influence, where you shouldn’t trust the picture.

What to remember

  1. A wavelet is a short wave of a few turns that stretches to look for lows and shrinks to look for highs, always with the same shape. The scalogram is the map of its resemblance to the signal, at all scales and at all instants.
  2. Each tile of a wavelet has the minimum Gabor area, but its shape changes with pitch: narrow and tall at the top, wide and short at the bottom. The relative resolution is constant, like the octaves of a piano, and that is why the axis is logarithmic.
  3. Wavelets don’t break the limit from lesson 12; they distribute sharpness differently. They locate hits and sudden changes very well and separate the lows, but at the top the harmonics melt together, and the picture is still blurry.

Common mistakes

  • “The scale is the frequency.” They go in opposite directions: a large scale looks for low frequencies. The conversion depends on the wavelet: with Morlet and w0 = 6, the period is 1.03 times the scale.
  • “Wavelets see better than the spectrogram at everything.” They distribute sharpness differently. At the top they are blurrier in frequency than a long-window spectrogram, and at the bottom, blurrier in time than a short-window one. The area of each tile is still the Gabor minimum.
  • “At the top, the harmonics melt together because of noise.” They melt together because the tiles are tall there. The harmonics are always at the same distance in hertz, but the width of the wavelet grows with frequency.
  • “In the scalogram, the brightest row is the one that sounds loudest.” It depends on the normalization: by energy, the lows look stronger, and by amplitude, noise looks stronger at the top. You have to look at the legend.
  • “What you see at the edges is part of the signal.” Near the start and the end, long wavelets spill out of the signal and the result gets distorted. That is the cone of influence, wider in the lows.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The family of copies

Each copy is the mother wavelet stretched to scale a and shifted to instant b. The factor in front chooses how it is normalized: one over a preserves amplitude, and one over the square root of a preserves energy:

\psi_{a,b}(t) = \frac{1}{a}\,\psi\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right) \quad\text{or}\quad \psi_{a,b}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\,\psi\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)

The continuous wavelet transform

For each scale and each instant you measure the resemblance between the signal and the copy: you multiply point by point, using the conjugate of the copy, and add up the area:

W(a,b) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,\overline{\psi_{a,b}(t)}\,dt

The Morlet wavelet

It is a Ferris wheel that turns \omega_0 radians per unit of scale, inside a Gaussian bell. The subtracted term ensures a zero average and is negligible if \omega_0 \ge 5:

\psi(t) = \pi^{-1/4}\left(e^{i\omega_0 t} - e^{-\omega_0^2/2}\right) e^{-t^2/2}

From scale to frequency

The peak of the response is at \omega_0 divided by two pi times the scale. Torrence and Compo use the equivalent Fourier period, which with \omega_0 = 6 is 1.03 times the scale:

f_{\text{peak}} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi a}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{4\pi a}{\omega_0 + \sqrt{2+\omega_0^2}} \approx 1.03\,a

Admissibility

To be able to reconstruct the signal, the energy of the wavelet’s spectrum divided by the frequency has to be finite. In practice, that requires a zero average:

C_\psi = \int_0^{\infty} \frac{|\Psi(\omega)|^2}{\omega}\,d\omega < \infty \;\Rightarrow\; \int \psi(t)\,dt = 0

Tiles of minimum area and variable shape

At scale a, the energy of the Morlet is spread in time with a standard deviation of a divided by the square root of two, and in frequency with a fixed proportion of its frequency. The product is always the Gabor minimum:

\sigma_t = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad \frac{\sigma_f}{f} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\omega_0}, \qquad \sigma_t\,\sigma_f = \frac{1}{4\pi}

Constant Q

The center frequency divided by the width doesn’t depend on the frequency. With \omega_0 = 6, the width is 11.8% of the frequency:

Q = \frac{f}{\delta f} = \text{constant}

Reconstruction

With energy normalization, the signal is recovered by adding up all the copies weighted by their resemblances, according to Grossmann and Morlet:

x(t) = \frac{1}{C_\psi}\int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} W(a,b)\,\psi_{a,b}(t)\,\frac{db\,da}{a^2}

Cone of influence

Near the edges, within root two times the scale, the result is not reliable:

\tau_{\text{edge}}(a) = \sqrt{2}\,a

The semitone

Each semitone multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of two, and in hertz the distance grows with the note:

f_{n+1} = 2^{1/12} f_n, \qquad \Delta f \approx 0.0595\,f

The ear’s filters

Glasberg and Moore (1990) gave their equivalent width in hertz, for frequencies in hertz between 100 and 10,000:

\mathrm{ERB}(f) = 24.7\,\left(4.37\,\frac{f}{1000} + 1\right)

Distance to an earthquake

It is obtained from the difference in arrival time between the S and P waves and their two speeds:

d = \Delta t\,\frac{V_P\,V_S}{V_P - V_S}
