Level 1 · Lesson 1

Every wave is a Ferris wheel

A tuning fork in front of a microphone, someone whistling, and the voltage at a wall outlet as an electrician’s instruments draw it. A piece of metal, the air coming out of a mouth, and the electricity in a house have nothing to do with each other, and yet all three draw almost the same curve. Why that curve and not another?

  • About 35 minutes

The idea in one picture

A sinusoid is what you see of something that spins when you look only at its height, like the shadow of a wheel cast on a wall. That same curve is also the natural motion of everything that vibrates, which is why it shows up in a tuning fork, in the wall outlet, and in a whistle.

Change the size or the speed of the wheel and watch where the change appears: it is born next to the wheel and moves away to the right, because the wave is the history of the cabin. If you turn on the orange cabin, you will see two histories running side by side.

Step by step

The shadow of a Ferris wheel

The air pressure in front of a microphone goes up and down, and so does the voltage at a wall outlet. A pulse, the temperature on a street, or the price of bread all change over time, and anything that changes and can be measured is a signal. To draw one you only need two axes: time and what is being measured.

A word with a history

“Sine,” the other name of this curve, comes from a bowstring. Indian mathematicians called the chord joining two points of a circle jyā. Arabic translators copied the sound as jība, which meant nothing in Arabic and was written without vowels, and in twelfth-century Latin translations it was read as jayb, “fold” or “pocket,” and translated as sinus. Through a misreading, the most important curve in the course is named after a fold.

Picture a carnival Ferris wheel turning slowly and watch a single cabin. Its height rises, reaches the top, drops, passes the bottom, and rises again. If you jot down that height at every instant and keep laying what you jotted down off to one side, a smooth, repeating curve appears: the sinusoid, or sine wave. Each point of the curve tells where the cabin was at some moment in the past.

What a phone stores, on the other hand, is numbers. When you record audio, the microphone jots down how hard the air is pushing tens of thousands of times per second, so, for a machine, a signal is a list of numbers in order. In the challenge you will see that list grow while you turn the wheel.

Grab the Ferris wheel with your finger or the mouse and give it one full turn. As you turn, the trace jots down the height of the green cabin.

Turns per second

The Ferris wheel brings, for free, the whole vocabulary of waves, and the first word is speed. If the wheel turns faster, the cabin goes up and down more times in the same time and the waves get squeezed together. The number of turns it makes in one second is the frequency, and it is measured in hertz: one hertz is one turn, one cycle, per second. The name honors Heinrich Hertz, the first to show that electromagnetic waves exist.

How long one turn takes is the period. Frequency and period are two sides of the same thing: if the wheel makes four turns per second, each turn lasts a quarter of a second.

The wall outlet at home is the most literal case. Much of our electricity comes from spinning generators, and a typical one in a thermal power plant has two poles and makes 3,000 turns per minute, which is fifty per second. Each turn pushes the voltage up and down once, and that is why the European grid runs at 50 Hz (so the outlet in Spain does too). Much of the Americas uses 60 Hz, and Japan has both, because in 1895 Tokyo bought German 50 Hz generators and, the following year, Osaka bought American 60 Hz ones. Today, in addition, solar panels and batteries produce that same curve with electronics, without spinning, imitating the Ferris wheel.

This wave lasts exactly one second. Count how many turns it makes and write its frequency; then write how long each turn lasts, its period.

If it helps, tap the crests to number them. You can write the period as “0.25”, as “1/4”, or as “250 ms”.

Size and speed can be heard

The second word is size. If the wheel is bigger, the cabin goes higher and lower, and the wave grows. That maximum height, measured from the center, is called the amplitude.

When the wave is a sound, each word is heard in its own way. Frequency is heard as the note: a Ferris wheel that turned 440 times per second would sound like the A that an orchestra tunes to, the one from the most common tuning fork. Amplitude is heard as loudness, so a big wheel sounds loud and a small one sounds quiet. The sensation of loudness also depends on the note, but this is enough to start with. We hear, more or less, between 20 and 20,000 Hz, and the upper limit drops with age.

Listen to the two tones and pick the higher one, the one with the higher note. Careful: the higher one is not always the louder one. Get three correct in a row.

The starting point

The third word seems the dullest. Put two cabins on the same Ferris wheel, a quarter of a turn apart: they draw exactly the same wave, but one runs ahead of the other. That offset, the point in the turn where each cabin starts, is called the phase, and it is measured in degrees (one full turn is 360°) or in fractions of a turn.

Size, speed, and starting point: with amplitude, frequency, and phase you can describe any sinusoid. In the challenge, an orange cabin draws a wave with three values you do not know, and you have to find them by moving your own Ferris wheel.

Adjust the size, the speed, and the starting point of your Ferris wheel until the green wave covers the dotted orange one.

When phase decides

With a single wave, phase hardly matters, because it only says where the wave starts. As soon as two waves are added, however, it decides whether they reinforce or cancel each other. If the two cabins go together, their heights add up and the wave doubles; if one is at the top just when the other is at the bottom, they compensate each other at every instant.

Noise-canceling headphones rely on this: a microphone listens to the noise outside and the headphone produces an equal wave with its starting point shifted, so that the two compensate each other inside your ear. Adding waves is the subject of lesson 3; here it is enough to see what phase does.

Drag the orange cabin around the Ferris wheel until the sum of the two waves disappears. With “Hear the sum” you can listen to it while you move it.

What vibrates

A tuning fork does not spin and yet it draws the same curve, for a deeper reason. If you pull a prong of the fork away from its position and let go, the metal itself pulls it back, and the farther you pull it, the harder it pulls. This is the rule of a spring: a force back toward rest, proportional to the displacement.

Everything that follows that rule moves the same way, with a motion physicists call simple harmonic motion, and that motion is exactly the shadow of a Ferris wheel. The prongs of a tuning fork, the air vibrating inside your mouth when you whistle, or a swing with a small push follow it, at least approximately. A freshly struck tuning fork also makes a sharp “clang” that dies out quickly and leaves only the pure note.

How fast a spring vibrates depends on two things: how hard it is, its stiffness, and how heavy the mass hanging from it is. A stiffer spring pulls the mass back with more force and vibrates faster, while a heavier mass is harder to move and vibrates more slowly.

Adjust the spring stiffness and the mass until the orange mass goes up and down in step with the green cabin. Every time you touch a control, the mass starts again from the height of the cabin.

In your voice

What to record. A sustained whistle of a few seconds and then a long “aah” on the same note: these are segments 2 and 3 of the series’ reference recording. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The oscilloscope shows a few milliseconds of the signal, and in that window your whistle should look almost like a perfect Ferris wheel. A study that recorded twenty people whistling found that a single frequency dominates in a whistle, almost always between 500 and 5,000 Hz. The “aah” repeats at the same pace, but with humps and peaks of its own that a single Ferris wheel cannot draw. Tap two consecutive crests to measure how long one vibration lasts: the frequency and the note come from that, and the lab tells you how much your signal resembles a single Ferris wheel.

What to remember

  1. A signal is something that changes and can be measured. For a machine, it is a list of numbers in order.
  2. The sinusoid is the shadow of something that spins, and it is described with three words: amplitude, the size; frequency, the turns per second; and phase, the starting point.
  3. It appears everywhere because it is the shadow of what spins and the natural motion of what vibrates. Almost nothing is a single Ferris wheel, but almost everything can be built by putting Ferris wheels together.

Common mistakes

  • “More frequency means more volume.” Frequency is the note, and volume depends on the amplitude. They are independent: a small, fast Ferris wheel gives a high, quiet note.
  • “Phase doesn’t matter.” With a single wave, almost. As soon as two are added, phase decides whether they reinforce or cancel each other, and noise-canceling headphones live off that.
  • “The sinusoid is a mathematical invention.” It is what a spring, a tuning fork prong, or the voltage of a generator does. Mathematics describes it, and the curve was already in the spring before anyone wrote it down.
  • “The computer stores the curve.” It stores separate numbers, one every so often, and it does not look between one and the next. Whoever draws the curve reconstructs it.
  • “Every wave is a sinusoid.” The title of this lesson is a slogan. The simplest wave is a Ferris wheel, and most waves, like your “aah,” are sums of Ferris wheels.

Math corner

The formulas, for anyone who wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The cabin

If the Ferris wheel turns with constant angular velocity \omega and the cabin starts at angle \varphi, its angle at time t is \theta = \omega t + \varphi. Seen from the front, the cabin has two shadows, one on the ground and one on the wall, and the one on the wall is its height, the wave of this lesson:

x(t) = A\,\cos(\omega t + \varphi), \qquad y(t) = A\,\sin(\omega t + \varphi)

Frequency and period

Angular velocity counts radians per second, and frequency counts turns per second. One turn is 2\pi radians, and the period is how long a turn lasts:

\omega = 2\pi f, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}

Two cabins

Two cabins on the same Ferris wheel separated by an angle \delta add their heights into another sinusoid of the same frequency, whose size depends on the separation. With half a turn, \delta = \pi, the cosine is zero and the sum disappears:

A\,\sin\,\theta + A\,\sin(\theta + \delta) = 2A\,\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\delta}{2}\right)\,\sin\!\left(\theta + \tfrac{\delta}{2}\right)

The spring

“The restoring force is proportional to the displacement and points toward rest” is written with Hooke’s law and Newton’s second law:

m\,\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -k\,x

Its solutions are precisely the shadows of a Ferris wheel turning with angular velocity \omega = \sqrt{k/m}. That is why, in the spring challenge, the only thing that counts is the ratio between stiffness and mass:

x(t) = A\,\cos\!\left(\sqrt{k/m}\;t + \varphi\right), \qquad f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}

The generator

A synchronous machine with P poles turning at N turns per minute produces a voltage of frequency

f = \frac{P\,N}{120}

With P = 2 and N = 3{,}000 you get f = 50 Hz.

A preview

The two shadows together form a complex number, and the whole Ferris wheel fits in a single expression, which reads “an arrow of length A that spins”:

A\,e^{i(\omega t + \varphi)} = A\,\cos(\omega t + \varphi) + i\,A\,\sin(\omega t + \varphi)
