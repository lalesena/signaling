Detour: wavelets for compressing and cleaning

In the early 1990s, the FBI had more than a hundred million fingerprint cards on paper and wanted to move them to digital, but every scanned card took up about 10 megabytes. The idea that made it possible to shrink them about fifteen times also works for removing the hiss from a recording, and both come from splitting a signal into a blurred version and the details it is missing. How do you split a signal without losing anything, and why can you then throw away almost everything that comes out and keep something almost identical?

  • About 45 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 13 a wavelet stretched and slid along to measure the resemblance to the signal at each scale, and hundreds of thousands of measurements came out, many of them almost repeated. This lesson asks the opposite question: what is the minimum number of values you need to store a whole signal. The answer is the pyramid in the picture, in which each level splits the signal into a blurred version, seen with less detail and with half the samples, and the details, which are what you have to add back to recover the sharpness. This whole split, with as many numbers as the signal had samples, is called the discrete wavelet transform.

This lesson is a detour from the main route of the course, which continues in lesson 15. It lets you look at the wavelets of lesson 13 from another angle and understand two very common uses of them: storing a signal in less space and removing its noise.

Step by step

Two numbers in place of two

Imagine a row of neighboring samples and take them two at a time. From each pair you can store their average, which is a blurred version of the two, and the half-difference, which is half of how far apart they are. That half-difference is the detail: what you have to add to the average to recover the exact pair.

With the average and the detail you recover both samples: the first is the average plus the detail and the second is the average minus the detail. Nothing is lost, because two numbers have been swapped for two others. This split into averages and half-differences is the Haar wavelet, the simplest of all, a step up followed by a step down. The Hungarian mathematician Alfréd Haar described it in his 1909 thesis, in Göttingen, long before the word wavelet existed.

These eight samples are grouped in pairs. Drag each orange bar to the average of its pair; the ink crosses tell you whether, with the detail that comes out, they return to the real samples.

You can also use the keyboard: the left and right arrows choose the bar, and the up and down arrows raise or lower it in half steps.

The pyramid

What you did with the samples can be repeated with the averages: the blurred version is also grouped two by two, and from each pair come another average and another detail. In this way, level by level, the blurred version shrinks to half and the details keep what was lost on each climb. This way of looking at the same signal from farther and farther away is called multiresolution, and its levels resemble the octaves of the piano, because each one sees the signal with half the detail of the one below.

In 1989, Stéphane Mallat showed that the wavelet transform can be computed exactly this way, as a pyramid. A low-pass filter, like those in lesson 6, gives the blurred version; a high-pass gives the detail; and from each one you keep every other sample. That pair of filters is called a filter bank, and it is like an equalizer split into two halves. Each detail is the wavelet from lesson 13 at one scale, twice as wide as the one on the level below.

The most surprising thing about the pyramid is its bookkeeping. Each level has half the samples of the previous one, but it also stores its details, so the total number of values doesn’t grow: it is the same as in the signal.

This signal has 512 samples. Climb the pyramid until the blurred version at the top level has only 16 samples.

Keeping only the big ones

The pyramid on its own doesn’t compress anything: it gives as many numbers as samples. Compression comes afterward, and it relies on something that happens in almost every signal: where the signal is smooth, the average of each pair explains almost everything and the detail is almost zero. There are big details only at edges, hits and sudden changes. If you keep the few big ones and throw away the small ones, when you undo the pyramid you recover almost the same signal with far fewer numbers, and this is called lossy compression: keeping what matters and accepting a small error in exchange.

A step is the ideal case. For Fourier’s Ferris wheels, a sudden jump is a hard time: you need very many of them and, even so, the fringes from lesson 3 remain. For the Haar pyramid, on the other hand, a jump leaves a single big detail on each level, and the rest are zeros. In the challenge you can compare the two approaches with the same signal, made of steps.

Keep at most 6% of the numbers and manage to get the signal back with a resemblance of more than 30 dB. Compare the Haar pyramid with Fourier’s Ferris wheels.

Each signal with its own wavelet

The fact that Haar stores steps so well doesn’t mean it stores everything well. A wavelet is a template, and transforming is measuring resemblance, as in lesson 4: each wavelet stores well what looks like it. Haar’s is a step and it stores steps. With a smooth curve, on the other hand, Haar’s steps are a poor match, and you need many details to draw it.

In the late 1980s, the mathematician Ingrid Daubechies found short, smooth wavelets with which the pyramid can be undone without error, and the Daubechies wavelet used here has eight coefficients. The ones used by the image standards you will see in the next step are cousins of hers, symmetric ones.

There are two signals. For each one, choose the wavelet that stores it best when only 5% of the numbers are kept.

From the FBI’s fingerprints to your photos

The story of the fingerprints is that of these steps with a big number behind it. In the early 1990s, the FBI kept on paper more than a hundred million cards with inked fingerprints. Scanned at 500 dots per inch, each card took up about 10 megabytes, and the whole archive would have taken on the order of 2,000 terabytes, at a time when a floppy disk held a megabyte and a half.

Today’s photos are compressed with JPEG, a 1992 standard that cuts the image into blocks of 8 × 8 pixels and summarizes each one with cosines. If you squeeze hard, each block ends up with little more than its average tone and tiles appear: the seams between blocks that don’t match. In a fingerprint scanned at 500 dots per inch, the ridges are spaced almost exactly as far apart as those blocks are wide, and the tiles fooled the programs that look for minutiae, the endings and bifurcations of the ridges. In the FBI’s tests, tiles were unavoidable above about 10 to 1.

The solution was a standard of their own, WSQ, which breaks each fingerprint down into a pyramid of wavelets, in two dimensions, by rows and then by columns, and reduces it about 15 times. In a blind test with fingerprint examiners, they noticed losses at that compression in only 7 of 202 comparisons, and at a compression of 20 to 1 they noticed them in 159 of 196, so 15 to 1 was recommended. When you squeeze, the image loses fine detail little by little, without seams, and that is what the FBI valued. A similar family of wavelets is that of JPEG 2000, which didn’t replace JPEG on the web, but is the format of digital cinema copies.

There are no real fingerprints or real codecs here: the challenge uses a synthetic fingerprint, drawn with a formula, and a simplified imitation of each method that keeps the same number of coefficients, the largest ones. On one side, cosines in 8 × 8 blocks; on the other, a pyramid of smooth wavelets. Haar’s, note, also makes blocks in images, so the comparison is made with Daubechies’s.

Squeeze the compression until at least 1 in every 20 numbers is stored, and tap the image that fills up with tiles.

Cleaning: the threshold

Removing noise is the same operation with a different purpose. White noise, the kind from lesson 10, spreads its energy equally and leaves a little in every detail, while a signal with jumps concentrates it in a few big details. There is usually a gap between the two, and if you draw a horizontal line there, at a certain height above the details, you can erase everything that falls below it and undo the pyramid. That line is the threshold. It can be applied in two ways: hard thresholding erases what doesn’t reach it and leaves everything else untouched, and soft thresholding also pulls what it keeps closer to zero.

In 1994, the statisticians David Donoho and Iain Johnstone proposed a rule that is surprising for being so simple: compute the pyramid, erase the details that don’t exceed a certain height, and undo the pyramid. The height depends on how much noise there is, and that is estimated from the finest details themselves, which almost only contain noise. The height they proposed is called the universal threshold, and it grows very slowly with the length of the signal: for one second of audio at 16,000 samples per second, it is about 4.4 times the typical noise level.

The threshold and the low-pass filter from lesson 6 clean in different ways. The low-pass decides by frequency, and takes away everything high, including the sharp edge of a jump. The threshold decides by size, and removes what is small at all frequencies, so it respects edges and hits. In the challenge you will compare the two with the same signal.

This step signal is full of noise. Move the threshold until the wavelet cleaning beats the best possible low-pass by 3 dB.

The threshold has limits that are worth hearing, not just measuring. The universal one is cautious and also erases what is weak in the signal: in a voice, the “s” and the faint harmonics resemble noise in size, and they go away with it, so the voice ends up muffled, as if underwater. If you lower the threshold, more voice remains and more noise too. And a 50 Hz hum doesn’t go away with a threshold, because it is big and constant, and the threshold takes it for signal: that is what the band-stop filter in lesson 6 is for. Your own voice, in the lab below, will show you.

In your voice

What to record. A spoken sentence of about 10 seconds: it is segment 5 of the series’ reference recording. If there is background noise in your room, a fan or the street, so much the better, because it is exactly what you want to remove; if not, the lab can add synthetic noise. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The lab computes the pyramid of your recording, estimates the noise level from the finest details, erases the ones that don’t reach the threshold and compares the result with a low-pass filter. Listen to the three versions and look at their spectrograms. With the universal threshold, the silences end up almost mute, but the voice sounds muffled and the “s” gets weaker or disappears; with a lower threshold, more voice remains and a bit more noise. The low-pass always eats the “s” and barely cleans the silences, because white noise also occupies the lows. Many phones already remove noise when recording, and then there is little to clean: add noise to see it.

What to remember

  1. The discrete wavelet transform splits a signal into a blurred version and details at scales that keep doubling, in a pyramid. It gives as many numbers as samples and, when undone, returns the signal without error.
  2. Compressing means throwing away the small details and keeping the big ones. Each wavelet stores well what looks like it, and in images a pyramid of smooth wavelets blurs where JPEG’s blocks make tiles.
  3. Cleaning is the same operation: noise leaves small details and the signal leaves few, big ones, so a threshold separates them by size and not by frequency. That is why it respects jumps, but it eats what is weak, like the “s,” and doesn’t remove a hum.

Common mistakes

  • “Compressing with wavelets means removing the highs.” You remove the small details, whether low or high. An edge, which is high, is kept because its detail is big. Removing the highs is what a low-pass filter does.
  • “The wavelet transform already compresses, because it gives fewer numbers.” It gives exactly as many numbers as samples, and from them you recover the signal without error. Compression comes afterward, when almost all the details, which are small, are thrown away or stored with few bits.
  • “The threshold only removes noise.” Noise and the weak part of the signal look alike, because both give small details. The universal threshold also eats the “s” and the faint harmonics, and the voice ends up muffled. If you lower the threshold, more voice remains and more noise.
  • “The threshold also removes the wall outlet hum.” A 50 Hz hum is big and constant, so its coefficients are big and the threshold takes them for signal. That is what the band-stop filter in lesson 6 is for.
  • “Any wavelet will do, and the more levels the better.” Each wavelet stores well what looks like it: Haar, steps, and Daubechies’s, smooth curves. And each level splits the blurred version in two, so when it already has very few samples, adding levels gains nothing and the edges of the signal start to get in the way.

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

One level with the Haar wavelet

In the version that preserves energy, the blurred version of each pair is its sum divided by root two, and the detail, its difference divided by root two. With the average and the half-difference from the lesson, only the scale changes:

a_k = \frac{x_{2k} + x_{2k+1}}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad d_k = \frac{x_{2k} - x_{2k+1}}{\sqrt{2}}

To go back, you add and subtract:

x_{2k} = \frac{a_k + d_k}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad x_{2k+1} = \frac{a_k - d_k}{\sqrt{2}}

One level in general

One level of the pyramid means filtering with a low-pass h and a high-pass g, and keeping every other sample (Mallat, 1989):

a_{j+1}[k] = \sum_n h[n - 2k]\, a_j[n], \qquad d_{j+1}[k] = \sum_n g[n - 2k]\, a_j[n]

In the orthogonal case, the high-pass is obtained from the low-pass by flipping it and alternating the signs; the sign and the shift change from book to book:

g[n] = (-1)^n\, h[1 - n]

The scales

All the wavelets in the pyramid are the same one, stretched by powers of two and shifted:

\psi_{j,k}(t) = 2^{j/2}\, \psi\!\left(2^{j} t - k\right)

And detail level j occupies, roughly, the octave between f_s/2^{j+1} and f_s/2^{j}:

d_j \;\leftrightarrow\; \left[\frac{f_s}{2^{j+1}},\; \frac{f_s}{2^{j}}\right]

What is lost when you throw away coefficients

With an orthonormal pyramid, the energy of the signal is the sum of the energies of all its coefficients, and that is why the error from throwing away coefficients is the energy of the ones you discard (Parseval):

\sum_n x[n]^2 = \sum_k a_L[k]^2 + \sum_{j=1}^{L} \sum_k d_j[k]^2

The resemblance of a reconstruction is measured in decibels, like the signal-to-noise ratio in lesson 10:

\mathrm{SNR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\sum_n x[n]^2}{\sum_n \left(x[n] - \hat{x}[n]\right)^2} \ \text{dB}

The threshold

The hard threshold keeps or erases; the soft one also pulls everything it keeps closer to zero:

\eta_{\text{hard}}(d) = d \cdot \mathbf{1}\{|d| > \lambda\}, \qquad \eta_{\text{soft}}(d) = \operatorname{sgn}(d)\, \max(|d| - \lambda,\, 0)

The universal threshold is the noise level times the square root of two times the logarithm of the length, and the noise level is estimated with the median of the finest details, divided by 0.6745, which is the median of the absolute value of Gaussian noise with standard deviation one (Donoho and Johnstone, 1994):

\lambda = \hat\sigma\,\sqrt{2\ln N}, \qquad \hat\sigma = \frac{\operatorname{median}\left(|d_1|\right)}{0.6745}

The reason for that height is that the largest of N independent Gaussian noise values barely exceeds \sigma times the square root of two times the logarithm of N:

\Pr\!\left(\max_{i \le N} |z_i| > \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}\right) \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } N \to \infty
