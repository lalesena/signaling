Following the ridge: from the image to the pitch curve

Synchrosqueezing leaves a sharp image, with one thin line for each voice or whistle, but a tuner does not want an image: it wants a number at every instant. When you ask a program for that number, it sometimes answers that a low voice is singing an octave higher or, the other way around, an octave lower, even though to you it sounds perfectly clear. How can a machine get it wrong like that, and what is done to keep it from happening?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 19, synchrosqueezing sent each little piece of energy to the row of its real speed, and the wide bands of the spectrogram turned into thin lines. That image already contains the note at each instant, but to get it out you have to travel along it, and doing that well is like walking at night along the ridge of a mountain: the sensible thing is to follow the highest ground, and at the same time not to make impossible leaps toward the peak across the way.

Move the jump penalty and watch what happens to the walker. With no penalty, any bright burst tempts the walker and off it goes; with a moderate penalty, the walker follows the whistle and jumps over the silence; and with a huge one, the walker stays at its height and stops following the melody. The whole lesson is about finding and understanding that middle ground.

Step by step

From the image to the curve

In a spectrogram like the one in lesson 11, time goes to the right, frequency goes up and color says how much energy there is. If you look at the map as a relief seen from above, with color as height, each component of the sound is an elongated mountain range, and the ridge is the line that joins its highest points.

That line has a concrete meaning. When a Ferris wheel changes speed slowly, as in lesson 15, its energy concentrates along a curve that follows that speed, and the classic work of Delprat and coauthors (1992) shows it for wavelets and for Gaussian windows. That is why the ridge is a good estimate of the instantaneous frequency, and traveling along it from one end to the other gives a curve with one value per instant: the pitch curve. That trip, from image to curve, is called ridge extraction.

In a whistle, which is almost a single Ferris wheel, the ridge is clean: a bright band that rises and falls. In the challenge you trace it yourself, by hand, and then you listen to your path as a pure tone to check that it sounds almost the same as the whistle.

Draw the ridge of the whistle: drag across the map from left to right, following the brightest band, from the left edge to the right edge.

The shortcut: always climb to the highest point

The simplest thing is to keep, at each instant, the brightest point of the map’s column. It is a greedy method: it remembers nothing of what it did before and only looks at what is in front of it. With a clean whistle it works, because the band is the brightest thing of all.

It fails as soon as something stronger than the whistle shows up elsewhere. A snap, a door slamming or a consonant draws a very bright blob that lasts a few hundredths of a second, and the greedy method jumps to it and comes back afterward. In a silence, it does something worse: it keeps choosing the brightest point of the column, which is now pure background noise, and invents a note that nobody played.

In the challenge, the orange curve is the one the greedy method produces, and you have to find the places where it departs from the whistle. With “Hear the greedy curve” you can hear how it sounds at each one.

The orange curve is the one the greedy method produces. Tap the map at the three places where it leaves the whistle; if you tap a mark again, it is removed.

Penalizing jumps

The solution is to score the whole path instead of each instant separately. A path earns points for the energy of the cells it passes through and loses points every time it jumps from one row to another, the more the bigger the jump: that is the jump penalty. A bright burst is only worth it if what you gain from it is more than it costs to go there and back.

That is how Carmona, Hwang and Torrésani (1997) framed it for the ridges of the wavelet transform: the best curve is the one that passes through a lot of energy without changing height abruptly or bending too much. With zero penalty the greedy method returns, and with a huge penalty the walker no longer leaves its row and does not follow the melody. Between the two extremes there is a range that works, and finding it is the challenge.

The signal in the challenge is a synthetic voice singing A, C, E and A. As in many voices, the second harmonic is almost as strong as the fundamental, and in two stretches the fundamental almost disappears, so the brightest ridge is an octave above the note. To follow the note you have to trust what happens before and after, not just the brightest thing at that instant.

Adjust the jump penalty so that the green curve follows the melody without climbing to the octave above and without arriving late at the new notes.

You do not need to try every path

There are a huge number of possible paths: with five heights and six instants there are already 15,625, and on a real map the number explodes. Trying them all is impossible, but you do not need to. For each cell it is enough to remember the best path that reaches it and where it came from; on reaching the last column you choose the best cell and go back following those bread crumbs. This way of searching is dynamic programming, and the number of calculations grows with the length of the signal, not explosively.

With the jump penalty, the version used for pitch is called the Viterbi algorithm. Andrew Viterbi published it in 1967 to decode messages that arrive with noise, and according to Forney’s (2005) historical review, by then it was already running in about a billion mobile phones. Following a ridge is the same problem: choosing, among a huge number of paths, the one that best explains what you see.

Pitch detectors use it for this reason. The one in Praat, a free voice analysis program, notes several pitch candidates per instant, including one that says “there is no voice here,” and looks for the cheapest path: every octave jump costs, and so does every time the voice turns on or off. The author of that method, Paul Boersma (1993), found that on a steady sound this step eliminates octave errors even when they affect 40% of the instants. Later, pYIN (Mauch and Dixon, 2014) combined the candidate proposal of YIN, which you will see two steps further on, with Viterbi decoding.

Choose one cell in each column to get the highest possible score: you add up the energy of the cells you pass through and pay a penalty for every row you jump.

Two lines, crossings and silences

A map can have more than one ridge, for example two people whistling. To get several, you extract the best one, erase a band around it and repeat. Carmona, Hwang and Torrésani (1999) went further with a method that releases many chains of “crazy climbers” onto the map at once and links their traces while allowing gaps, and they tested it on speech.

Silences call for something else. A tracker that always has to give a note invents one where there is none, so one more option is needed, “there is no note here,” with a cost for entering and leaving it. Praat calls that distinction voiced and unvoiced: voiced is an instant with a voice that has pitch, and unvoiced is a silence, a whisper or an “s.”

Crossings are the hard case. When two whistles cross, forming an X, penalizing only the jumps is not enough, because bouncing off and going straight cost the same, and the path goes off with the brightest branch. You also have to penalize a change of slope, like the curvature term of Carmona and colleagues: that is the inertia, the walker who passes through the saddle of the X without turning. Even with inertia, the energy map does not keep track of who is who: if the two whistlers really did bounce, the image would be almost the same.

From the ridge to the note: the octave error

The pitch of a voice is its fundamental, the slowest Ferris wheel of the stack of epicycles from lesson 3; the others turn two, three, four times faster and are its harmonics. But the brightest ridge is not always the fundamental. In some vowels almost all the energy is in one harmonic, and over a classic telephone, which barely lets anything through below 300 Hz, the fundamental of an adult voice, which usually lies between 90 and 255 Hz, disappears. The ear still hears the right note because it recognizes the family of harmonics, and that is called the missing fundamental. A detector that only looks at the highest peak, on the other hand, will say an octave or more above: that is the octave error.

Detectors that compare the signal with a shifted copy of itself, like the autocorrelation of lesson 9, have the opposite problem. A signal that repeats every period also repeats every two and every three, and if the match at two periods wins, the note comes out an octave lower: the subharmonic error. De Cheveigné and Kawahara (2002) warn that “octave error” is a misnomer, because the ratio between the note that comes out and the true one does not have to be two.

Auto-Tune

Harold Hildebrand’s patent (filed in 1998) measures the period of the voice with formulas derived from autocorrelation and acknowledges this problem: some vowels have very little energy at the fundamental, and the method detects the harmonic, so it also checks the double lag. Then it moves the voice to the nearest note of the scale. If that correction is instantaneous, the voice jumps from note to note without sliding, the robot effect made famous by Cher’s “Believe” (1998).

YIN is a detector that takes the definition of period literally, the smallest shift that leaves the signal unchanged. It measures how much the signal changes when it is shifted, normalizes that measure so that small shifts do not look like periods, and keeps the first valley that drops below an absolute threshold, not the deepest one. In the original paper, with a speech database, the gross errors (estimates that depart more than 20% from the reference) go down step by step from 10.0% for autocorrelation to 0.50% for the complete method.

A karaoke game from the inside

The free karaoke game UltraStar Deluxe, inspired by SingStar, does not understand the lyrics: it compares pitch. According to its code, which you can read, it tries 49 notes, from C2 to C6, measures which one fits best using a difference function and, before scoring, moves your note to the octave of the song. Then it allows 2, 1 or 0 semitones of margin depending on the difficulty. That is why you can sing a song meant for another voice an octave lower without losing points.

In the challenge, the detector analyzes a synthetic “a” vowel singing eight low notes. At the top you see the difference curve of one instant, with its valleys, and at the bottom the pitch curve of the whole melody, with the gross errors in red.

Choose the rule and the threshold so that the detector gets the note right at almost every instant: fewer than 5% gross errors.

In your voice

What to record. A short melody, sung or whistled, of about ten seconds, which is segment 7 of the series’ reference recording. If there is someone to do it with, also record segment 8: two people whistling at the same time, one rising and one falling. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The lab decides whether it is a voice or a whistle, draws the map with the notes labeled and plots the pitch curve on top. Change the method and notice where the highest peak goes wrong and how the path fixes it. With “Send through a phone,” frequencies below 300 Hz and above 3,400 Hz are removed: in a low voice, the octave error of the first method will appear. The orange curve is your melody moved to the nearest note, as a karaoke game does; with the correction speed at zero, the voice jumps from note to note. With the two-whistles example you can follow two lines at once, with and without inertia, and hear each one.

With a cracked voice or a lot of reverberation, or if the recording is clipped, detectors fail more; with consonants, whispers and breaths, a good curve should fall silent. If the audio has nothing to follow, the lab says so instead of inventing a curve.

What to remember

  1. The ridge is the line of peaks of a component on the time-frequency map, and following it turns the image into a curve with one value per instant. Keeping the brightest thing at each instant is not enough: a burst, a silence or a strong harmonic throws it off.
  2. A good tracker scores the whole path, with a penalty for jumping, another for changing slope and the option of staying silent. Dynamic programming finds it without trying every path, by keeping for each cell the best path that reaches it.
  3. A machine gets the octave wrong because the signal allows it: what repeats every period repeats every two, and sometimes the fundamental is not there. That is why detectors use rules like the first valley below a threshold, and the context of the whole path.

Common mistakes

  • “The note at each instant is the brightest point of the map.” It fails with noise, with silences and, above all, with voices whose second or third harmonic is stronger than the fundamental, like vowels with little energy at the bottom or a voice over the phone. You have to look at the whole path and, for pitch, at the family of harmonics.
  • “The more you penalize jumps, the better.” A huge penalty leaves the walker standing still: it does not follow note changes, it flattens vibrato and it can stay entirely on the wrong harmonic. The penalty is a balance that is adjusted to the type of signal.
  • “Octave error is always double or half.” The detector can pick three periods, an octave and a fifth below, or the third harmonic, an octave and a fifth above. There are also upward errors caused by a strong resonance of the mouth.
  • “After a crossing, the brightest line is still the same voice.” The energy map carries no labels: bouncing and crossing draw almost the same X. You need inertia, penalizing slope changes, or extra clues such as the timbre or the loudness of each source.
  • “Dynamic programming tries every path.” It only keeps, for each point, the best path that reaches it. In the grid of challenge 4 there are 15,625 paths and about 130 calculations are enough.

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The shortcut

The greedy method picks, at each instant, the row with the most energy:

\hat k(t) = \arg\max_{k}\, E(t,k)

The cost of Carmona, Hwang and Torrésani

A curve is good if it passes through a lot of energy, changes height little and bends little. In continuous time, with T the transform and \varphi(t) the curve, you look for the minimum of

\mathcal{E}(\varphi) = -\int \bigl|T\bigl(t,\varphi(t)\bigr)\bigr|^2\,dt + \int \Bigl(\lambda\,\varphi'(t)^2 + \mu\,\varphi''(t)^2\Bigr)\,dt

and on a grid, with c_t the row chosen at each instant, you maximize its discrete version, where \lambda is the jump penalty and \mu the penalty for changing slope, the inertia:

J(c) = \sum_t E(t,c_t) - \lambda \sum_t (c_t - c_{t-1})^2 - \mu \sum_t (c_t - 2c_{t-1} + c_{t-2})^2

Dynamic programming

The best score that can arrive at row k at instant t is its energy plus the best that could be brought from the previous instant, paying for the jump. You also keep which row it came from, to go back at the end:

D(t,k) = E(t,k) + \max_{|j-k|\le J}\Bigl[D(t-1,j) - \lambda\,(k-j)^2\Bigr]

The work grows with the number of instants, of rows and of allowed jumps, and not with all the possible paths, which are n_F to the power n_T:

\text{calculations} \approx n_T\, n_F\,(2J+1) \qquad\text{versus}\qquad n_F^{\,n_T}\ \text{paths}

The cost of Praat

The penalties of each transition are added up and the strength of the chosen candidates is subtracted. A jump between two notes costs in proportion to the octaves it spans, and turning the voice on or off has its own cost (Boersma, 1993):

\text{cost} = \sum_{n=2}^{N} \mathrm{tr}(F_{n-1},F_n) - \sum_{n=1}^{N} R_n, \qquad \mathrm{tr}(F_1,F_2) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{both unvoiced}\\ C_{\text{v/u}} & \text{one unvoiced}\\ C_{\text{oct}}\,\bigl|\log_2 (F_1/F_2)\bigr| & \text{both voiced}\end{cases}

The YIN difference function

It measures how much the signal changes when it is compared with itself shifted by \tau samples, in a window of W samples. The second form says it is autocorrelation seen upside down: where autocorrelation has a peak, the difference has a valley.

d_t(\tau) = \sum_{j=t+1}^{t+W} \bigl(x_j - x_{j+\tau}\bigr)^2 = r_t(0) + r_{t+\tau}(0) - 2\,r_t(\tau)

The cumulative normalization

Each value is divided by the mean of the previous ones, so that very small shifts do not look like periods. The period \tau^\ast is the first local minimum with d' below the threshold, which is 0.1 in the paper, or, if there is none, the global minimum:

d'_t(\tau) = \begin{cases} 1 & \tau = 0\\[2pt] \dfrac{d_t(\tau)}{\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau} d_t(j)} & \tau > 0\end{cases} \qquad f_0 = \frac{f_s}{\tau^\ast}

Semitones and cents

Musical distance depends on the ratio of frequencies, not on their difference. An octave is 12 semitones or 1,200 cents, and the 20% error that YIN uses to define a gross error is about 316 cents, a little over three semitones:

n_{\text{semitones}} = 12\log_2\frac{f_2}{f_1}, \qquad \text{cents} = 1200\log_2\frac{f_2}{f_1}, \qquad 1200\log_2 1.2 \approx 316

The karaoke notes

For each candidate note, the mean difference between the signal and its copy shifted by one period is measured, wrapping around at the end of the excerpt, and the one with the least difference is chosen. The 49 candidate notes of UltraStar Deluxe are:

D_C(\tau) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\bigl|x\bigl((n+\tau)\bmod N\bigr) - x(n)\bigr|, \qquad f_i = 440\cdot 2^{(i-33)/12},\ i = 0,\dots,48
