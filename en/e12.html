Level 4 · Lesson 12

The uncertainty principle isn’t just quantum

The tuning app takes a moment to decide which note you are whistling, and it isn’t that your phone is slow. If you give it a very brief note, it doesn’t get it right, and if you give it a finger snap, it doesn’t even try, because a snap has no note. Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle sounds like particle physics, but the tuner, the equalizer on your stereo and a bird’s trill run into the same limit every day. What is it, and why is there no window that gets around it?

  • About 45 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 11 the spectrogram was built by sliding a window along the sound and stacking one column of color for each chunk, and the duration of that window was a decision. Each cell of that map is a tile: a rectangle of the time–frequency plane, as wide as the window lasts and as tall as the frequency spacing that window can tell apart, which according to the rule from lesson 5 is roughly one divided by its duration.

A tile can be stretched wide by flattening it, or the other way around, but there is no way to shrink it in both directions at once. With a short window you separate two nearby events in time well, the time resolution, and two nearby notes poorly, the frequency resolution, and with a long window the opposite happens. That choice between seeing when well or seeing which one well is the time–frequency trade-off. Move the control and check that, while the tile changes shape, its area stays the same.

Step by step

A snap and a note that never ends

Snap your fingers. The sound happens at a very precise instant, but it has no note: its energy is spread over almost all frequencies at once, and that is why it sounds like a dry “tick” with no pitch. Now imagine the opposite, a note that sounded forever. It would have an exact frequency and no instant, because it wouldn’t start or end at any moment.

In 1946, the engineer Dennis Gabor realized that describing a sound only in time, or only in frequencies, were two exaggerations: the first forgets the notes, and the second, with the eternal waves of Fourier from lesson 4, forgets the instants. What we hear is both at once, so he drew a plane with time and frequency, and showed that no device can divide it into cells smaller than a minimum.

An inventor with two ideas

The same Gabor who drew the tiles invented holography the following year, in 1947, while working as an engineer at the British company Thomson-Houston, and he received the Nobel Prize in Physics for it in 1971.

Every real sound lives between those two extremes and occupies a tile with whatever shape fits it. That of a snap is tall and narrow, because you know well when it happens and poorly which note it has, and that of a long note is low and wide, because you know well which note it is and poorly exactly when it began. In the challenge, each of the six sounds has to go to its own.

Listen to each sound and decide which tile suits it: the tall, narrow one, which keeps the instant, or the low, wide one, which keeps the note. Manage to place all six.

The tile that can’t be shrunk

To see the limit with numbers you need a simple sound: a pure note, a single Ferris wheel from lesson 1, trimmed by a bell curve, so that it starts softly, grows to the center and fades out just as softly. That elementary signal is the Gabor atom, and the width of the bell decides how long the note lasts. The shorter it is, the wider its spectrum spreads, and the longer, the narrower.

To say how much, you have to decide how to measure the width of something that fades away gradually. The usual way is the standard deviation of its energy, the same idea that measures how spread out exam grades are around the average: here it says how spread out the energy is around its central instant and its central frequency. With that measure, the product of the two widths never drops below a specific value, a little less than a tenth, about 0.08 turns (the exact equation is in the math corner). A bell 10 milliseconds wide, for example, takes up at least about 8 hertz in frequency, and that minimum is called the Gabor limit.

The exact number depends on how the width is measured: with the “effective duration” that Gabor himself used, for example, the minimum is one half. What doesn’t depend on anything is that a minimum exists, and that only one shape reaches it exactly: the Ferris wheel trimmed by a Gaussian bell curve.

It is the same inequality that appears in quantum mechanics, and hence the name uncertainty principle. There, the position of a particle and its momentum are linked like the time and frequency of a sound, because one is the Fourier transform of the other. Gabor himself wrote that his results could have been derived from quantum mechanics by replacing position with time, momentum with frequency and Planck’s constant with one, and added that this is where the paths part ways. In a sound there is nothing mysterious and nobody disturbing it when you measure: it is a property of any wave, the same one that keeps a trimmed Ferris wheel from having a single speed.

Squeeze the note until it lasts as long as a snap, 2 milliseconds wide or less, and see how tall its tile gets. Then stretch it until the tile is less than 10 hertz tall. Drag the right edge of the tile or use the control; ▶ lets you hear the note.

The bell and the other windows

So far the window has been a Gaussian bell curve, but the spectrogram in lesson 11 used others: Hann, Hamming, rectangular. Each one leaves a tile of a different size, and only the bell, the Gaussian window, reaches exactly the minimum. The reason is that its spectrum is another bell just as smooth, with no edges or jumps for energy to escape to distant frequencies. Any abrupt edge spreads energy far from the peak, that leakage from lesson 5, and enlarges the tile.

“The best” here means the one with the smallest tile, and it isn’t good for everything. To measure the height of a peak well, or to keep the leakage far from it very low, other windows are used, and that is why Hann is the everyday choice.

Try the five windows and find one whose tile stays less than 0.2% above the minimum. The reading below says how many times the minimum the tile of the window you have selected measures.

The tuner

With this you can see why your tuner takes a while. You play two nearly identical strings, one at 440 Hz and one at 442 Hz, and you want the app to tell them apart. As we saw in lesson 5, two notes a few hertz apart blend into a beat, a slow pulse that goes up and down, and they can’t be separated until the window has listened long enough to see a couple of those pulses. With a Hann window, to see a 3 dB gap between the two peaks you need to listen for at least twice one divided by the difference between the notes, which is close to a second for 440 and 442 Hz.

It isn’t that the phone is slow: the more alike two notes are, the longer it takes for them to drift out of phase and for the difference to show. It is the law of the tile seen from the notes, because for the tile to be narrow in frequency it has to be long in time.

The equalizer from lesson 6 runs into the same thing. A very narrow band, a few hertz wide, is slow to react and, when the sound cuts off, keeps ringing a little. Gabor had already noticed it in resonators: the time they take to die out, multiplied by their tuning width, gives a number of the order of one.

The same wall, in the first spectrograph

The inventors of the spectrograph ran into it in their first paper, also from 1946. With the narrow filter, the fast trill of a thrush came out almost completely blurred; with the wide one, every note could be seen.

Listen to the two notes for the time the control sets and make their spectrum show two separate peaks, with a gap of at least 3 dB between them. With “Another pair of notes” you change the difference between the two and try again.

Bangs and harmonics at once

A sustained voice, an “aah,” is a sum of Ferris wheels: the fundamental and its harmonics, spaced from each other by exactly the frequency of the fundamental (lesson 3). To see them as separate horizontal lines, the window has to last at least about twice one divided by that frequency. But if the same recording has a snap, the window stretches it out in time: with a Hann window, the footprint of a snap takes up a little more than a third of the window’s duration.

With a high voice there is a range of windows that works for both things, even if it is narrow. With a low voice, as you are about to check, there is none.

Move the window until the harmonics of the “aah” show up separate, with a gap of at least 3 dB between the second and the third, and at the same time the snap is a thin line, no more than 5 milliseconds wide. Then pick the low voice and try again.

Can the two things be separated?

The two conditions of the previous challenge can be stated in general. To separate two notes a few hertz apart, the Hann window has to last at least twice as long as one pulse of their beat, that is, twice one divided by the difference between them. To separate two bangs, it can’t last more than twice the spacing between them. Both requirements fit in the same window only if the bangs are at least one beat pulse apart; put another way, if you multiply the spacing between the bangs, in seconds, by the difference between the notes, in hertz, the result has to be at least one.

It is the tile again, with another face: asking for both things at once is asking for a tile narrower in time and in frequency than its minimum area allows.

Each case mixes two bangs (two vertical lines) and two notes (two horizontal lines). Move the window until you separate the two bangs and the two notes at once, or press “Impossible” if you think no window exists that can do it. Solve two cases in a row.

It is worth being clear about what this limit does not say. It doesn’t prevent measuring the frequency of a single clean note very precisely: if there is little noise, a tuner can tune much more finely than the rule of one divided by the duration suggests. What it prevents is separating two very close notes, or knowing when and which at once with a single measurement. Nor does it prevent sharpening the picture if you assume something about the signal, and that is exactly what reassignment and synchrosqueezing will do in lessons 18 and 19.

Nor is there one window that is good for all signals. A whistle that rises while it sounds forces a choice between two evils: with a long window, the note changes inside it and the spectrogram line gets thicker, and with a short one it gets thicker because of the uncertainty. In between there is a window that leaves the line as thin as possible, and for a whistle that rises 2,000 hertz per second, the best Gaussian window is about 9 milliseconds wide; for one four times faster, half of that. The best window depends on the signal, and in a single recording there are sounds of every kind.

What about the ear?

In 2013, an experiment with twelve people, many of them musicians, found that some could tell both when a note arrives and whether it is higher better than a spectrogram would allow. Its authors concluded that the ear doesn’t work like a simple filter bank. Other researchers responded that the experiment didn’t measure the same thing as the limit. What nobody disputes is that the ear is not a spectrogram: it does more complicated things.

In your voice

What to record. Three claps and then a long “aah” on a comfortable note, all in a single recording of about 8 seconds: they are segments 6 and 3 of the series’ reference recording. Clap away from the phone so it doesn’t saturate, and if you can, in a room without much echo. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The same audio comes out in two spectrograms with the same color scale, one with a short window and one with a long one. In the short one, each clap is a thin vertical line and your “aah” breaks up into wide bands crossed by little vertical lines, one for each pulse of your vocal cords. In the long one, the harmonics of the “aah” separate as horizontal lines and each clap turns into a wide smear, with its echo tail. Bring the two windows closer with the controls and look for the middle ground: with a high voice it shows up, and with a low one it doesn’t.

What to remember

  1. What is concentrated in time is spread out in frequency, and the other way around. Each measurement occupies a tile of the time–frequency plane that can be stretched or flattened, but not shrunk, and that is why a short window shows when well and a long one shows which one well.
  2. Measured with standard deviations, the product of the width in time and the width in frequency has a minimum, the Gabor limit, and only a Ferris wheel trimmed by a Gaussian bell curve reaches it. The exact value depends on how the width is measured; that the minimum exists does not.
  3. It is the same inequality as Heisenberg’s, but in a sound there is nothing quantum: it is a property of all waves. It doesn’t prevent measuring a clean note precisely, but separating and locating things with a single window.

Common mistakes

  • “With a more powerful computer, or more samples per second, the limit disappears.” The limit depends on how long the window lasts, not on the sampling rate or the computing power. More samples per second add frequencies at the top, but they don’t sharpen the ones that were already there.
  • “Zero padding sharpens the frequency and gets around the limit.” Zero padding only draws more points of the same curve, as we saw in lesson 5. The tile stays just as tall.
  • “The limit prevents measuring the frequency of a note precisely.” What it prevents is separating two nearby components, or knowing when and which at once. A single clean note, with little noise, can be measured far more precisely than one divided by its duration.
  • “It’s the same as Heisenberg, so it’s quantum.” It is the same mathematical inequality with a different meaning: in a sound there are no particles or observers disturbing it when you measure. It is a property of all waves.
  • “With the rectangular window the tile is smaller, because it uses all the samples.” The other way around: its abrupt edges spread energy far from the peak and, measured with standard deviations, its tile is vastly larger. The smallest is the Gaussian bell curve.

Math corner

The formulas, for anyone who wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

Where a signal is and how spread out it is

The energy of the signal is the integral of its squared value, and it is treated as if it were a probability. The mean instant \bar t and the standard deviation in time \sigma_t are computed from the energy of the signal, and the mean frequency \bar f and the standard deviation in frequency \sigma_f, from the energy of its spectrum X(f):

E = \int |x(t)|^2\,dt, \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \frac{1}{E}\int (t-\bar t)^2\,|x(t)|^2\,dt, \qquad \sigma_f^2 = \frac{1}{E}\int (f-\bar f)^2\,|X(f)|^2\,df

The limit

The product of the two widths never drops below one over four pi, with frequency measured in hertz:

\sigma_t\,\sigma_f \;\ge\; \frac{1}{4\pi}

The equality

It is reached only by a Ferris wheel of any speed trimmed by a Gaussian bell curve, shifted in time to wherever you like:

x(t) = A\, e^{-\frac{(t-t_0)^2}{4\sigma_t^2}}\, e^{i(2\pi f_0 t + \varphi)}

Gabor’s version

Gabor multiplied each standard deviation by the square root of two pi to call them “effective durations,” and with that measure the minimum is one half:

\Delta t = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_t,\quad \Delta f = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_f \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta t\,\Delta f \ge \tfrac{1}{2}

Why it is true, in one line

With the center at zero, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality between t\,x(t) and the derivative of x gives a quarter of the squared energy. Since the energy of the derivative is four pi squared times the energy of f\,X(f), the limit follows. Equality requires the derivative to be proportional to t\,x(t), and only the bell curve satisfies that:

\left(\int t^2|x|^2dt\right)\left(\int |x'|^2dt\right) \ge \frac{E^2}{4}, \qquad \int |x'|^2dt = 4\pi^2\!\int f^2|X(f)|^2df

The same inequality in quantum mechanics

If momentum is written as Planck’s constant times the “spatial frequency” of the wave, according to the de Broglie relation, multiplying the limit by h gives Kennard’s inequality for position and momentum:

p = h\,\nu_x \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_x\,\sigma_p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi} = \frac{\hbar}{2}

The Hann window

A Hann window of duration T stays within 2.6% of the minimum:

\sigma_t \approx 0.1414\,T, \qquad \sigma_f = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}\,T}, \qquad \sigma_t\,\sigma_f \approx 1.026\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi}

Rules of thumb with the Hann window

Measured with a 3 dB gap between peaks: two notes \Delta f hertz apart are separated if the window lasts at least two divided by that difference, and two bangs d seconds apart, if they are at least half a window apart. Both fit together if the product is at least one:

T \ge \frac{2}{\Delta f}, \qquad d \ge \frac{T}{2} \;\Rightarrow\; d\,\Delta f \ge 1

The best window for a rising tone

For a tone that rises c hertz every second, with a Gaussian window of standard deviation \sigma measured in the amplitude, the spectrogram line is as thin as possible with this window, and its minimum width, as the standard deviation of the energy in frequency, is:

\sigma_{\text{opt}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi c}}, \qquad \sigma_f^{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{c}{2\pi}}
