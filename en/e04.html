Level 2 · Lesson 4

The Fourier transform, without equations

Someone plays a chord on a guitar and you record it with your phone. On the screen you get a tangled curve that goes up and down with no obvious order, and however long you stare at it, you can't read a single note in it. Yet your ear tells the notes that sound together apart without any effort, and a program that knows nothing about music can pull them out of that very same curve. If I give you only the final curve of a chord, how do you find out which notes make it up?

  • About 45 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 3 you saw that adding Ferris wheels is easy: you add their heights at every instant and out comes the curve of a chord, a vowel or a violin. This lesson goes the other way. You are given only the final curve, and you have to work out how many wheels make it up, how fast each one turns and how big each one is, none of which you can read at a glance.

What you do is ask. Next to the signal you place a test wheel that turns at whatever speed you choose, and you multiply the two heights at every instant. If the signal contains a wheel that turns at the same speed, the two rise and fall together, the product is almost always positive and the area grows. At any other speed, sometimes they agree and sometimes they disagree, and the positive parts cancel the negative ones until the area is zero. Repeating the question at every speed and writing down each area is the master idea of the course: to transform is to measure the resemblance to a template.

Step by step

Asking a test wheel

Imagine a signal hides a single Ferris wheel and you want to know how fast it turns. You try a test wheel and compare the two in a very specific way: you multiply their heights, instant by instant, and add up the result, which you see as the area between the curve of the product and the zero line. That operation is called correlation, and it measures how much two signals resemble each other.

When the two rise together, or fall together, the product is positive, and when one rises while the other falls, it is negative. If the test wheel turns like the hidden one, the product stays above zero almost all the time and the area gets large. At any other speed, the hits and the misses are split evenly and the area stays around zero.

The green signal hides a Ferris wheel. Move the speed of the test wheel until you find the one that gives the most area.

The area is shown so that a wheel of the same size turning at the same speed gives exactly 1.

When the area vanishes completely

For the area to vanish completely, the piece of signal you are looking at has to hold whole turns of both wheels. Then the positive and negative parts of the product are identical and cancel without leaving anything over. This property of wheels that complete whole turns, that of giving zero area when multiplied if they don't turn at the same speed, is called orthogonality, and it is what lets you pull one wheel out of the signal without the others getting mixed in.

If the piece has a leftover fraction of a turn, the product no longer cancels and a remainder is left. That remainder is called leakage and it is the subject of lesson 5; for now it is enough to know it exists and that it explains why an area sometimes doesn't vanish completely.

The signal lasts one second and is a 3 Hz Ferris wheel. Find a test speed other than 3 Hz that leaves the area at zero.

Two shadows so you don't miss any wheel

One problem is left. A wheel in the signal can start at any point of its turn, and for some starting points the test wheel doesn't see it. If the hidden wheel turns just like the test wheel but starts a quarter turn earlier, one is at the very top just when the other passes through the center, the product splits the positive and the negative into equal parts and the area comes out zero even though the wheel is there. That is what happens when you multiply a sine by a cosine.

The solution is to look at each test wheel with its two shadows, the ones from lesson 1: the one on the wall, which is the height and draws a sine, and the one on the floor, which is the sideways position and draws a cosine. You ask the question twice and get two areas. Those two areas are the legs of a right triangle: the hypotenuse is the size of the hidden wheel, which is called the magnitude, and the angle of the triangle is its starting point. Done for all the speeds, the set of starting points is the phase spectrum.

There is a hidden 4 Hz Ferris wheel. Turn on the two shadows and find out its size and its starting point.

Sweeping all the speeds

What remains is to repeat the question at every test speed and write down the size that comes out for each one. The result is a curve with the speed, that is, the frequency, along the horizontal axis and the size of the wheel on the vertical axis: the spectrum. Each bar is a wheel in the signal. The whole operation, comparing the signal with test wheels of every speed, is the Fourier transform.

The same signal can be described in two ways. In the time domain you say how it changes instant by instant, and that is the green curve. In the frequency domain you say which wheels it contains, and that is the spectrum. They are two descriptions of the same thing, and going from one to the other loses nothing as long as you also keep the starting points.

The word “spectrum”

In 1666 Isaac Newton got hold of a glass prism and, in his 1672 letter, called the band of colors it projected on the wall, about five times longer than it was wide, a spectrum. He argued that white light is a mixture of colors that the prism separates and puts in order. That is where the word we use for frequencies comes from. It is an analogy and not the same operation, because the prism separates the colors by bending each one through a different angle.

A piano that answers you

In the nineteenth century Helmholtz described an experiment that anyone with a piano can repeat. With the dampers lifted, for example by pressing the right-hand pedal, you sing a loud “aah” toward the strings, and the piano sends back a somewhat ghostly “aah.” The strings that match some of the wheels in your voice start to vibrate, and the others stay still: that is sympathetic resonance. The echo is imperfect, as his own translator already said, because the piano is tuned differently and the strings also respond to their harmonics.

Inside the ear

In the cochlea, the snail-shaped part of the inner ear, there is a strip called the basilar membrane. When a sound arrives, a wave runs along it that grows and dies out at a place that depends on the frequency, with high pitches near the entrance and low pitches at the far end, an order called tonotopy. It is a way of splitting the sound into its frequencies, but not an exact Fourier transform: the filters of the ear overlap, they are wider at high pitches and, in a living ear, some cells amplify and sharpen the response. On top of that, the brain also counts the rhythm of the nerve impulses to work out the note.

The signal is a chord of three notes. Sweep the speed of the test wheel and mark the speed of each of the three.

What the size doesn't tell you

The spectrum of sizes is the list of ingredients, but it doesn't say in what order they are added. With the same wheels, of the same size and at the same speeds, you can draw very different waves if the starting point of each one changes, and that is why the transform keeps two numbers per wheel: the magnitude and the phase.

In a sustained sound the ear barely notices the difference, but it is not deaf to phase: when there is a knock, the starting point of the wheels decides when each thing happens. From this will come the spectrogram of lesson 11 and the phases that synchrosqueezing takes advantage of at the end of the course.

Four Ferris wheels of the same size start with their cabins at the top and add up to a wave with a peak of 4. Change their starting points, without touching their sizes, until the peak drops below 2.5.

Reading a real spectrum

A spectrum is read like a recipe: each bar is a wheel, with its speed and its size. Voices and instruments draw a row of peaks at equal distances, the fundamental and its harmonics from lesson 3, and the note you hear is that distance and not the tallest peak. The tallest can be the second or the third harmonic, and sometimes the first isn't even there: over the phone, which doesn't let low frequencies through, a low voice is still heard at its own note even though its first wheel doesn't arrive.

Notice too that the peaks are not needles but mountains with some width. Why that happens, and how much time a test wheel needs to decide its speed, is the question of lesson 5.

This sound has one note, but its first wheel is not in the spectrum. Find out what that note is, in hertz.

In your voice

What to record. A sustained “aah” of a few seconds and, for comparison, a whistle: they are segments 3 and 2 of the series' reference recording. If you like, add an “ee” on the same note. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The lab keeps the half second in which your note is steadiest and launches test wheels from 50 to 2,000 Hz: you will see your spectrum grow as each one turns. Then it shows the complete spectrum with its peaks marked. In your “aah” there should be peaks at equal distances, multiples of your note, and the tallest doesn't have to be the first. The whistle leaves almost a single peak. If you see fixed lines at 50 Hz and its multiples, they are not your harmonics but the hum of the power grid (the wall outlet in Spain, and in most of Europe, runs at 50 Hz). Every peak is a few hertz wide, a preview of lesson 5.

What to remember

  1. To transform is to measure resemblance: you multiply the signal by a test wheel and look at the area. If the wheel is in the signal, the area grows; repeated at every speed, the result is the spectrum, the list of the wheels in the signal.
  2. Each test wheel is looked at with two shadows, the sine one and the cosine one, so that none escapes whatever its starting point. From the two areas come the size of the wheel, the magnitude, and its starting point, the phase.
  3. The spectrum of sizes isn't enough to draw the wave, because with other starting points the shape changes, and it doesn't say when each thing sounds either, only which wheels are in the piece being analyzed. The note is read in the spacing between peaks.

Common mistakes

  • “The Fourier transform tells you when each note sounds.” It tells you which wheels are in the whole piece being analyzed. The when is hidden in the phases, which the spectrum of sizes doesn't show, and to see it at a glance you need the spectrogram of lesson 11.
  • “If the test wheel doesn't match, the area is always zero.” Only when whole turns of both fit in the piece. If a fraction of a turn is left over, a remainder is left, the leakage of lesson 5.
  • “One shadow is enough, the sine one.” A wheel that starts at the top of its turn, a cosine, gives zero area against the sine even though it turns at the same speed. With both shadows none escapes, and you also find out its starting point.
  • “The tallest peak is the note.” The tallest can be a harmonic, and the fundamental can be missing altogether. The note is the distance between neighboring peaks.
  • “The inner ear does exactly a Fourier transform.” It splits the sound by places with filters that overlap, wider at high pitches, nonlinear and active, and the brain also uses the rhythm of the nerve impulses.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The question to the test wheel

The question to the test wheel of frequency f is asked with its two shadows: you multiply the signal by the sine and by the cosine of that frequency, add up the product over the time T that the piece lasts, and normalize so that a wheel of size 1 turning at the same speed gives area 1:

S(f) = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\,\sin(2\pi f t)\,dt, \qquad C(f) = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\,\cos(2\pi f t)\,dt

Size and starting point

If the signal is a single wheel A\,\sin(2\pi f t + \varphi), with the height measured as in lesson 1, and the test wheel turns at the same speed and completes whole turns in the piece, the two areas are:

S = A\,\cos\varphi, \qquad C = A\,\sin\varphi

From these come the size, by Pythagoras, and the starting point, by the angle of the triangle the two areas form:

A = \sqrt{S^2 + C^2}, \qquad \varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(C,\ S)

Orthogonality

Two wheels whose speeds are whole numbers of turns in the time T cancel when multiplied, and with the same speed they give 1 once normalized. It is written, with m and n positive integers:

\frac{2}{T}\int_0^T \sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi m t}{T}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi n t}{T}\right)dt = \begin{cases} 1 & m = n \\ 0 & m \neq n \end{cases}

The complex wheel

The two shadows together form a single wheel that turns in the opposite direction, and the result of the question is a complex number whose size and angle carry the magnitude and the phase:

X(f) = \int_0^T x(t)\,e^{-i 2\pi f t}\,dt = \frac{T}{2}\,(C - i\,S)

Since here the height is measured with the sine, the phase that comes out of this formula is shifted by a quarter turn compared with the one in the lesson. It is only a change of origin: the modulus, the size of the wheel, is the same.

The Fourier transform and its inverse

The transform looks at the entire signal, from minus infinity to infinity, and its inverse adds the wheels back up, each with its size and its starting point:

X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,e^{-i 2\pi f t}\,dt, \qquad x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(f)\,e^{\,i 2\pi f t}\,df

With samples

With N samples, the test wheel is only looked at at the instants of the samples (lesson 5 develops this), and the size of each wheel is recovered by multiplying by 2/N:

X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}, \qquad A_k \approx \frac{2}{N}\,\big|X[k]\big|

The notes of the piano

On a piano tuned in equal temperament, each key multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of 2. Counting m semitones up from the A at 440 Hz:

f = 440\cdot 2^{m/12}\ \text{Hz} \qquad (\text{C}_4:\ m=-9 \Rightarrow 261.63\ \text{Hz})

The map of the cochlea

According to Greenwood's model (1990), the frequency to which a point on the membrane responds best, located at a fraction x of its length counted from the apex, is:

f(x) = 165.4\left(10^{\,2.1\,x} - 0.88\right)\ \text{Hz}

What the filters of the ear measure

The equivalent width of the ear's filters (Glasberg and Moore, 1990) grows with frequency, which here is in kHz. At 1 kHz it gives about 133 Hz:

\mathrm{ERB}(f) = 24.7\,\big(4.37\,f + 1\big)\ \text{Hz}
