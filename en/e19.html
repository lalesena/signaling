Synchrosqueezing: squeezing the spectrogram

Reassignment sharpens the spectrogram into thin lines, but to do it, it moves energy in both time and frequency and piles up at one point what came from different places, so you can no longer get from the image back to the signal. Sharpness seems to cost the way back, and yet the phase of each cell holds what is needed to squeeze the blobs in a different way. Can you have the sharpness of reassignment without losing the ability to recover each component of the signal?

  • About 55 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 18, reassignment carried each point of the spectrogram to its center of gravity, and the clouds of energy contracted into lines. Here each cell still knows the real speed of its Ferris wheel, but it only moves vertically, inside its own column, to the row that matches that speed.

Look closely at what happens to each thing. The dots of the note and of the chirp gather into a thin line, while those of the knock have nowhere to go, because all their label says is the row they are already in. Since no dot changes column, the sum of each column is the same before and after squeezing, and that is what lets you go back.

Step by step

The label of each cell

Each cell of the spectrogram is a test Ferris wheel cut by the window, as in lesson 11, and its arrow turns at the pace of the real Ferris wheel inside it, not at the pace of its row, as we saw in lesson 18. That real speed, read from how far the arrow turns between two snapshots, is the cell’s frequency label.

The cells of one blob, however many different rows they occupy, carry the same label, because the same Ferris wheel is inside all of them. A 440 Hz note draws a wide blob in the spectrogram that covers tens of hertz, but every cell in that blob, read through its phase, says 440 Hz. That was the observation the method was born from: even though the transform is spread over many rows, its oscillating behavior points to the original frequency.

The idea arose in the mid-1990s, when Ingrid Daubechies and Stéphane Maes were looking for ways to analyze sound using ideas taken from models of the auditory nerve, and they presented it in 1996 with wavelets instead of the sliding window of this course. Here we mostly follow the version with a window, which came a little later.

The column in the picture is one instant of two notes, and each cell carries its label drawn as an arrow toward the row it names. On the axis, tap the two rows that the labels of the two blobs point to.

Squeezing vertically, and why you can go back

Synchrosqueezing, from squeeze, takes each cell with its whole complex number, size and phase, and adds it into the row its label names, without moving it to another column. The blob of each note is squeezed into a line, like a wet cloth wrung out only downward. Reassignment did something different: it also moved energy in time, and it kept only the energy.

Not changing column is what lets you go back. If at one instant you add up all the cells in a column of the transform, you get the sample of the signal at the center of that window. That is column-wise inversion, and moving cells to another row within their column does not change the sum. So, after squeezing, the sum of each column still returns the signal, and if you add up only the reconstruction band around one line, you get just that component. Reassignment, which moves values between columns, breaks that sum.

Daubechies brought the method back with Jianfeng Lu and Hau-Tieng Wu in 2011, with wavelets and with theorems: they defined precisely which signals are sums of slowly changing Ferris wheels and proved that synchrosqueezing separates them, which the mode decomposition of lesson 17 promised but could not prove. The version on the short-time Fourier transform, the one behind the spectrograms of this course, was studied by Thakur and Wu in 2011, and Oberlin, Meignen and Perrier named it in 2014 Fourier-based synchrosqueezing.

Squeeze the energy toward the lines as much as you can, without spoiling the signal you recover by adding up each column. Choose where each cell moves and how far, and watch what the sum returns.

The bet: separated components that change slowly

Synchrosqueezing makes a bet about the signal: that it is a sum of Ferris wheels that do not overlap and that change slowly. If the bet holds, each Ferris wheel ends up in its own line, and if not, the trick fails. The guarantees apply to components whose size and frequency vary slowly compared with the window, and which are separated in frequency by more than the width of the blob of one note. With a 10 ms Gaussian window, that is about 37 Hz, and less the longer the window. It is a separation condition similar to the one in lesson 12.

The other condition is slow modulation. If the Ferris wheel speeds up, the label a cell reads is that of an average over the window, and it ends up halfway between the true frequency and that of its row. For a linear chirp that rises α hertz per second and a Gaussian window with standard deviation σ, the part of the blur that survives the squeezing is the leftover blur, called κ here, and it grows with q = 2π·α·σ²: with small q almost everything gets squeezed, and with large q almost nothing does. The longer the window, the better the notes separate, but the worse a chirp is squeezed, and that is the tension in the challenge below.

It does not break the uncertainty principle

Synchrosqueezing does not violate the limit from lesson 12. It adds information: it assumes the signal is a sum of slowly changing Ferris wheels, and with that assumption the blob can be squeezed. For signals that do not meet it, there is no trick.

Find a window with which two close notes and a chirp are all thin at once, with more than 90% of the energy next to their lines. Then press “Another version”: with notes closer together and a much faster chirp, decide whether any window works.

Listening to just your fundamental

A voice with harmonics draws a ladder of lines in the spectrogram, and each line is a Ferris wheel. If you squeeze the image and add up only the band of the lowest line, the fundamental, you recover that Ferris wheel alone, that is, the same voice without its harmonics, which sounds like a low whistle that follows your melody. With the unsqueezed spectrogram, a narrow band leaves part of the blob outside and sounds worse, and to make it sound good you need a band as wide as the blob, which starts to let other things in.

To place the band you need to know where the line goes at each instant. Here it is done the simplest way: in each column you take the maximum of the spectrogram inside a search band that you choose, and the band is centered on it. How to follow a line with guarantees is the subject of lesson 20. The voice in this challenge is synthetic, and it carries some background noise.

Extract the fundamental of the voice with a quality above 25 dB using a band of 20 Hz or less on each side. Set the frequencies between which the lowest line is searched for, how wide the band is, and whether you add up over the squeezed image or the unsqueezed one.

When two lines cross

That synchrosqueezing is invertible does not mean you can reconstruct any piece of the image, because what gets added up are frequency bands within each instant. At a crossing, two Ferris wheels go at the same speed at the same instant, their two lines share a band and no label can say which bit belongs to whom. Away from the crossing, each line has its own band and is reconstructed very well.

There is one last case where it does not help, that of a knock. For a knock, the label of each cell is its own row, so nothing moves, and in time it stays as wide as the window. Reassignment does sharpen it, because it moves in time, and that is part of what you pay for being able to go back.

Two whistles cross: one rises and the other falls. Tap the start and the end of the rising one to place its band, listen to what comes out, and decide why the sound gets spoiled near the crossing.

Noise, and which version to use

Noise gets squeezed too. The method does not know what is noise and what is signal, and it moves all the energy according to its label, so the noise piles up into bright specks that can steal the ridge from the line. It has been proved that synchrosqueezing is robust to bounded perturbations and to Gaussian white noise (Thakur, Brevdo, Fučkar and Wu, 2013), but that does not mean it works miracles: if the band holds as much noise as line, the line is lost just as in the spectrogram, and finding the ridge by maxima can fail somewhat more with the squeezed spectrogram than with the normal one.

The version with wavelets, the original one, sees like a piano: the separation it asks for between components is relative to their frequency, like the octaves of lesson 13. The Fourier version sees like a ruler: it asks for the same separation in hertz everywhere, which is exactly the one between the harmonics of a voice. That is why, to pull harmonics out of a voice, the Fourier one usually works better.

A line rises from 600 to 800 Hz with a small wiggle, and white noise is added to it. Lower the signal-to-noise ratio until you find the lowest one at which the ridge is still healthy, with fewer than 5% of the instants wrong.

In your voice

What to record. A short melody, sung or whistled: it is segment 7 of the series’ reference recording. About 3 seconds is enough, and the lab keeps the 3 seconds with the most sound. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples.

What to look at. On one side, the spectrogram, and on the other, the same spectrogram squeezed, with the same window. Sung, your melody should come out as thin lines: the fundamental and its harmonics, all with the same shape. Then the lab looks for the lowest line, between 80 and 500 Hz if you sing or between 500 and 1,900 Hz if you whistle, as you choose, adds up its band and lets you hear the original, your fundamental alone and what is left when you remove it, which keeps the timbre and sounds hollower. With a whistled melody, the reconstructed fundamental is almost the original. If your fundamental is weaker than the second harmonic, the line jumps to it and you reconstruct an octave higher, which is the octave error of lesson 20. In consonants and silences there is no line, and a clap is still a wide column, as you would expect.

What to remember

  1. Each cell of the spectrogram carries a label, the real speed of the Ferris wheel inside it, and synchrosqueezing moves each cell, with its whole complex number, to the row of its label, without changing its column. The blobs squeeze into lines.
  2. Because no instant changes, the sum of each column is preserved and you can go back to the signal: adding up the band of one line recovers its component alone. Reassignment, which moves in time, loses that possibility.
  3. It does not break the uncertainty principle, but rests on an assumption: components separated in frequency by more than the window and changing slowly. It fails with fast chirps, at crossings, with a lot of noise and with knocks. The question that remains is how to decide where the line goes at each instant.

Common mistakes

  • “Synchrosqueezing is reassignment under another name.” It only moves in frequency, inside each instant, and it moves whole complex numbers. That is why it preserves the sum of each column and lets you go back to the signal.
  • “Synchrosqueezing breaks the uncertainty principle.” It rests on an assumption, that of separated components that change slowly, and if the signal does not meet it, the blob does not squeeze.
  • “If it is invertible, I can reconstruct any piece of the image.” You reconstruct by adding up frequency bands at each instant. At a crossing, the two components share a band and cannot be separated.
  • “It works with any signal.” With chirps too fast for the window it only squeezes partway, with a lot of noise the line is lost, and a knock does not sharpen because nothing moves in time.
  • “Squeezing cleans the noise.” The noise gets squeezed too and forms bright specks. With a lot of noise, finding the ridge by maxima can fail even a bit more in synchrosqueezing than in the spectrogram.
  • “The thin line is already the note.” You still have to decide where the line goes at each instant, the ridge of lesson 20. If the ridge jumps to the second harmonic, the note comes out an octave higher.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The model

The signal is a sum of components whose size and frequency change slowly:

x(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_k(t)\cos\big(2\pi\phi_k(t)\big), \qquad f_k(t) = \phi_k'(t)

The conditions

With a Gaussian window of size \sigma, in radians per second, the frequency of each component changes little during the window, its size changes little in one turn, and two components are more than twice the width of the window apart in frequency:

\sigma^2\,|\varphi_k''(t)| \le \varepsilon, \qquad |a_k'(t)| \le \varepsilon\,\varphi_k'(t), \qquad |\varphi_k'(t) - \varphi_l'(t)| \ge 2\Delta, \quad \Delta = \frac{\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{\sigma}

In hertz, the minimum separation between two components, with the standard deviation of the window in seconds, is:

|f_k - f_l| \ge \frac{\sqrt{2\ln 2}}{\pi\,\sigma}

The short-time transform referred to the center of the window

The test Ferris wheel starts counting at the center of the window:

\tilde V_x(t,f) = \int x(s)\,g(s-t)\,e^{-i2\pi f (s-t)}\,ds

The label

The frequency that the phase of each cell gives, computed with the transform of the derivative of the window, which matches how fast the phase turns in time:

\hat f(t,f) = f - \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\operatorname{Im}\!\left\{\frac{V_x^{g'}(t,f)}{V_x^{g}(t,f)}\right\} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\arg \tilde V_x(t,f)

Squeezing

Each cell, with its complex number, is added into the row of its label, at the same instant, and only the cells whose size exceeds a threshold \gamma are used:

T_x(t,f') = \frac{1}{g(0)}\int_{|\tilde V_x(t,f)|>\gamma} \tilde V_x(t,f)\;\delta\big(f' - \hat f(t,f)\big)\,df

Column-wise inversion

Adding up the transform of one instant over frequency gives the signal at that instant, multiplied by g(0):

x(t) = \frac{1}{g(0)}\int \tilde V_x(t,f)\,df

In its discrete version, with N cells per column and a real signal:

x[n] = \frac{1}{N\,g[0]}\Big(\tilde V[n,0] + \tilde V[n,\tfrac{N}{2}] + 2\,\operatorname{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{N/2-1}\tilde V[n,k]\Big)

Squeezing does not change the sum of the column

Moving to another row inside the column leaves the sum of what exceeds the threshold unchanged:

\sum_{k'} T[n,k'] = \frac{1}{g[0]}\sum_{k:\,|\tilde V[n,k]|>\gamma} \tilde V[n,k]

Reconstructing one component

You add up only the band of width 2d around its line, and with a real signal you take twice the real part:

x_k(t) \approx 2\,\operatorname{Re}\int_{|f - f_k(t)|<d} T_x(t,f)\,df

What is left with a chirp

With a linear chirp of \alpha hertz per second and a Gaussian window, the label ends up between the true frequency and that of the row, and the fraction of the blur that survives is \kappa. It follows from equation 17 of Auger and coauthors (2013) and matches the simulations in this lesson:

\hat f = (1-\kappa)\,f_i(t) + \kappa\,f, \qquad \kappa = \frac{q^2}{1+q^2}, \qquad q = 2\pi\,\alpha\,\sigma^2

The wavelet version

The label is the turning speed of the wavelet transform in time, in each frequency row you add up the scales that point to it, and the signal comes out by adding up rows, with a constant that depends only on the wavelet:

\omega(a,b) = -i\,\frac{\partial_b W_x(a,b)}{W_x(a,b)}, \qquad T_x(\omega_\ell,b) = \frac{1}{\Delta\omega}\sum_{a_k:\,|\omega(a_k,b)-\omega_\ell|\le\Delta\omega/2} W_x(a_k,b)\,a_k^{-3/2}\,(\Delta a)_k
x(b) \approx \operatorname{Re}\Big[C_\psi^{-1}\sum_\ell T_x(\omega_\ell,b)\,\Delta\omega\Big]

With wavelets, the separation asked for is relative:

|\varphi_k' - \varphi_{k-1}'| \ge d\,(\varphi_k' + \varphi_{k-1}')
