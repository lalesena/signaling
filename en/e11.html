Fourier doesn’t know when: the spectrogram

A song and the same song played backwards have exactly the same amplitude spectrum: the same Ferris wheels, each one the same size. And yet they sound very different, because a piano played backwards starts softly, swells and cuts off all at once. The spectrum knows everything about which frequencies are in a sound and still can’t tell a recording from its mirror image. What got lost along the way, and how do you get it back?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 4, the spectrum came from comparing the signal with a test Ferris wheel for each frequency, and each wheel turned for the whole recording. The spectrogram does the same with one chunk at a time. You cut out a short chunk with a smooth window, the Hann window from lesson 5, which leaves no edges for energy to escape through. You run Fourier on that chunk, and its spectrum is turned on its side and becomes a column of colors. You slide the window a little and repeat, and the columns, side by side, form a map with time across, frequency up and the energy of each cell as color, in decibels.

The procedure is called the short-time Fourier transform, and the spectrogram is the energy of each of its cells. Move the window along the wave and watch how each position adds a column; with the other control, lengthen or shorten the window and watch how the picture changes.

Step by step

What Fourier knows and what it doesn’t

Each test Ferris wheel from lesson 4 turns for the whole recording, and the spectrum says how much the signal resembles each one. That answers very well which wheels are there and how big they are, but since the wheels have no beginning or end, the spectrum has no way of saying when each thing sounds.

The clearest proof is to reverse a recording. If you read an audio file from back to front, its amplitude spectrum doesn’t change at all: the same wheels show up with the same size. The only thing that changes is the phase, the starting point of each wheel (lesson 1), and there, spread among all of them, the order in which things sound is hidden. This holds for the spectrum of the whole recording, computed in one go, and it is true of any real signal, as all audio is.

As long as the sound doesn’t change, it doesn’t matter: a tuning fork sounds the same from start to finish and its spectrum tells the whole story. Voice, music or birdsong change from one instant to the next, and a signal like that is called a nonstationary signal. With it, the question is no longer which wheels are there, but which wheels are there right now.

Listen to the two versions of a three-note melody and find the one that plays forward. The amplitude spectra of both, drawn above, are identical, so you will have to trust your ear. Get it right twice in a row.

Cut, look and stack

To know which wheels are there right now, it is enough to look only at what is happening right now. You place a short window over the signal, a few tens of milliseconds long, and only what falls inside counts. You run Fourier on that chunk and its spectrum says what sounds at that instant. If you repeat the calculation with the window in one place and then in another, you have a photograph of what sounds at each moment.

The window advances in jumps, and the distance between the start of one window and the start of the next is the hop. A small hop gives many columns per second and a large one gives few, but it doesn’t change how sharp each column comes out, which is down to the duration of the window. In lesson 10 the signal was also cut into chunks, but there the chunks were averaged to remove noise; here they are lined up, one next to the other, to see how things change.

With a symmetric window, the spectrogram of the reversed recording is exactly the original seen in a mirror, with time turned around. That is the sign that the when is now stored: each column takes its own place, and reversing the recording changes that place.

Four notes sound, one after another, and one of them is 554 Hz. Drag the window, or move it with the keyboard arrows, until it sits on top of that note, well inside it. The spectrum of the chunk tells you what sounds at each spot.

Four shapes to read almost any sound

A spectrogram reads like a score that the sound draws itself, with time across and the height of the note vertical. A tone that holds, like a sustained whistle, is a horizontal line. If the tone changes, the line curves, and a whistle that rises draws an ascending diagonal. A bang, like a clap or the start of a “p,” lasts an instant and sounds at many frequencies at once, so it draws a vertical line. Noise, like an “s” or the wind, has no note or instant: it spreads over a zone of frequencies and draws a cloud.

The fourth shape is a stack of horizontal lines at the same distance from each other, which is what a sung vowel draws and which the next step covers. With these four, the curving line, the vertical, the cloud and the stack, you can interpret almost any spectrogram.

Listen to the four sounds and choose the spectrogram that matches each one. Each drawing fits only one sound, and they are shuffled on every visit.

A note is a stack of lines

When you sing an “aah,” you don’t get a single line, but a stack of horizontal lines at the same distance from each other. They are the harmonics from lesson 3, the wheels sounding at once at a frequency that is a multiple of the fundamental, and the distance between two consecutive lines is exactly the frequency of the fundamental. The note you hear is the lowest line, and the ones above it make up its timbre. A low voice has the lines closer together and a high voice has them farther apart.

If you sing an “ah” and then an “ee” on the same note, the lines stay where they were, because the note hasn’t changed. What changes is which ones light up, because the mouth and throat reinforce some zones of frequencies and mute others. Those brighter bands within the stack are the formants. In the “ee” the first band sits very low and the second very high; in the “ah” the two come closer together in the middle. The numbers vary from one person to another, and that is why it is better to look at the shape than at the figures.

Formants also explain why a whisper can be understood. When you whisper, the vocal cords don’t vibrate and the harmonic lines disappear, but the bright bands stay in place, and with them, the vowels.

This melody has five notes. Tap the spectrogram once per note, at the height of its fundamental, which is the lowest line of each stack. You can drag the marks to correct them.

The window is a decision

The duration of the window decides the sharpness, and there is no duration that is good for everything. With a short window, onsets and bangs come out thin, but notes blur in frequency and the harmonics merge. With a long window the opposite happens: notes are thin lines and the harmonics separate, but bangs spread out into wide smears. Lesson 12 explains why you can’t have both at once.

The first spectrograph already had to choose. It was presented in 1946 by Koenig, Dunn and Lacy, of Bell Labs, and it had two filters, a narrow one of 45 hertz and a wide one of 300. With the narrow one you could see each harmonic of the voice going up and down with the intonation; with the wide one, the vowels appeared as dark bands and each vibration of the vocal cords as a vertical line. Its inventors settled on the wide one as a compromise. Today people speak of a narrowband spectrogram, made with a long window, which shows the harmonics, and a wideband one, with a short window, which shows the formants and the bangs.

The other controls of a spectrogram do different things. The hop only decides how many columns there are per second, and zero padding, as in lesson 5, draws the same column with more points without adding information. The decibel range decides what is seen and what sinks into the black, and that is why the color is not the volume you hear: it depends on the range you have chosen. The sharpness is decided by the duration of the chunk, which is tied to the spacing between rows: with chunks of 512 samples at 11,025 Hz, each chunk lasts about 0.046 s and the rows of the map are 21.6 Hz apart, and the longer the duration, the closer together the rows.

This synthetic birdsong is a trill, a series of very closely spaced notes, followed by a whistle. With the long window it starts with, the notes merge into a band. Shorten the window until they separate, count how many notes the trill has in its second, and type the number.

The axis is a decision too

The frequency axis of a spectrogram can be linear, with the hertz spread out evenly, or logarithmic, with each octave taking up the same space, like the octaves on a piano keyboard. The linear one gives almost all the room to the high pitches and very little to the low ones, while the logarithmic one treats notes of any pitch equally.

A famous case: in 1999, Aphex Twin hid his own face in sound. It is at the end of the second track of the single Windowlicker, the one titled with a mathematical formula, starting at minute 5:27 and lasting about ten seconds. To see it you need a spectrogram with the frequency axis on a logarithmic scale, and on a linear one it comes out distorted. It is attributed to a program called MetaSynth, which turns an image into sound. The song that gives the single its name hides another image at the end, a spiral. Neither the image nor the audio of those tracks can be here, because they belong to third parties, and that is why the challenge repeats the trick with a word hidden in a synthetic sound.

There is a four-letter word hidden in this sound, drawn with tones. Find the spectrogram setting that lets you read it and type it in the field.

The spectrogram is good for much more than looking. Shazam, according to the method Wang published in 2003, doesn’t compare whole songs: it turns each song into a spectrogram and keeps only its peaks, scattered points that look like a starry sky. To recognize what is playing, it slides the constellation of your recording over that of each song until many stars coincide at once, and each pair of peaks forms a small audio fingerprint that is easy to search for. It withstands a huge amount of noise: in the tests in the paper, it was enough for 1 or 2% of the fingerprints of a 15-second sample to survive. That is the 2003 method, and I don’t know how the app works today.

Ornithologists have been reading birdsong with their eyes for more than seventy years. The 1946 paper on the spectrograph already included the song of a nightingale and that of a thrush, which at the end of its song emits two notes at once. In 1954, W. H. Thorpe used the device to study how chaffinches learn to sing. The machine sold afterward, the Sona-Graph, gave its name to the birders’ sonagram. Today the Merlin app, from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, turns each recording into a spectrogram and classifies it with image-recognition techniques; when its sound identification feature came out, in 2021, it distinguished more than 400 species from the United States and Canada.

To view your own recordings outside this page, Audacity has a spectrogram view in which you choose the window size and the scale of the axis, linear or logarithmic.

In your voice

What to record. An “aah” followed by an “eee” on the same note, about 3 seconds each, which are segment 3 of the series’ reference recording. If you want more, try a whistle that goes up and down (segment 4), a sentence (segment 5) or a whistled melody (segment 7). Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or pick one of the synthetic examples.

What to look at. The spectrogram of whatever you load, with the spectrum of the whole recording turned on its side next to it. In narrowband, your “aah” breaks up into a stack of horizontal lines, closer together the lower your voice is. In wideband, the lines melt into bands and each vibration of your vocal cords shows up as a vertical line. Notice that in the “eee” different bands light up than in the “aah,” even though the note is the same. If you see thin, fixed lines at 50, 100 and 150 Hz even in the silences (60, 120 and 180 Hz in much of the Americas), that is the hum of the power line and not your voice.

What to remember

  1. The spectrum of a whole recording says which wheels are there, but not when they sound: a recording and the same one backwards have the same amplitude spectrum, and the order is hidden in the phase.
  2. The spectrogram cuts the signal with a sliding window, runs Fourier on each chunk and stacks the spectra as columns of color: time to the right, frequency upward and energy in decibels. It is read by shapes: the line, the vertical, the stack of harmonics and the cloud.
  3. The duration of the window is a decision: short, it shows when well; long, it shows which note well. The hop and the zeros sharpen nothing, and the color depends on the decibel range you choose.

Common mistakes

  • “The spectrum already says when each note sounds.” The amplitude spectrum of a recording and that of the same one backwards are identical. The order is in the phase, spread among all the wheels, and can’t be read at a glance. To know when, you have to cut the signal into chunks, which is what the spectrogram does.
  • “To see more detail I reduce the hop or pad with zeros.” The hop only adds columns and padding with zeros only draws more points of the same curve, as we saw in lesson 5. The sharpness in frequency is decided by the duration of the window.
  • “Each horizontal line is a different note.” A single note from a voice or an instrument is a whole stack of lines, its harmonics. The note is the lowest line, or the distance between the lines, and the “ah” and the “ee” sung on the same note have the same lines, with different bands lit up.
  • “The color is the volume you hear.” The color is the energy of each cell in decibels relative to the maximum, and it depends on the chosen range: if you lower the floor, things that couldn’t be heard appear, and if you raise it, they disappear. Besides, the ear doesn’t hear all frequencies equally loud.
  • “The longer the window, the better.” A long window sharpens the notes, but turns each bang into a wide smear. There is no best window in general: it depends on what you want to see, and that is what lesson 12 is about.

Math corner

The formulas, for anyone who wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The short-time Fourier transform

For each instant \tau, the signal is multiplied by the window centered at \tau and the result is compared with a Ferris wheel of frequency f, adding up the area of the product:

X(\tau, f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, w(t-\tau)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt

The spectrogram

The spectrogram is the energy of each cell, and in decibels, ten times its logarithm:

S(\tau, f) = |X(\tau, f)|^2, \qquad S_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10 \log_{10} S

In samples

Column m starts m hops further on, row k corresponds to k times the sampling rate divided by the chunk size N, and H is the hop in samples:

X[m,k] = \sum_{n} x[n]\, w[n - mH]\, e^{-i 2\pi k n / N}, \qquad t_m = \frac{mH}{f_s}, \qquad f_k = \frac{k f_s}{N}

Chunk duration and row spacing

The chunk lasts as long as it takes N samples to go by, the rows are separated by the sampling rate divided by N, and the product of the two always equals one:

\Delta t_{\text{chunk}} = \frac{N}{f_s}, \qquad \Delta f_{\text{row}} = \frac{f_s}{N}, \qquad \Delta t_{\text{chunk}}\,\Delta f_{\text{row}} = 1

The song backwards

If y is x reversed within a duration T, and x is real, the transform of y is the conjugate of that of x with a phase twist, and the magnitudes coincide:

y(t) = x(T - t) \;\Rightarrow\; Y(f) = e^{-i 2\pi f T}\, \overline{X(f)} \;\Rightarrow\; |Y(f)| = |X(f)|

Its spectrogram, in the mirror

With a symmetric window, the spectrogram of the reversed version is the original with time inverted:

|Y(\tau, f)| = |X(T - \tau, f)|

The Shazam fingerprint

Each pair of peaks gives a fingerprint with two frequencies and a time difference. If the sample comes from the song, the differences between the song’s instants and the sample’s instants pile up at a single value, the offset:

h = (f_1,\, f_2,\, t_2 - t_1), \qquad \delta t_k = t'_k - t_k \approx \text{constant}
