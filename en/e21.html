The frontier: what we still don’t know

The modern theory of synchrosqueezing dates from 2011, and many variants have been invented since. They all try to fix something the original method takes for granted about the signal, and that assumption fails in very ordinary ways: a Ferris wheel that speeds up fast, one that is not round, two that cross, or one wrapped in noise. What has happened since then, and what is still unsolved?

  • About 55 minutes

The idea in one picture

The method from lesson 19 asks each bit of energy how fast its wheel is turning and places it in the row for that speed. That question has a clean answer only under certain conditions, and almost everything invented since 2011 is a finer way of finding out how fast each wheel turns at each instant when one of those conditions fails.

Choose how the wheel gets spoiled and watch the trace: with acceleration it climbs faster and faster, with shape it stops being a sine wave, with the crossing two speeds coincide for an instant, and with the fog it fills up with noise. In this lesson each step fixes one of the four, and at the end we come back to this wheel with a melody.

Step by step

The 2011 promise and its fine print

The idea of synchrosqueezing dates from 1996: Daubechies and Maes took it from a model of the auditory nerve, and the theory that turned it into a mathematical method with guarantees arrived in 2011, with wavelets (Daubechies, Lu, and Wu) and, that same year, with the short-time transform (Thakur and Wu). The guarantees hold for a signal that is a sum of Ferris wheels whose size and speed change slowly and which are well separated in frequency. Pham and Meignen (2017) summarize it this way: weak frequency modulation and components separated by at least a fixed distance.

Since then, almost everything new tries to fix what happens when that assumption fails, and it fails in four ways: the wheel speeds up fast, it is not round, it crosses another one, or it is wrapped in noise. The methods in this lesson do not break the wall of tiles from lesson 12, because no measurement shrinks its time–frequency rectangle. What they do is get around it by assuming something more about the signal.

The first broken assumption is the easiest to see. Synchrosqueezing takes for granted that the wheel does not change speed while the window lasts. If, inside the window, the frequency changes by more than the width of the tile, the line widens. With an 8 ms Gaussian window, that starts to happen at a few thousand hertz per second, and in the challenge you will measure it yourself.

A synthetic whistle climbs faster and faster. Find the acceleration at which the synchrosqueezing line stops being thin and widens to three rows or more.

One wheel or two?

The second assumption is that the wheels are kept apart. Two tones of the same height and almost the same frequency add up to a single tone whose height pulses: the sum of two cosines of equal size is a cosine at the average frequency multiplied by another, very slow one that rises and falls. To the ear, that is a “wah-wah” that speeds up as the tones move apart, until suddenly you hear two notes.

For the map, “two wheels” and “one wheel that pulses” are the same signal written in two ways, and choosing one is a modeling decision. Rilling and Flandrin (2008) studied it with the empirical mode decomposition from lesson 17: its answer depends on the ratio between the frequencies and the ratio between the sizes. That is why synchrosqueezing requires, by hypothesis, a minimum separation between components, and no finer method makes it go away.

Separate the two tones just enough for the spectrum of a 64 ms window to show two peaks instead of one.

When the wheel speeds up fast

The most direct way to fix acceleration is to ask about it. Second-order synchrosqueezing (Oberlin, Meignen, and Perrier, 2015) estimates, besides the speed of each bit, how much it is accelerating, and corrects the speed with that acceleration. For a linear chirp with a Gaussian envelope the line comes out exact, and since it only moves energy vertically, the signal can still be reconstructed. The price is five short-time transforms, with the window and four other functions derived from it, instead of one.

Higher-order synchrosqueezing (Pham and Meignen, 2017) goes down the same road with more terms, and it was tested on the first gravitational wave ever detected: a chirp that accelerates faster and faster until two black holes collide. With a fourth-order version, the line followed the end of the collision better, and the reconstructed wave looked more like the one that comes out of numerical relativity.

There are two other routes, born in industrial engineering. The synchroextracting transform (Yu, Yu, and Xu, 2017) does not move energy: it keeps only the coefficients that are already in the row of their estimated frequency and discards the rest. The result is a very thin line, but information is thrown away, and with strong modulations the line can drift away from the true frequency, according to an open-access review by You and colleagues (2020), who apply it to bearings. Multisynchrosqueezing (Yu, Wang, and Zhao, 2019) repeats the synchrosqueezing push several times to concentrate the energy little by little, without losing the ability to reconstruct, according to its abstract.

In the challenge you will compare the spectrogram, synchrosqueezing, synchroextracting, second order, and the reassignment from lesson 18 on a very fast chirp. The synchrosqueezing, synchroextracting, and second-order versions in the challenge are simple ones written for this lesson and are labeled as such; the reassignment is the one from the course library.

The whistle now climbs at 8,000 Hz per second. Choose the method and the window that leave the line thin, at one and a half rows or fewer, and that also let you reconstruct the signal with an error below 10%.

When the wheel is not round

A vowel, a violin, or a heartbeat does not draw a sine wave: it repeats a more complicated shape, and on the map each source leaves a stack of lines, one per harmonic, like the epicycles from lesson 3. Two sources can overlap, and then it is hard to tell which line is the note and which ones are its harmonics.

The de-shape method (Lin, Su, and Wu, 2018) uses the cepstrum, a measure of the rhythm at which the lines of a spectrum repeat, to switch on only the fundamental of each family. In their paper they test it on a fragment of Mozart’s Violin Sonata in E minor, K.304, in which a B3 on the piano (246.9 Hz) sounds at the same time as a B4 on the violin (493.88 Hz), exactly double: every harmonic of the violin falls on a harmonic of the piano. In a spectrogram it looks like a single note with an odd timbre, and the de-shape separates the two and lets you see the violin’s vibrato.

It has limits that the authors themselves point out: a piece of music can have many more components, more than ten in a symphony, and the patterns of multiples get complicated. And since it multiplies by the original map, it cannot switch on a line where there is no energy, so it does not invent a fundamental that is missing altogether. The challenge uses a simple version of the de-shape, written for this lesson, with two synthetic notes.

Two notes sound at once: a piano B3 and a violin B4, both synthetic. Tap the map to mark the frequency of each note, not the frequency of their harmonics. If you get lost, press “De-shape”.

When two wheels cross

At the instant two whistles cross, they sound at the same pitch: on the time–frequency plane they occupy the same point, and no image, however sharp, can separate them. But one goes up and the other goes down. If you add a third axis to the map, how much each wheel is accelerating, the two lines pass over one another without touching.

That is the idea of the chirplet transform (Mann and Haykin, 1995): the window also accelerates, at a rate you choose, and when it matches that of a component, the component becomes a steady note inside the window and its line sharpens. The result is a three-axis representation: time, frequency, and chirp rate, which is how many hertz the frequency goes up or down each second. The chirplet blurs along that third axis, and that is why Chen and Wu (2023) propose a synchrosqueezing on top of it, with which they separate components whose frequencies cross.

In the challenge there are two synthetic whistles, one that rises at 600 Hz per second and one that falls at the same rate, and a 40 ms Gaussian window that accelerates at whatever rate you choose.

Find the two window accelerations that make each whistle sharp, one at a time. Move the slider and press “Lock this acceleration” when one of them looks bright and thin; you need two.

When there is a lot of noise, and when there is no model

With a low ratio between signal and noise, synchrosqueezing manufactures false concentrations: bright blotches where there is nothing. ConceFT (Daubechies, Wang, and Wu, 2016) repeats the computation with many different windows, random combinations of several Hermite functions, and averages. The true lines always show up in the same place, and the blotches from the noise change place every time, so they dilute. It is the averaging from lesson 10, applied to synchrosqueezing. Other open routes are statistics, which makes it possible to decide whether a line is real (Sourisseau, Wu, and Zhou, 2022), and looking at the zeros of the spectrogram instead of its ridges: those of white noise are spread out regularly and do not pile up (Bardenet, Flamant, and Chainais, 2020).

Everything above assumes a signal model. When you do not want to assume any, what is left is learning from examples. CREPE (Kim and colleagues, 2018) is a neural network that takes about 64 milliseconds of audio and returns the note, without computing any spectrogram: 1,024 samples at 16 kHz go in and 360 bins of 20 cents come out. In their tests it was more accurate than pYIN and SWIPE, and more robust to noise in most cases. But it failed with a Chinese flute, the dizi, because in the test split there was none among its training examples, and it estimates each chunk separately, without the path from lesson 20. Learning from examples works very well on whatever resembles the examples.

More networks

SPICE learns pitch without labels, taking advantage of the fact that transposing a note is shifting it on an octave map. PESTO is another network without labels, with fewer than 30,000 parameters. Basic Pitch transcribes several notes at once, and LEAF learns its own tiles instead of using the ones an engineer designed.

In the challenge, the signal is two components with white noise of the same power as they have: that is, at 0 dB. Each “random window” is a different combination of the Hermite functions, and the bubbles reading measures how much shines outside the lines, compared with how much the lines shine.

Bring the false bubbles below 0.26 without erasing the lines, by averaging random windows. With a single Gaussian window they come to 0.30.

What to remember

  1. Synchrosqueezing assumes a signal made of wheels that change slowly and are kept apart. The methods that came after 2011 each fix one broken assumption, and none of them breaks the wall of uncertainty: they get around it by assuming something more.
  2. Every method pays a price. Second order, more computation; synchroextracting, energy it throws away; the de-shape and the chirplet, more parameters; window averaging, more time; and networks, dependence on the examples they learned from.
  3. Some questions have no single answer, such as whether two very close tones are one wheel or two. Still open are crossings, strong noise, signals with many components, the choice of parameters, and the guarantee that a network will work on something it has never seen.

Common mistakes

  • “Synchrosqueezing, or the newest method, breaks the uncertainty principle.” None of them does. They all assume something about the signal, that it has few wheels, which change slowly and are kept apart, and they use that assumption to sharpen the picture. If the signal does not meet it, the sharp image can be false.
  • “The sharpest map is the most reliable one.” Synchroextracting gives very thin lines by throwing energy away, and with strong modulations its line can drift. A thin, displaced line is worse than a wide band in the right place.
  • “Artificial intelligence has already solved it.” CREPE is right most of the time on things that resemble its examples, and it failed on a flute it did not know. It also decides each chunk separately and does not allow the signal to be reconstructed.
  • “Every line on the map is a different source.” A voice or a violin leaves a stack of lines, one per harmonic, and two notes an octave apart share many. Counting sources requires knowing which lines belong to the same family, and that is what the de-shape does.
  • “If two components cross, a sharper image is enough.” At the crossing they occupy the same instant and the same frequency. You need another clue, such as how much each one accelerates, which is a third axis, or their timbre.
  • “There is one best method.” Each one fixes one broken assumption and pays a price. You choose according to what is happening to the signal.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The model of the series

The signal is a sum of wheels whose size and speed change slowly, and which are kept apart by at least a fixed distance:

f(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_k(t)\,\cos\bigl(2\pi\phi_k(t)\bigr), \qquad |A_k'(t)| \le \varepsilon,\quad |\phi_k''(t)| \le \varepsilon,\quad \phi_{k+1}'(t) - \phi_k'(t) \ge 2\Delta

The short-time transform

The signal, seen through a window g centered at t, is compared with a wheel of frequency \eta:

V_f^{g}(t,\eta) = \int f(\tau)\,g(\tau - t)\,e^{-i2\pi\eta(\tau - t)}\,d\tau

The speed the phase tells you

The frequency of each bit is that of its row, corrected by how its phase changes, and it is computed with a second transform made with the derivative of the window:

\hat\omega_f(t,\eta) = \operatorname{Re}\Bigl\{\eta - \frac{1}{i2\pi}\,\frac{V_f^{g'}(t,\eta)}{V_f^{g}(t,\eta)}\Bigr\}

Synchrosqueezing and reconstruction

Each coefficient moves, only vertically, to the row of its speed. Summing a band around a line, with \varphi_k its frequency and d the width of the band, recovers its component:

T_f(t,\omega) = \frac{1}{g(0)}\int_{\{|V_f^{g}|>\gamma\}} V_f^{g}(t,\eta)\,\delta\bigl(\omega - \hat\omega_f(t,\eta)\bigr)\,d\eta, \qquad f_k(t) \approx \int_{|\omega - \varphi_k(t)| < d} T_f(t,\omega)\,d\omega

Second order

We also estimate the instant of each bit and how much its frequency accelerates, and correct the speed with that acceleration. Everything comes from five transforms, with the windows g, g', t\,g, g'', and t\,g':

\tilde\tau_f = t + \frac{V_f^{tg}}{V_f^{g}}, \qquad \tilde q_f = \frac{1}{i2\pi}\,\frac{V_f^{g''}V_f^{g} - \bigl(V_f^{g'}\bigr)^2}{V_f^{tg}V_f^{g'} - V_f^{tg'}V_f^{g}}, \qquad \hat\omega^{[2]}_f = \operatorname{Re}\Bigl\{\tilde\omega_f + \tilde q_f\,\bigl(t - \tilde\tau_f\bigr)\Bigr\}

For a linear chirp with a Gaussian envelope, the real part of \tilde q_f is exactly its acceleration. This is how the second order in this lesson is computed.

When the first order blurs

A chirp with acceleration c falls outside the assumption when the amount its frequency changes inside the window approaches the width of the tile. With a Gaussian window of width \sigma, this is a rule of thumb, consistent with the measurements in this lesson, and it does not come from a paper:

|c|\,\sigma \gtrsim \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |c| \gtrsim \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}

Synchroextracting

According to the description by You and colleagues (2020), a coefficient is kept only if its estimated frequency falls in its own row, and nothing is moved. In discrete form, with \Delta\omega the width of a row:

Te_f(t,\omega) = V_f^{g}(t,\omega)\,\delta\bigl(\omega - \hat\omega_f(t,\omega)\bigr) \qquad (|\omega - \hat\omega_f(t,\omega)| < \Delta\omega/2)

The de-shape

At each instant we measure the rhythm at which the lines of the spectrum repeat, the generalized cepstrum; that rhythm is converted back into frequency and multiplied by the original map. With \gamma = 2, the first step would be the autocorrelation, and in the paper’s tests \gamma = 0.3 is used:

C_f(t,q) = \int \bigl|V_f(t,\xi)\bigr|^{\gamma} e^{-i2\pi q\xi}\,d\xi, \qquad U_f(t,\xi) = C_f\bigl(t,1/\xi\bigr), \qquad W_f(t,\xi) = V_f(t,\xi)\,U_f(t,\xi)

The chirplet

The window also accelerates, at rate c. When c matches that of a component, the component becomes a steady note inside the window and its line sharpens:

V_f^{(g,c)}(t,\eta) = \int f(\tau)\,g(\tau - t)\,e^{-i\pi c(\tau - t)^2}\,e^{-i2\pi\eta(\tau - t)}\,d\tau

ConceFT

We average the magnitudes of synchrosqueezings made with windows that are random mixtures of several orthonormal windows h_j, with weights of norm one:

C_f(t,\omega) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \bigl|T_f^{(h_n)}(t,\omega)\bigr|, \qquad h_n = \sum_{j=1}^{J} r_{n,j}\,h_j, \qquad \sum_{j} r_{n,j}^2 = 1

One wheel or two?

Two wheels of equal size are exactly one wheel at the average speed whose size pulses:

\cos(2\pi f_1 t) + \cos(2\pi f_2 t) = 2\cos\bigl(\pi(f_1 - f_2)t\bigr)\,\cos\bigl(\pi(f_1 + f_2)t\bigr)

The output of CREPE

The note is the average of the bins weighted by the network’s activation \hat y_i, converted from cents to hertz, with a reference frequency of 10 Hz:

c(f) = 1200\log_2\frac{f}{f_{\text{ref}}}, \qquad \hat c = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{360}\hat y_i\,c_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{360}\hat y_i}, \qquad \hat f = f_{\text{ref}}\,2^{\hat c/1200}, \qquad f_{\text{ref}} = 10\ \text{Hz}

Back to the Ferris wheel

This is the path of the whole course: a signal is a sum of Ferris wheels, and almost everything we do with it comes down to finding the size and speed of each one, at every instant. The Fourier transform does it for wheels that do not change, tiles do it when they change slowly, and the methods in the last lessons do it when they change faster, cross, or hide in the noise. The pitch curve of a melody is the speed of the voice’s wheel, drawn over time.

The same maps are used in many places. In voice and music, to transcribe notes and separate voices. In the operating room, a team at Massachusetts General Hospital followed the electroencephalogram of several people with spectrograms as they fell asleep under propofol and as they woke up: when consciousness is lost, very slow waves and alpha waves appear at the front of the head, and everything reverses on the way back (Purdon and colleagues, 2013). Synchrosqueezing has also entered the operating room, but reading the heart, not the brain: Hau-Tieng Wu and his colleagues used it to extract respiration from an electrocardiogram, with a preliminary result on its relationship to anesthetic depth. All of this is research, and it is not presented as clinical practice.

In a factory, a bearing that is starting to fail leaves a trace that changes with the speed of the motor, and reading it sharply is one of the applications of these methods. And on September 14, 2015, the LIGO detectors picked up a wave that in two tenths of a second rose from 35 to 150 hertz, faster and faster: the most extreme Ferris wheel in the series, two black holes spinning around each other until they collide. Its visual proof, in the discovery paper, is a time–frequency map with tiles like those of wavelets, where the signal draws a rising line.

Some things remain open, and it is worth saying so honestly. No method in this lesson breaks the wall of uncertainty: all of them get around it by assuming something about the signal, and when the assumption fails, they fail. There are questions that the signal alone cannot answer, such as whether two very close notes are two wheels or one that pulses. Crossings are still hard, strong noise is still open, signals with many components complicate the methods, and choosing the parameters is still a craft. And a neural network knows what it has seen, not what it has not seen.
