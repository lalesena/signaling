Level 5 · Lesson 15

What frequency does a siren have?

An ambulance goes down your street with its siren rising and falling, and you hear without any effort that it is going up right now. Yet the frequency you have learned to measure with Fourier is a list of turns per second for the whole signal: a siren comes out as a broad smear between its lowest note and its highest, without saying when each one sounds. If every Fourier Ferris wheel turns at a fixed speed from start to finish, what can “the frequency right now” mean?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 1, the Ferris wheel always turned at the same speed, and that speed was the frequency of the wave. Here the wheel can speed up and slow down, like the sound of a siren, and the question changes: how many turns per second it makes now. That speed of the moment is what, in this lesson, is called frequency, and you can see it in the picture in how tightly packed the crests are just to the right of the wheel.

Step by step

Timing one turn

The simplest thing you can do with a turning Ferris wheel is time one turn. Each time the cabin passes through the same point, for example the very top, it has made one full turn, and the time between two passes is how long that turn lasted, the period from lesson 1. The frequency is its inverse: if one turn lasts 1.25 s, the wheel makes 0.8 turns per second. In a signal, the most convenient reference point is the zero crossing, the instant when the wave passes through the height of the axis, and two upward crossings in a row enclose one turn.

As long as the wheel always turns the same way, it makes no difference when you measure. If it changes speed, each turn lasts a little differently and each measurement tells you the speed of that moment, not that of the whole signal.

This wheel turns at a speed you don’t know. Press “Up!” each time the green cabin passes the very top, at least four times, and find out how many turns per second it makes.

The space bar or tapping the wheel also works. “Another wheel” changes the speed and clears your marks.

The wheel’s speedometer

What the stopwatch did has a name. The phase of the wheel is the total angle the cabin has traveled, counting the full turns, and it acts as an odometer. The instantaneous frequency is how fast that phase advances at this moment, how many turns the wheel would make in one second if it kept up the current pace, and it acts as a speedometer. As in a car, one instrument is the slope of the other: where the phase curve steepens, the wheel turns fast, and where it flattens, slowly.

This answers the question in the header. The frequency from the Fourier transform of lesson 4 belongs to wheels that turn at a fixed speed from start to finish, and that is why a siren comes out as a broad smear: to describe its rise with fixed wheels you need many of them. With a single wheel that speeds up and slows down, on the other hand, a frequency that changes with time is enough. The spectrogram from lesson 11 also draws it as a line, but a blurry one, because of the tiles from lesson 12: with a short window the note comes out fat, and with a long one the rise gets smeared.

Chirps: the wheel that speeds up

A signal whose frequency rises or falls over time is a chirp: a bird’s warble, the sweep of a bat, a siren. On the spectrogram it leaves a slanted line. A linear chirp rises the same number of hertz each second, and an exponential chirp rises the same number of octaves each second, that is, it doubles its frequency every equal amount of time. The ear counts octaves, not hertz, so only the second one sounds like a uniform rise. Listen to both, which rise from 200 to 3,200 Hz in two seconds: the linear one seems to rush at the start and get tired at the end.

There is a classic trap in making a chirp with a computer. The phase is what has accumulated, the sum of how much the wheel has turned at each instant, and it isn’t enough to put the current frequency inside the sine: in a linear chirp that starts at f0 and rises k hertz per second, the phase grows with the square of time, and the math corner has the calculation.

Chirps are everywhere. A big brown bat chasing a moth screams in ultrasound, too high for us, and each scream is a chirp: in two to five thousandths of a second, its note drops from about 65,000 to about 25,000 hertz, and from the echo it works out how far away the prey is, with a precision of a centimeter and a half. A slide whistle, a tube closed by a piston, is the easiest chirp to make: the shorter you leave the tube, the higher the note, and if you move the piston while you blow, you make a chirp.

The siren going by

When an ambulance’s siren approaches, its crests reach you bunched together, and it sounds higher than it is; when it moves away, they reach you stretched out, and it sounds lower. This change of frequency due to motion is called the Doppler effect, and it was proposed by Christian Doppler in 1842. In 1845, the Dutchman Buys Ballot put a horn player on a train between Utrecht and Maarssen and placed people with a good ear on the platforms to check it.

The drop as it passes depends only on the ambulance’s speed: at 90 km/h, a siren drops suddenly by about two and a half semitones, whatever the note. The distance at which it passes decides something else, how abrupt the change is: right next to the road, it happens in a fraction of a second, and from far away it is spread over several. A common confusion is to believe that, while the ambulance approaches, the siren keeps rising: it approaches at constant speed, sounds higher than the real note and steady, and what rises is the volume.

Animals use it too. The greater horseshoe bat, which lives on the Iberian Peninsula, screams through its nose a nearly steady note of about 83,000 hertz. When it flies toward something, the echo comes back to it higher because of the Doppler effect, and then it lowers its note just enough for the echo to always reach it at the same frequency, the one it hears best.

Make the siren’s note drop exactly two semitones when the ambulance passes. The distance to the road can be whatever you want.

The message, in the size or in the speed

The Ferris wheel is also the way to send a message by radio. You start from a background wave of fixed speed, the carrier, and the message, the voice or the music, is added to it by changing something about the wheel. In amplitude modulation, AM, the wheel changes size at the rhythm of the message. In frequency modulation, FM, it changes speed: it speeds up and slows down around the carrier’s speed, and how far it strays from it, up and down, is the frequency deviation.

An FM station turns at about 100 million turns per second and, at most, speeds up and slows down by 75,000 turns per second up and down, less than a thousandth, which is the maximum deviation in Western Europe and the United States. Edwin Armstrong patented his system in 1933. A singer’s vibrato is the same thing, a very slow FM of the voice. If the wheel speeds up and slows down hundreds of times per second, the ear stops hearing a note that rises and falls and hears a new timbre.

This signal is an 800 Hz carrier with a slow message, three turns per second, put into its size. Make a pure FM, with no changes in size, that deviates 100 Hz up and down.

Reading a real chirp

On September 14, 2015, the two LIGO detectors, in the United States, recorded a vibration of space itself. For about two tenths of a second, the signal rose from 35 to 250 Hz, faster and faster, in about eight cycles: it was the chirp of two black holes, of about 36 and 29 solar masses, orbiting each other until they merged into one. As it is, it is low and brief, and on a phone speaker it is barely audible. That is why, to listen to it, it is usually raised in pitch, like painting a telescope photo in false color; GWOSC, the center that publishes the data, shifts it 400 Hz upward.

To read its frequency, the LIGO and Virgo physicists did the same thing you did with the wheel in the first challenge: time how long each half turn takes, between two zero crossings in a row, and from that they got 35 turns per second at the start and more than 150 shortly before the collision. The signal on this page is not what the detector measured as it was, but the numerical-relativity waveform that best fits what the Hanford detector measured, already filtered and noise-free, as GWOSC publishes it for figure 1 of the discovery paper.

In your voice

What to record. A whistle that slowly rises and falls, about 10 seconds long: it is segment 4 of the series’ reference recording. Record with the microphone if your browser allows it; if not, upload audio recorded with your phone or try the synthetic examples, which include a siren.

What to look at. The lab first looks for where your whistle is on the spectrogram, filters around it and times the turns between zero crossings, eight at a time. The orange curve that comes out is your instantaneous frequency, and you will see it over the spectrogram: a much thinner line than the blurry smear underneath. The wheel turns at the measured pace, in slow motion, and speeds up and slows down with your mouth. If you sing instead of whistling, the harmonics create extra crossings and the curve comes out messy or jumps from harmonic to harmonic, and that is exactly what lesson 16 will explain.

What to remember

  1. The phase is the angle the wheel has traveled, counting the full turns, and the instantaneous frequency is its rate of change: the wheel’s speedometer. You can measure it by timing turns between zero crossings.
  2. A chirp is a wheel that speeds up. You make it by adding up how much it turns at each instant, and the ear feels it as uniform when it rises the same number of octaves each second, not the same number of hertz.
  3. The Doppler effect, AM and FM all play with the wheel: Doppler changes the speed at which the crests reach you, AM changes their size and FM, their speed. Instantaneous frequency only makes sense with a single wheel that changes slowly compared with one turn.

Common mistakes

  • “The instantaneous frequency is what the spectrum shows at that moment.” The Fourier spectrum looks at the whole signal, and the spectrogram at a piece, and it smears it. The instantaneous frequency is something else: how fast the wheel’s angle advances right now.
  • “While the ambulance approaches, its siren rises in pitch.” While it approaches at constant speed and in a straight line toward you, it sounds higher than the real note, but steady, and as it passes it drops suddenly. If the road passes to one side, the change is spread over the seconds around the pass, and it is more abrupt the closer it passes. What does rise as it approaches is the volume.
  • “To make a linear chirp, you just put the frequency of each instant inside the sine.” The phase is what has accumulated. If you write sin(2π·(f0 + k·t)·t), the real frequency rises twice as fast, f0 + 2·k·t, and it is a classic mistake when programming chirps.
  • “A linear chirp sounds like a uniform rise.” The ear counts octaves, and a linear one from 200 to 3,200 Hz fits more than three octaves in the first second and less than one in the second, so it seems to slow down. The exponential one sounds uniform, because it doubles its frequency every equal amount of time.
  • “Instantaneous frequency always exists.” It makes sense with a single wheel that changes slowly compared with one turn. With two notes at once, or with changes as fast as the spin, it stops meaning “the note you hear,” and that is what lesson 16 is about.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

Phase and instantaneous frequency

A wave is a Ferris wheel whose size A and angle \varphi can change over time:

x(t) = A(t)\,\cos\varphi(t)

The instantaneous frequency is the rate of change of the angle, counted in turns per second:

f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{d\varphi}{dt}

And the angle is the accumulated sum of how much the wheel has turned:

\varphi(t) = \varphi_0 + 2\pi \int_0^t f_i(\tau)\, d\tau

Chirps

A linear chirp starts at f_0 and rises k hertz every second, and its phase grows with the square of time:

f(t) = f_0 + k\,t, \qquad \varphi(t) = 2\pi\left(f_0\,t + \tfrac{k}{2}\,t^2\right)

An exponential chirp multiplies its frequency by the same factor in every equal stretch of time, and in T seconds it goes from f_0 to f_1:

f(t) = f_0 \left(\frac{f_1}{f_0}\right)^{t/T}, \qquad \varphi(t) = \frac{2\pi f_0 T}{\ln(f_1/f_0)}\left[\left(\frac{f_1}{f_0}\right)^{t/T} - 1\right]

Timing turns

Timing N turns between two upward zero crossings, the frequency is N divided by the time they took; between two consecutive crossings, one upward and one downward, there is half a turn:

\hat f \approx \frac{N}{t_{k+N} - t_k}, \qquad \hat f \approx \frac{1}{2\,(t_{k+1} - t_k)}

AM and FM

In AM, the message s changes the size of the carrier; in FM, it changes its speed, and the instantaneous frequency is that of the carrier plus the deviation times the message:

x_{AM}(t) = \left[1 + m\, s(t)\right]\cos(2\pi f_c t), \qquad x_{FM}(t) = \cos\!\left(2\pi f_c t + 2\pi\,\Delta f \int_0^t s(\tau)\,d\tau\right), \qquad f_i = f_c + \Delta f\, s(t)

Carson’s rule says that an FM signal occupies roughly twice the sum of the deviation and the message frequency:

B \approx 2\,(\Delta f + f_m)

Doppler effect

For a source moving with speed v and a listener at rest, where \theta is the angle between the velocity and the line from the source to the listener, and c is the speed of sound:

f_o = f_s\,\frac{c}{c - v\cos\theta}

And the drop as it passes, in semitones, depends only on the speed:

\Delta_{\text{st}} = 12\,\log_2\frac{c + v}{c - v}

The echo received by a bat flying at v toward something at rest carries the effect twice, on the way out and on the way back:

f_{\text{echo}} = f\,\frac{c + v}{c - v}

The slide whistle

The note of a tube closed at one end is the speed of sound divided by four times the length, with the open-end correction, which is about six tenths of the tube’s radius:

f \approx \frac{c}{4\,(L + 0.6\,r)}
