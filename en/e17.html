Level 5 · Lesson 17

When several Ferris wheels sound at once

In a choir, each voice carries its own note and they all change at once, but the microphone only picks up the sum: a single wave that goes up and down. Lesson 16 made it clear that the instantaneous frequency only tells a clean story when there is a single wheel, and a choir is made of many. Can you separate the voices from a single recording?

  • About 50 minutes

The idea in one picture

In lesson 3, a sound was a stack of wheels, the epicycles, each with its own size and speed. Here each wheel can also change its size and speed while it spins, as long as it does so slowly compared with its own turning. We will call each of those wheels a mode, and a signal made of several of them a multicomponent signal. The choir problem is to pull the modes out of a sum without knowing beforehand how many there are or at what frequency they sound.

Step by step

The two ropes

The method in this lesson peels the signal like an onion, from the fastest layer to the slowest, and it only uses the peaks and valleys of the signal itself. It is called empirical mode decomposition, and it was published in 1998 by Norden Huang, who worked at NASA’s Goddard center, together with eight colleagues. The word empirical means that you do not decide beforehand which frequencies to look for: the layers come out of the signal in front of you.

The first move is to join all the maxima with a smooth curve, like a taut rope over the top of the wave, and all the minima with another one underneath. Those two curves are the envelopes, and the line running exactly halfway between them is the slow part of the signal, the one the fast wave keeps riding. To pull the ropes tight you have to find the peaks and valleys, which are not always as obvious as they seem, because a maximum is a point higher than both of its neighbors even if it is below zero.

Tap each maximum and each minimum of the signal, or move between them with the keyboard arrows and mark with Enter. When you have all those of one family, its rope will appear, and when both are there, the middle line.

Peeling a layer: sifting

Subtracting that middle line from the signal leaves it more centered, though not quite yet: when you remove the average, the envelopes change and the new middle line is no longer exactly zero. You repeat, and each round is called sifting, like sifting flour, which lets the fine part through and holds back the coarse. When the middle line barely moves anymore, what is left is the first layer, the fastest one, which fulfills what Huang called an intrinsic mode function: it has as many peaks and valleys as zero crossings, or one more or less, and its two envelopes are symmetric around zero. It can change its size and its speed, and it does not have to be a fixed wave.

The layer is subtracted from the signal and you start over with what is left, until all that remains is a trend with no ups and downs, the residue, which is the heart of the onion. Since each layer is subtracted from what is left, the sum of all the layers and the residue gives back the exact signal; what nobody guarantees is that each layer means anything.

You still need to know when to stop, and for that there is a stopping criterion. If you sift too much, the layer loses its volume swells and dips and ends up as a wave of constant size, and Huang himself warned against overdoing it. Rilling, Flandrin and Gonçalvès (2003) propose comparing the middle line with the size of the layer, and many people settle for a fixed number of passes, about ten, which is more a habit than a rule. If you then apply the analytic signal from lesson 16 to each layer, its radius and its speed draw a time–frequency map, the Hilbert spectrum, and the two steps together are called the Hilbert–Huang transform. In this lesson’s lab you will draw one.

Press “Sift” until the middle line is smaller than 5% of the size of the layer for at least 95% of the time, and never exceeds 50%.

One wheel or two?

What happens when the two wheels are almost the same? Two very close notes, say at 1,000 and 1,100 Hz, sound like a single note that throbs, that is, like beats. The sum then does not have two families of peaks to separate, but just one with the volume going up and down, and the method does the same thing as the ear: it keeps one wheel that throbs. Rilling and Flandrin titled their 2008 study “One or two frequencies?”, and that is exactly this question.

Listen to each pair of notes and decide whether the method will see one wheel that throbs or two wheels. After you choose, you are shown what it pulled out of each one.

The octave rule

The limit has a number. With two wheels of the same size and ten sifting passes, the method only separates them if the slow one turns at less than about two thirds of the speed of the fast one, and in practice the comfortable margin is narrower, around one half, which is an octave. Rilling and Flandrin (2008) proved it: above that ratio it is impossible to separate them, whatever the difference in size. And if the slow one is much bigger than the fast one, the limit drops even lower. Huang acknowledged in his 1998 paper that this is perhaps the biggest limitation of the method.

Raise the slow wheel as high as you can without spoiling the separation: the first mode has to keep resembling the fast 1,000 Hz wheel, with an error under 20%. Listen to mode 1 as you raise it.

When the slow one is much bigger

Size matters too. If the slow wheel is much bigger than the fast one, most of the maxima of the sum belong to it, and those of the fast one get lost among them. A research case illustrates this well: a radar pointed at the chest measures its movement, which carries the breathing, slow and large, and on top of it the heartbeat, fast and tiny. According to Hu and Jin (2016), the heart moves the chest about 0.6 millimeters and breathing moves it between 12 millimeters and several centimeters, which is why work in this field uses improved variants of the method or prefilters.

Here everything is synthetic: a pretend chest with 72 heartbeats and 15 breaths per minute, which is a game and not a medical instrument. The speeds are well below the octave, because the heartbeat goes about five times faster than the breathing, so the only problem is the size.

Raise the size of the breathing, from 0.5 to 20 times that of the heartbeat, up to the maximum at which the first mode still resembles the heartbeat, with an error under 20%.

Mode mixing and its remedies

One flaw remains, and it is the best known. When an oscillation appears and disappears, a layer ends up mixing different scales: while the fast beep sounds, the first layer carries it, and when it goes quiet, that same layer picks up the next fastest thing it finds, which is the slow wave. That is mode mixing, and Huang already warned about it in 1998.

The best-known remedy is EEMD, by Wu and Huang (2009). You do the decomposition many times, each on a copy of the signal with different noise, and average the layers. The noise puts wheels of every speed everywhere, so each scale finds its layer, and when you average, the noise cancels out, just as averaging lowered the noise of a spectrum in lesson 10. Other alternatives change strategy: Gilles’s EWT (2013) splits the spectrum into bands with wavelet filters, and the VMD of Dragomiretskiy and Zosso (2014) pulls out all the layers at once, each one narrow around its own frequency, although you have to tell it beforehand how many.

None of them separates wheels that cross. Empirical decomposition is defined by an algorithm and not by a mathematical model that guarantees what will come out, and it does not know what a voice or a piano is either: the tools that separate the voice from a song today have learned from many songs how each instrument sounds. To separate with guarantees you have to look at what the spectrogram throws away, the phase, and that is what lesson 18 is about.

A slow wave carries two fast beeps that appear and disappear. Get one layer to carry the beeps without letting the slow wave leak in (under 5%) and to resemble the beeps (error under 0.5). Try first with the decomposition alone and then with the one repeated with noise.

In your voice

What to record. The segment of the series’ reference recording in which two people whistle at once, one going up and the other going down (segment 8). If you are alone, record a whistle that goes up and down with the microphone and then another one that goes down and up, or try the synthetic examples. If the microphone is blocked, upload audio recorded with your phone.

What to look at. At the top, the spectrogram of the sum, and below it, the Hilbert spectrum: the instantaneous frequency of each of the first layers, thicker when its volume is greater. With whistles that start far apart, the first mode follows the higher one and the second follows the lower one at the beginning and at the end, and toward the crossing the first one carries both and sounds like beats. If in your recording the whistles never get more than an octave apart, the method does not separate them at any point: that is its limit, not a fault of your recording. With your “aah,” no layer is your note: the first takes the highest harmonics and the following ones go down, roughly octave by octave.

What to remember

  1. A real sound is a sum of modes, wheels whose size and speed change slowly. Empirical decomposition peels them from the fastest layer to the slowest using only the peaks and valleys, and the sum of the layers and the residue gives back the exact signal.
  2. It only separates two wheels if the slow one turns at less than between one half and two thirds of the speed of the fast one and is not much bigger. Closer than that, it sees a single wheel that throbs, just as the ear hears beats.
  3. It is an algorithm with no theory behind it: it mixes modes when something appears and disappears, it bends at the edges, and it depends on the stopping criterion. EEMD, EWT and VMD improve it, but none of them separates wheels that cross, and for that you have to look at the phase.

Common mistakes

  • “Each layer is an instrument or a person.” The layers separate by scales of oscillation. A voice with harmonics is spread over several layers, and two sources less than an octave apart fall into the same one.
  • “A mode is a fixed frequency.” A mode can change its size and its frequency, as long as it does not do so too fast. Huang put it this way: it can be modulated in amplitude and in frequency.
  • “The more sifting passes, the better.” With too many, the layer loses its volume changes and ends up as a wave of constant size. You have to stop with a criterion.
  • “It separates any pair of different notes.” Only if the slow one goes at less than between one half and two thirds of the fast one and is not much stronger. With 1,000 and 1,300 Hz it never separates them.
  • “The first mode is noise and gets thrown away.” The first mode is the fastest thing in the signal. It can be noise or it can be what you are looking for, like the heartbeat in the radar example.

Math corner

The formulas, for whoever wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

The model

The signal is a sum of wheels, each with a radius and a speed that change slowly, plus a trend:

x(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_k(t)\,\cos\theta_k(t) + r(t), \qquad f_k(t) = \frac{\theta_k'(t)}{2\pi}

The two conditions of a mode

A mode has as many extrema as zero crossings, or one more or less, and the average of its two envelopes is zero:

\left|N_{\text{extrema}} - N_{\text{zeros}}\right| \le 1, \qquad m(t) = \frac{e_{\max}(t) + e_{\min}(t)}{2} = 0

One sifting pass

From what you have, subtract the average of its two envelopes:

h_{j+1}(t) = h_j(t) - \frac{e_{\max}^{(j)}(t) + e_{\min}^{(j)}(t)}{2}

When to stop: Huang’s criterion

Each pass is compared with the previous one, and you stop when the accumulated relative change drops below a typical value between 0.2 and 0.3:

\mathrm{SD} = \sum_{t} \frac{\left|h_{k-1}(t) - h_k(t)\right|^2}{h_{k-1}^2(t)}

When to stop: the two thresholds

The average has to be small compared with the size of the mode: below a small threshold almost all the time and below a larger one at every moment (Rilling, Flandrin and Gonçalvès, 2003, with typical values of 0.05 for the first, 0.5 for the second and 0.05 for the fraction of time that is forgiven):

a(t) = \frac{e_{\max}(t) - e_{\min}(t)}{2}, \qquad \sigma(t) = \left|\frac{m(t)}{a(t)}\right|, \qquad \sigma < \theta_1 \ \text{for}\ (1-\alpha)\ \text{of the time}, \quad \sigma < \theta_2 \ \text{always}

The complete decomposition

Each mode is removed from what is left, and at the end everything adds up to the signal:

x(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i(t) + r_n(t)

The Hilbert spectrum

For each mode you compute its analytic signal, and its radius and its speed draw the time–frequency map:

c_i(t) + i\,\mathcal{H}[c_i](t) = a_i(t)\,e^{i\theta_i(t)}, \qquad f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{d\theta_i}{dt}

Two wheels and their limit

With a fast wheel of size 1 and a slow one of size a and relative speed f:

x(t) = \cos 2\pi t + a\,\cos(2\pi f t + \varphi), \qquad 0 < f < 1

The extrema of the sum are those of the fast one if a f is less than 1, and those of the slow one if a f^2 is greater than 1. With ten passes, above f \approx 0.67 there is no separation, whatever a is (Rilling and Flandrin, 2008):

a f < 1 \Rightarrow \text{extrema of the fast one}, \qquad a f^2 > 1 \Rightarrow \text{extrema of the slow one}

EEMD

Each final mode is the average of the modes of I copies with different noise, and the remaining noise drops with the square root of I:

\mathrm{IMF}_k = \frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathrm{IMF}_k\!\left[x + w_i\right], \qquad \text{residual noise} \propto \frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{I}}

VMD

It looks for K modes that add up to the signal and that, shifted down to zero frequency around their center, change as little as possible (Dragomiretskiy and Zosso, 2014):

\min_{\{u_k\},\{\omega_k\}} \sum_{k} \left\| \partial_t\!\left[\left(\delta(t) + \frac{i}{\pi t}\right) * u_k(t)\right] e^{-i\omega_k t} \right\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_k u_k = x
