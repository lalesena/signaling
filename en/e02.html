Snapshots of a Ferris wheel: sampling without fooling yourself

In a movie, a car pulls away and its wheels spin faster and faster, until suddenly they seem to slow down, stand still, and even turn backward while the car keeps moving forward. The camera works fine and so does the wheel, and yet what we see is false. The same deception happens when a device turns the world into numbers, including when it records a sound, and that is why it matters so much how many measurements it takes per second. How can something that measures correctly fool us?

  • About 45 minutes

The idea in one picture

A strobe is a light that flashes at regular intervals, and under it we only see the world at the instants of the flash. With a Ferris wheel turning in the dark, what reaches the eye is a collection of separate photos, and between one and the next we do not know what happened. Turning the world into numbers is exactly that: a device that looks every so often and, between one look and the next, does not look.

Change the speed of the Ferris wheel and the rate of the flash and watch the orange Ferris wheel. With many photos per turn, the orange one and the real one turn the same way. With few, the orange one separates from the real one and turns more slowly, stands still, or goes the other way, while the photos stay exactly the same.

Step by step

Taking photos with a flash

In lesson 1, the height of a Ferris wheel cabin, jotted down instant by instant, drew a wave, and for a machine that wave is a list of numbers in order. But a machine cannot jot down the height at every instant, because there are infinitely many: it jots it down every so often. Each time it looks, it stores a number, the height of the cabin at that moment, and that number is called a sample.

Taking samples at regular intervals is sampling. How many samples are taken per second is the sampling rate, which is measured in hertz because it counts things per second, and the gap between one sample and the next is the sampling period, which is its inverse, just as the period of a turn is the inverse of the frequency. A phone records audio at 44,100 or 48,000 samples per second, and the movies, at 24 photos per second.

Under a flash, each flash is a sample. If many fit in each turn, the eye reconstructs the spin effortlessly. If the flash goes more slowly, what happens between one photo and the next is not seen: you see the cabin in one place and, a moment later, in another, and you have to guess how it got there. What you see in those cases, when something spinning is lit by flashes and seems to go slower, stop, or move backward, is called the stroboscopic effect.

The Ferris wheel makes one turn per second. Move the flash rate until it looks still even though it keeps turning and, after that, until it seems to turn slowly backward.

The wagon wheel

A movie camera takes 24 photos per second, and each photo is a sample of what happens in front of the lens. Picture a car wheel with six identical spokes. If between one photo and the next the wheel turns almost the distance between two spokes, in each photo the nearest spoke appears a little behind where the previous one was, and on the screen the wheel seems to go backward even though the car moves forward. If it turns exactly the distance between two spokes, all the photos are the same and the wheel seems to be stopped. This is the wagon-wheel effect, and the camera has no fault: it does what any system that looks at intervals does.

Since the spokes are identical, what counts is not the whole turn, but how long it takes the wheel to look the same. With six spokes, a sixth of a turn is enough, so the camera actually samples “spokes times turns per second.” A car wheel about 60 cm in diameter makes about 7 turns per second at 50 km/h, so the spokes pass in front of the lens much faster than the camera can count.

Also in daylight

Sometimes the wheel seems to turn the wrong way even without a camera in between, when you look at it in sunlight. Researchers still argue about why: some think the brain processes motion in jumps, and others that motion detectors looking in opposite directions compete. What cannot be said is that the eye sees at 24 photos per second: that figure is a decision of the movie industry.

You do not need a camera for the deception to happen, either. A lamp that flickers at the rhythm of the power grid, which in Europe is 100 flashes per second, acts as a strobe: a saw blade spinning at 6,000 turns per minute, exactly 100 per second, shows up in the same place at every flash and seems stopped. With modern lighting this is rare, but workplace safety guidelines ask to avoid it, because it can be dangerous. Used well, the same deception works as a tool: many turntables have a ring of little marks on the edge of the platter, 180 on the European ones, which under a lamp like that seem still only when the platter turns at exactly 33⅓ turns per minute.

Make the wheel look still in the video even though the car keeps moving forward. Choose how many spokes it has and how many turns per second it spins at.

The car goes to the right, so “forward” is clockwise. The real wheel turns so fast that to the naked eye it would be a blur.

The same photos, many Ferris wheels

An uncomfortable consequence comes out of all this: the photos do not say how many turns the Ferris wheel made between one and the next. If between two photos the cabin makes one extra whole turn, it shows up in exactly the same place, and the photos do not notice. That is why there is not one Ferris wheel that leaves those photos, but many.

An example with numbers: with 10 photos per second, a Ferris wheel of 7 turns per second leaves the same photos as one of 17, which makes one more turn between photos, and as one of 27. And since the height of a cabin does not tell whether the wheel turns one way or the other, a Ferris wheel of 3 turns per second also leaves them, which is what 7 lacks to reach 10, and so do all the ones obtained by adding turns of 10 at a time to that one: 13, 23, and so on without end.

This disguise is called aliasing. The name comes from the word alias, the false name of a criminal: the fast Ferris wheel passes itself off as a slower one. When something turns faster than half the sampling rate, in the samples it shows up as another, slower Ferris wheel, still or reversed, and there is nothing in the photos that gives away the change.

More than two photos per turn

The good news is that the disguise has a limit. If between one photo and the next the cabin advances less than half a turn, of all the Ferris wheels that leave those photos only one advances less than half a turn between photos, the real one, and the others make extra turns. Advancing less than half a turn between photos is the same as taking more than two photos per turn. A real sound, as you will see in lesson 3, is a sum of Ferris wheels of different speeds, and the one in charge is the fastest: the sampling rate has to be greater than twice the frequency of that Ferris wheel. That is the Nyquist rule.

Half the sampling rate is called the Nyquist frequency, and it is the fastest the photos can count without disguising it. Some books use that name for twice the frequency of the signal, but in this course it is half the sampling rate. Notice that the rule asks for more than two photos per turn, not two: with exactly two, the photos can always land when the cabin passes through the middle height, and all the samples would be zero, as if there were no Ferris wheel.

A rule with a borrowed name

The rule is named after Harry Nyquist, who in 1928 studied how many pulses per second could travel along a telegraph line. The complete theorem was published independently by Vladimir Kotelnikov, in 1933, and Claude Shannon, in 1949, and before them several mathematicians had studied how to fill in a curve from points at equal intervals.

With sound, aliasing can be heard. If you play a whistle that rises without stopping, but you take only 8,000 samples per second, when it passes 4,000 hertz the tone does not keep rising: it turns around and goes down, as if bouncing off a mirror placed at the Nyquist frequency. When the rule is met, on the other hand, between the points there is room for only one smooth curve with nothing faster than that frequency, and it can be recovered whole: that is the reconstruction.

A whistle rises from 100 to 6,000 Hz in three seconds. Choose the lowest sampling rate at which the whistle rises all the way to the end without bouncing. With “Listen” you hear it as it would be recorded.

The filter comes before the photo

What if the sound brings something faster than you expected? Once the photos are taken, the false and the true occupy the same frequencies and there is no way to separate them, just as you cannot separate coffee from milk. That is why every converter has an anti-aliasing filter in front, which removes, before sampling, whatever is too fast for the photos that are going to be taken.

A real filter does not cut off sharply, so it needs a margin between what you want to keep and the Nyquist frequency. We hear, more or less, from 20 to 20,000 hertz, and the CD stores up to there with 44,100 samples per second: its Nyquist frequency is 22,050 Hz, and it has 2,050 Hz of margin for the filter. The classic telephone, on the other hand, takes 8,000 samples per second and lets through roughly 300 to 3,400 hertz: the voice is understandable, but it sounds muffled, because the high frequencies are missing.

The exact number of the CD, 44,100, has a more prosaic story. In the late seventies, digital audio was recorded on videotape, with three samples on each line of the picture, and 44,100 was the figure that fit European and American television at the same time. Sony and Philips chose it for the CD in 1980, among other reasons because it was easier to remember than 44,056, the one that came out of American color video.

This sound contains two good tones, of 1,000 and 3,000 Hz, and two high-pitched intruders, of 5,200 and 7,400 Hz, and it is going to be sampled at 8,000 Hz. Adjust the filter cutoff so that the intruders do not sneak into the photos, without silencing the 3,000 Hz tone.

The staircase of heights

So far we have talked about the when of each photo. The how much is missing: each sample is a number, and a computer writes numbers only with a fixed amount of binary digits, the bits. With 2 bits you can write 4 different heights, with 8, 256, and with 16, 65,536. It is a staircase of steps, and each sample is rounded to the nearest step. That rounding is called quantization.

Rounding makes an error of, at most, half a step, and that error behaves like noise: the quantization noise, which sounds like a faint hiss. Each extra bit splits each step in two and moves the hiss about 6 decibels farther away. A decibel (dB) is a way of comparing sizes with a compressed scale: every 20 dB, one signal is ten times larger than another, and 40 dB is a hundred times. Decibels are taken calmly in lesson 5. The CD uses 16 bits, and leaves the hiss about 98 dB below a sound that fills the whole range, almost 80,000 times quieter, which in practice is not heard.

These steps are steps of height, of the values each sample can take, and they really exist. They must not be confused with the steps in drawings that join the samples with horizontal segments: those exist only in the drawing, because your phone’s converter delivers the smooth curve that passes through all the samples. In the challenge you will also see why it pays for the sound to fill almost the whole range before it is stored.

Find the smallest number of bits with which the rounding error ends up a hundred times below the tone (40 dB). Do it with the loud tone and then with the quiet one, which fills only a tenth of the range.

What to remember

  1. Sampling is taking photos at regular intervals: each photo is a number, and between one photo and the next nothing is looked at. The sampling rate counts how many are taken per second.
  2. With more than two photos per turn of the fastest Ferris wheel, the photos tell the truth. With fewer, that Ferris wheel disguises itself as a slower one, still or reversed. That aliasing cannot be undone afterward, and that is why the filter comes before the photo.
  3. Each photo is stored with a limited number of bits, which rounds the height to a step of a staircase. Each extra bit doubles the steps and moves the rounding noise about 6 dB farther away.

Common mistakes

  • “Exactly double is enough.” More than double is needed. With exactly two photos per turn they can always land when the cabin passes through the middle height, and the wave would come out flat. The rule asks for more than two, not two.
  • “Digital audio is a staircase.” The steps are only in drawings that join the samples with horizontal segments. If the rule is met, the converter delivers the smooth curve that passes through all the samples. The real steps are steps of height, and they are the ones of quantization.
  • “Aliasing can be removed afterward with a filter.” Once the photos are taken, the folded part occupies the same frequencies as the good part and cannot be separated. The filter has to come before sampling.
  • “The wheel turning backward is a camera fault.” The camera works fine: it does what any system that looks at intervals does. The same happens with a strobe, with a flickering lamp, or with an audio converter.
  • “More samples per second always sounds better.” What matters is meeting the rule with margin for the filter. Beyond CD quality, the improvement is small and disputed: an analysis of 18 studies found that only trained listeners can tell the difference, and not always.

Math corner

The formulas, for anyone who wants them. Each one comes after its reading in words.

Sampling

Sampling is reading the signal every T_s seconds. Sample number n is the value of the signal at time n\,T_s, and the sampling period is the inverse of the sampling rate:

x[n] = x(n\,T_s), \qquad T_s = \frac{1}{f_s}

Why there are impostors

A Ferris wheel of frequency f and another one that makes k\,f_s more turns per second, with k an integer, leave the same samples, because in each photo the second one has made k extra whole turns:

\cos\big(2\pi (f + k f_s)\, n T_s\big) = \cos\big(2\pi f\, n T_s + 2\pi k n\big) = \cos\big(2\pi f\, n T_s\big)

And since the cosine does not distinguish the direction of the spin, the Ferris wheel of f_s - f turns per second also leaves the same samples:

\cos\big(2\pi (f_s - f)\, n T_s\big) = \cos\big(2\pi f\, n T_s\big)

The apparent frequency

To find out at what frequency a folded tone sounds, subtract the nearest multiple of f_s and take the absolute value, which ends up between 0 and f_s/2:

f_a = \left|\, f - f_s \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right) \right|

For a Ferris wheel or a wheel, which do show the direction, the sign is kept: if it comes out negative, it seems to turn backward:

f_{\text{turn}} = f - f_s \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right), \qquad -\frac{f_s}{2} < f_{\text{turn}} \le \frac{f_s}{2}

The wheel with spokes

A wheel with N identical spokes repeats its pattern N times per turn, so what the camera samples is N times the spin frequency, and the apparent turn is that folded frequency divided by N:

f_{\text{pattern}} = N\,f, \qquad f_{\text{video}} = \frac{1}{N}\left( N f - f_s \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{N f}{f_s}\right) \right)

The Nyquist rule

The fastest Ferris wheel in the signal has to turn at less than half the sampling rate:

f_{\max} < \frac{f_s}{2}

The reconstruction

When the rule is met, between the samples the curve is the sum of a “sinc” centered on each sample and scaled by its value (the Whittaker–Shannon formula):

x(t) = \sum_{n} x[n]\; \operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{t - nT_s}{T_s}\right), \qquad \operatorname{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u}

The staircase

With N bits in a range from -A to A, the step measures \Delta, and the rounding error, spread evenly between -\Delta/2 and \Delta/2, has a standard deviation of \Delta divided by the square root of 12:

\Delta = \frac{2A}{2^N}, \qquad \sigma_q = \frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{12}} \approx 0{.}29\,\Delta

For a sine that fills the whole range, the ratio between its power and that of the rounding noise grows about 6 dB per bit. If the sine fills only a fraction a of the range, the ratio drops by as much as its level drops:

\mathrm{SNR}_q \approx 6{.}02\,N + 1{.}76 + 20\,\log_{10} a \ \text{dB}

With a = 0{.}1, that is 20 dB less, about three extra bits to reach the same result.

The data rate

Uncompressed audio takes samples per second, times bits per sample, times channels:

R = f_s \cdot N \cdot C, \qquad \text{CD: } 44{,}100 \cdot 16 \cdot 2 = 1{,}411{,}200\ \text{bit/s}
